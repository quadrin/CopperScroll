## Coverage and limits

The European-language running text, footnotes, appendices/tables, review notices and captions throughout this scope were read sequentially from the extraction, rather than searched for keywords. All 103 pages were also inspected as page-image contact sheets to check figure presence, page order and blank pages. Plates I–III were inspected at full rendered-page scale. Selected pages were inspected at full rendered scale to resolve OCR/layout issues.

Do not label the entire scope “fully read” without qualification: substantial Arabic edition and apparatus on printed pp.89–97 (PDF94–102) were visually inspected but not independently read/translated from their badly corrupted OCR. In Matthews, Greek/Hebrew quotations and proposed Hebrew emendations have not been independently transcribed character by character; their surrounding prose and the author's stated argument were read. Isolated original-script quotations in other articles have the same transcription limitation. Source photographs on the plates are low contrast; seeing a depicted object does not independently establish its date, provenance, or interpretation.

The supplied sequence jumps from printed p.82 (PDF89) to printed p.85 (PDF90; PDF91 is printed86). Printed pp.83–84, including the treasurer's report advertised in the contents, appear absent. PDF54 is a blank verso. Plates I–III are present. No geographical map or cave plan occurs within this reader's span.

## Article coverage and source findings

### P. Dhorme, Le déchiffrement des tablettes de Ras Shamra

Printed pp.1–6; PDF4–9. Complete European-language text and notes read. No figures.

Dhorme recounts the decipherment of the early Ras Shamra alphabetic tablets, including identification of a prefixed l, the Baal word, and a priestly dedication on tools. He explicitly records that an initial proposed axe word used to launch decipherment was subsequently read differently (printed pp.3–4/PDF6–7). His pp.5–6 supply lexical/morphological examples and connect the name Ṣapuna with inscriptions then known. The report is a 1930/1931 state of decipherment, including the author's contemporary “Phoenician” classification; it must not silently become current linguistic consensus. No Copper Scroll landmark, cave relationship, hydraulic feature, or archaeological comparator directly resolves the project's open questions here. Even the name discussions do not authenticate a Copper Scroll name occurrence.

### G. M. Crowfoot and L. Baldensperger, The Rose of Jericho

Printed pp.7–14; PDF10–17. Complete text and notes read. Printed p.12/PDF15 inspected at full-page scale. No figures.

The authors describe the dry annual Anastatica hierochuntica and contemporary birth charms across Palestine, Syria, Egypt and Sudan, with medieval and early modern literary comparators. They distinguish a traded plant-name from actual local occurrence: on printed pp.12–13/PDF15–16 they say Anastatica does not grow at Jericho, while Asteriscus pygmaeum, also called Rose of Jericho, does. Their suggestion that most Jerusalem sale specimens came from Egypt is explicitly uncertain, and should stay uncertain.

The printed p.12 itself says the nearest location is near “Masada” on the *eastern* shore of the Dead Sea. The image confirms that this is printed wording, rather than just an OCR accident. The geographic wording is internally problematic; preserve it as a source problem and do not turn it into a verified site fact. No Roman pit/grave, Tell es-Sultan plan, or Koḥlit relationship occurs here. The occurrence of “Jericho” in this article's title supplies no such archaeological evidence.

### T. Canaan, Light and Darkness in Palestine Folklore

Printed pp.15–36; PDF18–39. Complete European-language text/notes read, with original-script transcription limits above. No figures.

Canaan describes then-contemporary lighting equipment, shrine lighting, popular beliefs and Arabic idioms, continually comparing them with Biblical and later literary language. Printed pp.15–18/PDF18–21 distinguish shallow open and covered oil lamps, single and multiple wick openings, metal lamps, raised brackets and wall niches; pp.20–22/PDF23–25 discuss shrine oil and olive-tree beliefs. Printed p.34/PDF37 mentions an explanation of a periodic spring in his separate Dämonenglaube publication; this is a cross-reference to folklore, not a measured spring report. It supplies no measured location, flow direction, construction phase or dating for Atara or another project target. Treat contemporary/medieval practice and proposed ancient continuity as separate evidence layers. The article's generalized claims about ancient survival cannot date a particular installation.

### Robert Koeppel, Naturwissenschaftliche Methoden bei archäologischen Ausgrabungen

Printed pp.37–41; PDF40–44. Complete text read. No published diagram appears; printed p.40/PDF43 preserved at full-page scale as a methodological reference.

This reports field methods at Tell Ghassul, described as about 7 km from the Dead Sea. Printed p.38/PDF41 records the author's geological model: delta-like deposits from Ajun Musa/Hesban/Kefren valleys, generally west/southwest slopes of 2°, a near-flat north approach to Ghassul and a 4° south descent. A clay horizon traced by trenching and 2m boring was used to estimate a locally maximum 2.60m natural sand thickness. Printed pp.39–40/PDF42–43 describe later discovery of underlying walls/silos/hearths, a 3m boring tool, microscopical ash/pollen observations, matching ash spreads to a stratigraphic surface, drainage/erosion mapping, and grid/datum control over a roughly 700×400m area.

These are observations and interpretations from Ghassul, with a generic note on ash/sand comparisons from Ain Shems, Beit Mirsim and Jericho (printed p.39/PDF42). They do not establish the phase or origin of a particular Jericho pit/grave, and do not establish an Atara pool/channel relationship. The useful comparator is methodological: natural substrate and phase attribution require actual sectional/datum evidence. The text's grid language is historical and awkward (“Je 100 Quadratmeter” followed by 10m subdivisions); do not promote it into a newly computed survey precision claim.

### J. G. Matthews, Tammuz Worship in the Book of Malachi

Printed pp.42–50; PDF45–53. Complete English argument and footnotes read; Hebrew/Greek quotations and reconstructions not independently transcribed character by character. No figures.

Matthews argues that Malachi2:10–16 attacks a fertility cult, rather than marriage/divorce. The crucial proposed reconstructions are authorial emendations of the Masoretic text. Printed pp.47–48/PDF50–51 explicitly discuss alternative conjectures for “wife of your youth” and state that the versions provide no help in deciding between them. The complete proposed translation on printed pp.49–50/PDF52–53 embodies those changes. This provides no direct manuscript witness or lexical adjudication for a Copper Scroll entry. Preserve it as a history of interpretation, if retained at all; do not treat the reconstruction as visible ancient wording.

### A. Reifenberg, Der Thoraschrank auf den Tetradrachmen des zweiten jüdischen Aufstandes

Printed pp.51–54; PDF56–59; plate I at PDF55. Complete German text and notes read; plate visually inspected at full rendered-page scale.

Reifenberg surveys competing interpretations of the architectural obverse on second-revolt tetradrachms and favors a Torah shrine through comparisons with gold glass, an Olivet ossuary, a Palestinian glass, and synagogue decoration. Printed pp.52–54 distinguish depicted columns/arched structure from proposed functional interpretation. The possibility that outer columns denote a temple or synagogue remains in his discussion. This is iconographic argument, not an excavated plan of a local building. Plate I assembles the comparison objects and coins; none supplies the cave threshold, Atara hydraulics, or Koḥlit pool/grave geometry sought by the project.

### A. Reifenberg, Vergleichende Beschreibung einiger jüdisch-palästinensischen Lampen

Printed pp.63–67; PDF68–74; plates II–III at PDF70–71. Complete German text and notes read; both plates visually inspected at full rendered-page scale.

The article describes ten lamps through menorah, palm, vessel, architectural and geometric motifs and compares them with coins and synagogue ornaments. Printed pp.64–65/PDF69,72 distinguish competing iconographic interpretations and explain that one object's original was unavailable. The multi-wick type is proposed as a Hanukkah object; that is interpretation, not independently demonstrated function.

The important limitation occurs at printed pp.66–67/PDF73–74: the author says satisfying dating is impossible without excavation reports. Some lamps resemble Roman forms, most Byzantine forms. Provenance is insecure except for No.2 stated as Nazareth; most allegedly came from Galilee. Nos.1,4,5,8,10 were accessible only as photographs and their current ownership was unknown; No.3 was loaned by American Colony Stores; Nos.2,6,7,9 belonged to the author. These objects cannot independently date a project feature. Captions and photographs do not repair the missing archaeological contexts.

### Reviews, printed pp.68–82

PDF75–89. All European-language review text and footnotes read. No figures in these reviews.

- A. Barrois on Alan Rowe, The Topography and History of Beth-Shan, and G. M. Fitzgerald, The Four Canaanite Temples of Beth-Shan, PartII: The Pottery: printed pp.68–71/PDF75–78. Review-level summary of excavated stratigraphy and Egyptian monuments; dated stone inscriptions offer chronological anchors for that site's architecture/pottery. No direct project-site observation. The cited books remain separate primary publications, not supplied inside this review.
- A. Barrois on E. L. Sukenik and L. A. Mayer, The Third Wall of Jerusalem: printed pp.71–73/PDF78–80. The reviewer credits separation of observed masonry from identification but objects that bearings were not numerically tabulated and stresses competing wall identifications. This is a useful caution for reconstructing bearings from plates of different scales. It neither establishes a new Jerusalem wall identification here nor identifies a Copper Scroll landmark.
- Elias N. Haddad on P. K. Hitti's edition of Usamah ibn Munqidh, Kitab al-i'tibar: printed p.74/PDF81. Bibliographic/review lead about Crusader-period narrative, maps and a fortress plan in the reviewed book; none reproduced here. No target-site evidence.
- St. H. Stephan on H. Schmidt/P. Kahle, Volkserzählungen aus Palästina: printed pp.75–82/PDF82–89. Full detailed correction list read. The review frequently distinguishes actual idiom from misleading literal translation, including names, titles, deictic expressions and containers/measures. Printed p.81/PDF88 gives a local modern capacity/weight example (two sāʿ, about24kg in Jerusalem district); it must not calibrate an ancient Copper Scroll measure. This material is a review of modern dialect/folklore, not a manuscript apparatus for the scroll.

### L. A. Mayer, A Sequel to Mujir ad-din's Chronicle

Printed pp.85–97; PDF90–102. English introduction/notes on printed pp.85–89 completely read; substantial Arabic text and original-script apparatus on printed pp.89–97 visually inspected, not independently translated/read in full. No figures.

Mayer traces fragments of a sequel through Leiden Or.339b (fols277r–279v), Bodleian Marsh611 (fol215vff) and a Khalidiyyeh Library manuscript completed 6JumadaII902AH. Printed pp.86–89/PDF91–94 distinguish witnesses and explain why numerals that look like952/953 should be read902/903 in the narrative sequence; printed dates written out as906 and914 support that argument. The account includes earthquakes affecting Jerusalem/Hebron/Nablus and a snowfall at Jerusalem in1509. This is medieval documentary history. Do not use its disaster list to assign a period to an unstratified ancient feature. The Arabic edition remains an explicit remaining reading gap.

### Aapeli Saarisalo, Topographical Researches in the Shephelah

Printed pp.98–104; PDF103–109. Complete text/notes and entire tabulated site list read. Printed p.103/PDF108 table inspected at full rendered-page scale. No map is reproduced in this article.

The author reports excursions in1929–1930 and surface pottery/site observations in the northwest of Survey sheet21, between Idna and Wadi es-Sur. He proposes Moresheth-Gath at Tell el-Beida from topography and pottery (printed p.98/PDF103), while the absence of Byzantine sherds requires a separate proposed transfer of the name to neighboring Kh.Boran. Printed p.99/PDF104 says names marked by the Survey were sometimes unknown to contemporary inhabitants: Kh.Mahbiyeh and Kh.Abu es-Silasil; the name el-Hammam elicited no local knowledge of hot springs. Printed pp.101–103/PDF106–108 compare Bet Nasif/Bet Nasib, roads, sherd periods and a proposed euphemistic name at et-Taiyibeh. These are observations plus explicit name/site-identification inferences; they show the danger of treating a surviving or evocative name as a physical installation. No measured pool/channel, first-century cave entrance, or Koḥlit pit/grave relation occurs.

The site list includes cisterns and caves broadly, with rock-cut kokhim tombs and a small spring at Kh.Sebraqah (printed p.101/PDF106); the latter's settlement chronology is said to reach Hellenistic times. These remain exploratory regional comparators; no physical correspondence with a Copper Scroll entry was tested.

### W. F. Albright, The Third Campaign at Tell Beit Mirsim and its Historical Results (opening only)

Printed pp.105–109; PDF110–114, continuing in parent reader's PDF115+. Complete opening text, footnotes and chronology table read. Printed p.108/PDF113 table preserved and visually inspected.

The opening describes the1930 excavation and division of work areas, recognition of five lower strata previously lumped together, burned levels, and a period/stratum chronology under the terminology then in use. Printed pp.107–109/PDF112–114 state that subdivisions and chronologies changed after deeper excavation. This is useful history of excavation and chronological terminology, not evidence for a target site's Roman feature. Do not treat the historical date scheme or the author's claimed error bounds as independently validated modern dating. Whole-article synthesis belongs to the parent reader after the continuation.
