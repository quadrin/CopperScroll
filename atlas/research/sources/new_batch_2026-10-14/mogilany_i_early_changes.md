## Added arguments and their limits

1. **Community and corpus require separate definitions.** Davies pp. 51–52 distinguishes a literary genre, eschatological beliefs, a corpus and a social group. His comparison of CD with 1QM explicitly allows different communities and distinguishes eschatology from an apocalypse's revelation form. This extends the already recorded methodological caution; it supplies no new community-location observation.

2. **Golb's Copper Scroll conclusion is an argument, not a mapped connection.** P. 56, point 14, invokes treasures, writings and place names as support for Jerusalem origins of the manuscript collections. Points 3–13 and 15 supply his fortress, cemetery, scribal-hand, autograph and concealment reasoning. This synopsis provides neither a primary excavation section nor a demonstrated route between an identified deposit and Jerusalem. Its description of the Copper Scroll as an autograph and its collective origin claim remain attributed assertions. Repetition alongside the volume's other authors does not turn their shared excavation/manuscript literature into independent observations.

3. **Schiffman retains uncertainty about the kind and date of MMT.** P. 60 distinguishes an actual letter from a later literary presentation of the group's founding dispute and explicitly awaits publication and palaeographic study. Pp. 61–65 contain restored wording and an argument from shifts between singular and plural addressees. P. 62 explains that the modern name MMT derives from a description of contents rather than an explicit ancient title. His Sadducean and pre-Teacher reconstruction on pp. 66–70 is an interpretation of these readings and of parallels in later literature; manuscript multiplicity alone cannot establish its proposed historical episode.

4. **A generic text term cannot establish a particular document's identity.** Schiffman pp. 66–67 rejects identifying MMT with the Torah sent to the Wicked Priest, while Betz pp. 87–89 discusses Yadin's proposed Temple Scroll identification. Both depend on edition readings, restorations and wider reconstructions. They should be preserved as alternative arguments rather than combined into one identification. Schiffman p. 63 n. 11 discusses the disputed reading in 11QT 29:9 and cites Yadin's supplementary infrared plate; this is an external image reference, not an image inspected in this pass.

5. **Betz's syntheses repeat earlier interpretive work.** Pp. 79–100 compare literary traditions, purity practices and proposed Essene/early Christian relationships; p. 98 identifies his earlier cultic-ministry article and its translated version. P. 100's Essene-quarter argument cites Pixner and Riesner. Pp. 101–104 interpret the Temple Scroll and Gospel accounts concerning Jesus's trial and execution. These are attributed historical and theological interpretations. No new Jerusalem gate, latrine, baptismal facility or archaeological phase is observed here, and the trial essay supplies no physical evidence for the project's landmark tests.

6. **Brooke offers a bounded literary reconstruction.** Pp. 105–129 compares the position and functions of Levi/Levites across manuscripts and New Testament texts. His proposed arrival of a Levitical group at Qumran and its later Christian connections are repeatedly qualified. At p. 114 he explicitly distinguishes the inferred identity of an overseer from what CD actually states; at p. 117 n. 37 he preserves the distinction between common background and literary dependence. These qualifications should accompany any reuse of the parallels. An interpreted gate list or literary well remains distinct from an observed gate or water installation.

7. **The Baptist chapters disagree about what their parallels establish.** Burgmann pp. 131–137 builds his membership claim largely from Daniélou's earlier list and adds an oath/food explanation. Lichtenberger pp. 139–152 distinguishes Josephus's presentation from the Synoptic presentation, compares the shared purity/virtue vocabulary, and argues that common motifs do not settle personal membership. Pp. 142 and 151–152 identify an entry-then-exit model as two additional hypotheses. Preserve both authors' arguments; proximity, baptismal vocabulary and shared scriptural interpretation cannot be counted as independent proof of the same affiliation.

8. **Sieg's opening is comparative exegesis.** Pp. 153–156 reads kingship and sanctuary imagery in Qumran texts and Revelation, relying explicitly on Camponovo's 1984 study and existing editions. The buildings discussed belong to interpreted literary imagery. This assigned portion ends before the contribution's final page; the continuation reader owns p. 157.

9. **The Carmignac essays expose dependence within a proposed convergence.** Kapera pp. 239–242 describes the developing Gospel-retroversion programme and calls each retroversion a hypothesis. Lathuilière pp. 245–262 traces the connection between Carmignac's initial 1963 experience, Robinson's dating argument and subsequent publications, including revisions within authors' own positions. His comparative table on p. 257 distinguishes successive Tresmontant proposals and leaves Carmignac's John column empty. Agreement among these writers cannot be counted as three independent manuscript observations. Their proposed dates remain attributed literary-historical arguments; neither essay presents a recovered first-century Hebrew Gospel or a new archaeological date.

10. **Indexes provide locators rather than additional evidence.** The native 3Q15 entry, p. 282/viewer 286, points to Part I pp. 31, 42, 56, 184, 187, 204 and 222, and Part II pp. 115 and 185. The Roman part markers must accompany the page numbers. Reading this row does not itself establish coverage of those target pages or imply that both component volumes contain the same argument. Name-index aliases and interpretive groupings likewise describe the indexer's arrangement; they do not independently identify historical persons.
