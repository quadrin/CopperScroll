Jacek Michniewicz and Mirosław Krzyśko, “The Provenance of Scroll Jars in the Light of Archaeometric Investigations,” in Humbert and Gunneweg’s *Khirbet Qumrân et ‘Aïn Feshkha II* (2003), pp.59–99 / supplied PDF viewers89–129. ## How the original changes the evidential use

The same-batch [Magness2016 reading](qumran_changes.md) already recorded the regional-chemistry limitation and the disputed identification of jars with Qumran sediment. Those broad conclusions are **already captured through a synthesis**. The new contribution here is direct verification of the original methods, results and specimen catalogue, with the qualifications below. Repeated interpretation of the same assay is not another independent dataset.

- **Geochemical similarity does not locate a workshop.** The authors expressly state at p.76, conclusion10, that Moza clays from the Hebron and Jerusalem areas cannot be precisely distinguished by these geochemical results. Pages60 and75 describe both lateral similarity and vertical variation within a formation; the Moza group averages samples from different profile levels. The p.74 claim that Jerusalem/Hebron clays lie closer to the ceramics than el-Jib clays is a comparison of the sampled group means, not identification of a specific ancient clay pit or workshop.
- **The sediment comparison has a finite sampled basis.** Pages62–63 describe22 sampling entries, including Qumran coastal/Lisan deposits, clay beneath Cave4 and clay above the aqueduct. Pages65–66 and74–76 report differences between the sampled wadi material and the non-silty jar fabrics, quartz temper and preserved shale particles. The authors conclude that the investigated jars used material unavailable in the immediate vicinity. Their observations constrain those sampled materials. They do not inventory every ancient deposit, distinguish imported clay from imported finished vessels, or date/cause the aqueduct’s construction. Pool sediment cannot by itself establish an installation’s intended hydraulic function.
- **Petrography can distinguish material that chemistry groups together.** Page75 explicitly gives Nahal Arugot clay as chemically similar to Moza clay but petrographically different through its abundant silt and quartz/carbonate pelite. The authors excluded that clay from the final grouped analysis. Settlement samples also differ: p.67 and TablesI.62–I.66/p.99 distinguish the silty5/89/1,15/87/1,15/87/2 andGDG fabrics from the relatively non-silty5/89/2. Their presence at one site does not make one homogeneous manufacture group.
- **Firing and temper confound a simple provenance reading.** Pages74–75 leave natural versus added carbonate grains unresolved and explain how firing can destroy carbonates or optical anisotropy, even within one kiln. The modern Jericho potter used el-Jib clay with coastal sand; Photos14/p.68 and discussionp.75 are a modern comparison. Similar sand is not evidence that this same ancient supply chain operated. Page75 also offers carbonate breakdown/reaction as an alternative origin for white patina; it is an interpretation, not a measured deliberate salt recipe.
- **Shared jar material cannot date shared deposition.** The opening pp.59–60 separately asks where jars were made, how they relate to settlement pottery and whether manuscripts and jars entered caves together. Conclusion9/p.76 finds no unusual different raw material separating the sampled cave and site jars. It does not establish a common manufacture year, ownership, deposition event or scroll identity. The chapter’s introductory functional/sectarian statements are interpretations inherited from the literature.

Page73’s seven groups were chosen after cluster analysis, and p.60 itself warns that microregional subdivisions may depend on statistical assumptions and investigator judgment. An exploratory grouping must not be presented as a blinded geographical prediction. Any quantitative reuse needs the complete original specimen/element mapping, detection-limit handling and test specification; the source’s negative numeric entries must not silently become measured negative concentrations.

## Object and image provenance safeguards

The catalogue has internal label inconsistencies, checked against native pages rather than normalized away. The p.66 list putsGQ19–2 andGQ19–3 under Cave19, whereas TablesI.40–I.41/p.91 head them Cave8 and Cave3. TableI.42/p.91 similarly headsGQ22-A Cave8, while the p.66 sample list assignsGQ22A to Cave22; TableI.46/p.93 headsGQ28B Cave8, while the list assigns it to Cave28. Photo11/p.68 describesGQ3–8 as from Cave8, while TableI.1/p.79 calls it Cave3. These are reporting discrepancies requiring a dated object-record crosswalk. Cave numbers andGQ prefixes cannot be converted globally into manuscript-cave identities; see the [Bélis qualification](qumran_ib_changes.md).

The authors’ p.66 denominator, the specimens marked as awaitingINAA, the chemical table and the66-item descriptive appendix represent different stated scopes. Do not substitute one denominator for another without reconciling actual object IDs and available assays. For example,GQ39–7 has an entry but its three picture slots at p.94 explicitly say “Not documented”; TableI.51/p.95 has no microscopic determinations. This is absence within the publication’s documentation, not a failed scan or a recovered micrograph. GQXII is an uncertain-provenance **ceramic specimen** in this catalogue, not a Copper Scroll column image.
