## Uziel, Lieberman and Solomon 2019: Wilson's Arch

*Tel Aviv* 46(2), 237–266, DOI [10.1080/03344355.2019.1650499](https://doi.org/10.1080/03344355.2019.1650499). Supplied publisher PDF: viewer 1 is the publisher wrapper; printed page = viewer + 235 thereafter. Text, notes, tables and bibliography read through viewer 31. Figs. 1–12b inspected, including their scales/captions and the pottery-locus tables. Source SHA-256 `2961958ef26565e6f641168b6fdf18dd3d98ba00e2d8fae22ebc80a5c4a59a49`.

- **Additional primary reporting within an already represented site/campaign; no extra corroborating site.** The authors report August 2015–July 2018 excavation, licenses 7514/7633/7900/8205, about 200 m² beneath the arch (p.237/viewer2). Earlier reports and later specialist studies from these excavations share this observation lineage. Earlier Bahat/Onn investigations are cited separately; their observations are not silently reassigned to this campaign.
- **Already captured uncertainty, now with source-controlled dimensions.** W4493 has an exposed width of 8 m and length of 13 m; bedrock was not reached (p.240/viewer5, n.2). Its southern face uses uncut boulders and its core smaller stones with orange-yellow mortar. The Western Wall cuts it, and subsequent structures reuse it. Dam, bridge foundation and fortification are competing published interpretations. The excavators regard a First Wall identification as possible and explicitly caution that its date is uncertain and the mortar differs from other attributed portions (pp.240–241/viewers5–6). The general HA-ESI uncertainty remains warranted.
- **New detail for phase discrimination.** Northern pier W4204 is 7.5 m wide/3.5 m thick, with Room4402 and a western bath whose plaster coats the pier. Southern W4202 doubles the pier's width and overlies the uppermost stair of another stepped pool. Rooms4404/4405 are original southern openings; northern Rooms4401/4403 were inserted later. Building4420's pedestals subsequently block4405 and4401 (pp.243–245,254–255/viewers8–10,19–20; Figs.2/4). This is a relative sequence; this article reports no unequivocal contemporary surface at the pier (p.254).
- **Distinct channels.** Stratum7 drain: about 7 m from the Western Wall, 0.95 m wide, southern ashlar fabric and northern cutting through W4493's mortar with plaster; sealed by Building4420 (p.245/viewer10, Fig.5 p.246). Stratum5 Channel4261: 0.30 m internal width, 0.64 m external, 0.50 m deep, capped fieldstone walls, no plaster or built floor, and cut by BuildingF's foundation trench (p.249/viewer14). They cannot be merged by proximity.
- **New explicit phase warning for historical pool comparisons.** The large pool under the modern prayer floor is Stratum1, with two plaster/foundation stages and Early Islamic deposits sealed beneath it; authors therefore propose Late Islamic construction. Approximate dimensions15×13 m and depth3.2 m are estimates from plaster traces, giving a calculated capacity624,000 L; no pottery came from its foundation/plaster (pp.252–254/viewers17–19). The distinct northern26×26 m pool is assigned to Stratum3, with exact date uncertain because its dismantling yielded little pottery (p.252). Neither is interchangeable with the earlier western baths or a secure Second Temple pool.
- **Date/function limits retained.** Building4420 is dated ceramically to the second century CE from two cavea-foundation probes; the radiocarbon results were pending in this publication (pp.247–248/viewers12–13,n.5). Unfinished stairs and carving support incomplete construction. Odeon/bouleterion and reasons for abandonment remain interpretations (pp.256–260/viewers21–25). This 2019 article cannot substitute for the later radiocarbon publication.

No numerical fit to a Scroll entry was attempted. The pool sequence and Fig.9 section provide comparative source controls, rather than an independently identified Scroll landmark.

## Netzer and Damati1975: Cypros preliminary note, printed24–25

The browser capture identifies *Cypros (Tell el-ʿAqaba)* and reports November–December1974 salvage excavation by E.Netzer and E.Damati with the Hebrew University and the Staff Officer for Archaeology. The capture itself lacks a publication title/imprint and begins the following Herodion1974 note. Publisher metadata identifies it as *Hadashot Arkheologiyot*54–55 (July1975),24–25; the complete volume already exists in the older project corpus. Do not conflate this two-page notice with Netzer's longer1975 *Qadmoniot* article already listed in the corpus. SHA-256 `edf8a48b0db493e14fcb334d8bb4a7f4079dbb8ca914c9b01228aba8af01ed6d`.

- **Repeated campaign, earlier reporting stage.** Summit Hasmonean remains include an outer wall, cistern and stepped pool; the lower terrace is reported largely unbuilt except for a round tower of about14 m diameter in its southern corner. Herodian development spans summit and lower terrace. The notice reports a summit bath and a lower northwest bath, including stepped cold pools. Byzantine remains reuse the ruined palace. This belongs upstream of later Netzer accounts of the same excavated structures; it does not independently confirm their dates.
- **Additional measurements with unresolved crosswalk.** Summit bath warm room: white mosaic and painted/plastered walls; hot room: hypocaust, multicolored marble paving fragments, circular marble basin about1.4 m diameter and nearly complete gypsum bathtub1.8×1.1×0.6 m (p.24). Adjacent hall about10×8 m (p.25). Lower northwest bath entrance room about6×6 m, two warm rooms, two cold rooms with stepped pools, hot room with preserved hypocaust supports, and an additional round hot room described as later (p.25). These are source-specific notices; the capture gives no locus plan, basin-wall aperture section, threshold elevation or sealed dating deposit tying the baths to the lower four cisterns.
- **Coverage limit.** The two printed pages are split over viewer1–3. A narrow text strip at the viewer2/3 seam is clipped, including part of the sentence concerning burning and destruction. The capture by itself remains textually incomplete. The existing full-volume copy was then retrieved and its native printed24–25/viewers25–26 read in full, repairing the clipped sentence about destruction and preserving the original capture gap as copy metadata. Viewer3–4 also capture the following Herodion note; that incidental fragment remains outside this Cypros assessment. The recovered p.25 also reports burnt roofing beams and burning in many summit rooms, with destruction attributed to Zealots through Josephus; the physical burning and literary event identification remain separate. No figures occur in the Cypros notice. [Publisher metadata](https://publications.iaa.org.il/ha_hebrew_series/25/) identifies Netzer and Damati and the page range; its [download](https://publications.iaa.org.il/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1024&context=ha_hebrew_series) returned403 on14October2026. The already indexed full-volume copy supplied the successful fallback; no full-volume reading follows from this two-page recovery.

## Frumkin 2002: hydrogeology

Amos Frumkin, “The hydrogeology of Israel and the problem of water supply in antiquity,” in Amit, Patrich and Hirschfeld (eds.), *The Aqueducts of Israel*, JRA Supplementary Series46, pp.21–24. Four supplied scanned pages read visually in full, including notes1–14; final viewer also gives volume/imprint information. No figures/tables. SHA-256 `5d8d6073c13ac23f40576b0ba21a18b31b14bdea3221556849a1be803dcf32dd`.

- **Context and repeated source claims.** Carbonate aquifers and perched springs account for differing collection strategies (pp.22–23). Springs in the highlands commonly have low discharge and strong seasonal variation; the author's statements concern natural classes, not an observed flow direction at Atara or a particular ancient pool inlet.
- **Methodological question, not measured correction.** The author suggests checking whether ancient aqueduct geometry matches present source discharge/water level, given changing climate (p.22). Present flow therefore should not automatically become the ancient hydraulic datum. No quantified ancient discharge for a project candidate is supplied.
- **Cited dependencies.** The desert-fortress discussion contrasts spring-fed Herodion/Cypros with runoff/cistern systems and cites separate Doq, Hyrcania and Masada chapters (p.24). These are references to other reports, not independent field observations. Spring tunnels can collect water or merely convey it (pp.23–24); tunnel morphology alone does not establish a feeder or measuring origin.

## Hachlili and Killebrew 1983: Goliath family tomb

“The Saga of the Goliath Family—As Revealed in Their Newly Discovered2,000-Year-Old Tomb,” *Biblical Archaeology Review*, January/February1983,44–53. All ten viewer pages' text, notes and captions read; image-only p.45 was inspected separately. All native figure pages inspected, including the genealogical reconstruction, artist’s tomb reconstruction, ossuary inscriptions and photographs; all are TombH material or background illustrations, with no reserved north-strip imagery. SHA-256 `0923a9820b9f495bf047c9fa2208016f0cae1e84b95f43d526bb2738ee7a230c`.

- **Already captured/repeated campaign.** This is the popular account of TombH already described and coded from IAA Reports7. Courtyard, two chambers, repository pit, inscribed ossuaries and adjacent bath are the same archaeological complex, not another control tomb or an independent campaign.
- **Additional caution on inference.** The computer genealogy uses kinship statements, osteological data and explicit assumptions about co-burial and ossuary location (pp.48–49). The authors distinguish certain and probable links. Nathanel=Theodotus is inferred from name equivalence and family reconstruction; inscription, biography and priestly status must remain separate (pp.52–53). Theodotus's inscription does not itself state all the reconstructed life events.
- **Spatial detail already exposed in the final report, source-specific here.** Two baths are reported north of the42.7-ft-square courtyard: storage pool7.9×5.6 ft/depth5.9 ft; immersion pool10.8×8.5 ft/depth6.6 ft, five steps and connecting pipe (pp.44,46). Water collection/purification explanations belong to the authors. Their dimensions cannot date an aperture independently of TombH's existing phase record.
- **Small inscription context.** A charcoal Greek alphabet on an ossuary lid is reported in the main chamber pit, facing the entrance; magical significance is proposed, not observed (p.47). This alphabet is neither a Copper Scroll engraving nor evidence of a Greek-letter key for the Scroll.
- **Copy/reporting disagreement retained.** This article calls the cemetery over seven miles long (p.44); the1999 final report prints16 km, already recorded with a1.6 km sensitivity in the tombs baseline. The broad lower-bound statement can accommodate16 km and therefore is not a demonstrated contradiction. It supplies no measured support for selecting the1.6 km sensitivity or a new surveyed footprint.

## Zertal, Ben-Yosef, Cohen and Be'eri 2009: Kh. ʿAujah el-Foqa

*Palestine Exploration Quarterly*141(2),104–123, DOI [10.1179/174313009x437819](https://doi.org/10.1179/174313009x437819). All20 supplied pages' text, notes and bibliography read, with Figs.1–7 inspected at native-page renders. Printed page = viewer+103. SHA-256 `d1e6272d269eef142df0d588fa802da0eb08800dc5ec7d6b203aae1c36a4ab6c`.

- **Additional primary survey record, with scope limits.** The site was traced in winter2004 and described by plane-table/alidade survey, supported by a1969 aerial showing its earlier condition (p.104; Fig.3 p.107). About350 randomly collected sherds came from seven visits by7–10 surveyors,90% classified Iron Age; Roman and medieval material forms a small residual component (pp.109–110). These are surface collections. They do not constitute sealed construction deposits. The article's printed Israel grid1879/1574 on p.104 was checked in the native page and differs by7 km in northing from MHCS V2019 site143's1879/1504; Ben-Shlomo2023 p.602 gives ITM237908/650482, consistent with the latter northing convention. Preserve the conflicting printed coordinate until reconciled; no project point was changed.
- **Observation versus architectural reconstruction.** Site110×85 m; central tower7.5×7.5 m, about5 m standing height, with western opening and15×15 m court (pp.104–105). CW1's90-m length/average5-m width and CW2's45-m length are reported; some casemate rooms are restored, and the proposed entrance through a5-m gap remains inferred (pp.105–106). Fig.2 distinguishes standing wall, single course and fallen stones, with north arrow and0–25 m scale. Neither complete wall contemporaneity nor two phases is established by excavation.

## Hizmi 2016: Archelais, Phasaelis and proposed Livias

“The Toparchy of Salome, Sister of King Herod, and its Towns: Archelais, Phasaelis, and Livias,” *Judea and Samaria Research Studies*25(2),*137–*170. All34 supplied pages read, including footnotes, bibliography and historical-source list. Figs.1–23 inspected; small labels in the reproduced plans6/12 remain poorly legible. Maps1/2/21–23 are interpretive regional maps, not surveyed administrative boundaries or fine imagery north of Tell es-Sultan. SHA-256 `fbc3819ec303f4ad4b3347c822e8353196b928d26276b193c85a87cd1935628c`.

- **Lineage division.** The article derives from Hizmi2002's thesis and earlier Archelais excavation reports1992/2005, while p.*138 n.1 explicitly announces later Phasaelis excavation observations first presented here. Survey descriptions attributed to Guérin, SWP, Bar-Adon1972 and Porat1983 must remain those earlier lineages. Repetition in this review does not add another field observation. Its occasional reference to Hizmi2012 lacks an entry in the supplied bibliography.
- **Archelais: observed hydraulic geometry, incomplete second basin.** The tower-side pool reportedly has a northwest conduit and eastward overflow toward fields. West of the residential building, paired pools have an upper linking conduit, a feeder and a round settling basin; one pool was not fully excavated and its steps are assumed (p.*144/Fig.5). A separate northern complex also has paired pools and five wide steps in one pool (p.*145/Fig.7). These are separate installations. The complex's road-station/pilgrimage function is the author's interpretation.
- **Archelais supply lineage to compare with MHCS V.** Hizmi reports1.2 km of aqueduct visible by the western ridge, tested width about1.5 m/depth0.6 m and two branches towards the tower/northern complex. The source is attributed to Auja spring8.5 km away in a straight line (p.*146). The text does not document the full continuous route or date each branch. The narrated96 AgrippaI coins are a collection without a published container, sealed context or placement description here; no new concealment event follows.
- **Phasaelis: distinguish town cistern from the separate spring reservoir.** AreaN's cistern30×41 m/depth9 m, walls2.8 m thick and corner stairs, is beside a1-m-wide aqueduct outside its eastern wall. Two eastward walls' relation to its plastered exterior surface is expressly unclear (pp.*151–*152/Figs.10–11). The45×45 m/depth7–8 m spring-system reservoir cited from Marcus1992 on p.*158 is a different notice. Guérin's paces and Bar-Adon's50×60 m are historical descriptions requiring crosswalk, not additional pools inferred from differing dimensions.
- **New explicit comparative aperture notice.** In the residence about60 m east of the large town cistern, a6×2 m immersion pool with six steps shares a wall containing a5-cm connecting pipe with an incompletely excavated reserve pool. A later dome covers the bath; narrow and broad feeders may belong to different stages (p.*153/Figs.12–13). The article gives neither sealed date of the pipe installation nor a measured wall/floor section. Function labels remain the excavator's, and no Scroll measurement fit was performed.
- **New explicit comparative drain notice, date unresolved.** The bathhouse north extension leads to a10×10 m mosaic courtyard with a central3.60×3.60 m pool, about1.2 m deep, five rounded steps in the southeast and a bottom northeast drain into a covered northward channel (p.*155; n.10 says this excavation portion was previously unpublished). The described drain/basin relation is worth retaining; a phase-controlled section and dated construction remain absent. It is not silently added to the frozen entry29 regional denominator or treated as a named Scroll landmark.
- **Toponymic interpretation with a stated failed dating requirement.** Identification of Pliny's western Livias with Kh.el-Auja derives from textual geography, name succession and assumed proximity to Neara. Hizmi explicitly says initial test excavation has produced no conclusively Second Temple finds there (p.*165). This supplies an unresolved identification proposal, not an archaeological anchor for a Scroll place name.
- **Internal source corrections needed.** The abstract dates Salome's death10 AD and Archelaus's removal6 AD, but prose prints10 BC and6 BC in places (*138,*140,*142); these are internally inconsistent date labels, not alternative archaeological chronologies. Fig.3 caption says unhewn tower stones where the prose says hewn (*143). P.*158 prints identical coordinates for two springs said to be200 m apart. Retain the source conflict rather than generating two identical precise points. The same page's flow figures are cited from modern guides and are not ancient discharge measurements.

The additional pool and drain descriptions supply exploratory comparisons. Updating a frozen feature roster or reopening a closed identification test requires its own declared scope and the specified new observation; this reading does not perform that step.

## Cross-group addition: Ben-Shlomo 2023 corrects the 2009 ʿAujah survey model

David Ben-Shlomo, “The Site of Khirbet ʿAujah el-Foqa: Identifying Its Iron Age Architecture,” in Ben-Yosef and Jones (eds.), *“And in Length of Days Understanding”*,601–624, chapter26, DOI [10.1007/978-3-031-27330-8_26](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-27330-8_26). This chapter belongs to intake37, assigned by the reference reader to this regional comparison. All24 chapter pages, notes, Table1, bibliography and Figs.1–12 were read/inspected. Whole-volume coverage is not implied. The PDF's text layer omits many body paragraphs, so a24-page OCR extraction was read with native-image checks; native figure-only pages616–617 were separately inspected (viewers628–629). Chapter printed page = viewer−12.

- **Correction supported by a different field campaign.** The2009 survey proposed that many well-preserved structures, including chains15–22 interpreted as barracks, were Iron Age. The2019–2021 excavations separate the main rectilinear casemate/fortification phase from rounded upper structures. Building64's floor yielded a nearly complete Ottoman vessel in a burnt patch; the upper phase is provisionally Mamluk/Ottoman, with later occasional use (pp.610–612/Table1; viewers622–624). P.620 explicitly identifies the2009 “barracks” as those later structures. Reuse of Iron Age stones and wall bases explains why surface preservation alone combined phases. The new observation is excavation superposition and its assemblage, rather than the author's general phase-colored aerial reconstruction.
- **Further correction, preliminary reporting limit.** Structure62, called a northern outlying tower in2009, is reported as Middle BronzeIIB–C from the2022 AreaC season (p.615/viewer627). The detailed results are deferred elsewhere. Preserve that source's explicit correction and preliminary status; it is not a dated Iron Age anchor.
- **Independent destruction observations, uncertain event.** AreaA has crushed/intact pottery, ash and iron weapons in the casemate entrance deposits; Structure1042 RoomsA/C have comparable destruction deposits, with pottery attributed to the eighth century BCE (pp.607,609–611/Figs.4/7/8). This replaces simple surface-derived abandonment as the model for those excavated contexts. Identification with701 BCE/Sennacherib, Ammonite or Aramean attacks remains unresolved (p.620). Radiocarbon dates from secure contexts are still proposed future work here (p.623).
- **Unresolved earliest date and scope.** Phase3 walls precede the main fortification but have practically empty floors and cannot confidently be dated (p.611). The excavation has not confirmed IronI occupation claimed from survey sherds (p.620). This does not prove its absence throughout the unexcavated site. Gate, towers and storage complexes seen on drone photos remain partly reconstructed, with dashed outlines in Figs.11–12 and the author's warning that earlier walls beneath later clusters are difficult/speculative (pp.618–619).
- **Differing pottery samples and affiliation inference.** The survey's southern/Judahite parallels contrast with the predominantly northern parallels from excavated deposits; footnote3 p.614 suggests the survey types could represent a later phase elsewhere. The affiliation argument is interpretive and the article notes that casemate plans occur in several regions (p.621). Neither source establishes a modern territorial claim or an independently read place name.
- **Internal coordinate/direction conflict.** P.602 gives hilltop ITM237908/650482, while p.619 locates site140/AujaFortress about400 m northeast but prints238851/650082. Those two pairs differ by943 m east and400 m south (about1.02 km), so the written bearing/distance and coordinates cannot all describe the same two reference points. MHCS V2019 site140(p.384) gives Israel grid1883/1506, and site143(p.394)1879/1504: the surveyed grid centres differ by400 m east/200 m north, broadly supporting the northeastern relation but disagreeing with the2023 exact coordinate. No coordinate was silently corrected or imported into candidate data.
- **Water and names stay open.** No hilltop reservoir had been identified; jars/pithoi/skins are suggested conditionally as a water-storage solution (pp.619–620). No new Atara pool/channel contact results. The author favors Naʿarath over Ataroth and discusses a proposed Ataroth identification at Marjameh (p.621), while expressly placing biblical identification outside the chapter's main purpose. These remain competing textual-geographical proposals.

The2009 surface survey,2019 MHCS synthesis and2023 excavation chapter should remain separate records linked by field lineage and explicit correction. Copy arrival and repeated publication cannot make a phase reconstruction independent evidence.
