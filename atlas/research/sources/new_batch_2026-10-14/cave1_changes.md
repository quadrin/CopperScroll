## Source comparisons

García Martínez’s overviewpp1–5/viewer 4right–6 discusses the cave as a collection rather than treating each manuscript alone. P1n1 preserves conflicting discovery testimony (1946versus1947), not independent certainty from repeated anniversaries. P2 identifies substantial purchased rather than excavated manuscript material. Pp3–5 distinguishes Stegemann’s proposed master-manuscript rescue from the archaeological reports: Harding mentions an uncertain collapsed lower opening and a later illegal opening; deVaux describes both ancient and looting breakage. P5 notes jars/lids, phylactery cases, comb, bowls,cookingpots and lamps that the master-manuscript interpretation does not explain. These are the author’s source comparisons, not a newly excavated Copper Scroll context. The argument reinforces the need to separate find provenance, container function and a reconstructed single emergency deposit.


### Completed overview (pp1–13)

García Martínez pp6–7/viewer7 tests the proposed master-copy role against copies in other caves that are dated earlier and against duplicate texts inside Cave1. These are comparisons of published editions, not new excavation observations. Pp8–10/viewer8–9 contest early-deposit and genizah models: the latest manuscript supplies a terminus after which deposition occurred, not the deposit date itself. The author treats broad palaeographic and radiocarbon ranges as objections to particular early-deposit proposals; these are contested dating arguments, not newly dated Cave1 archaeological layers. Pp11–12/viewer9right–10left distinguish a disputed order of literary development from a demonstrated master-copy function, and caution that one preserved transition between psalms cannot establish the sequence of an entire fragmentary collection. P13 prefers concealment for protection but explicitly leaves interruption versus ancient partial emptying unresolved. Classification: independent source comparison and competing interpretation; no new Copper Scroll context.

### Isaiah comparisons: Parry (pp17–32) and Flint (pp33–53)

Parry compares consonantal readings, expressly omitting disputed vocalizations (pp19–20). His p21 observation separates ordinary corrections and supralinear additions in Isaiah manuscripts from the much later paired Masoretic ketib–qere apparatus: similarity of individual readings does not demonstrate the same scribal system. His 58-item catalogue pp27–32 identifies editions and manuscript witnesses individually; it is not a table of independent archaeological finds. P21n18 names his primary transcription and photograph checks. Classification: independent textual comparison with methodological relevance; no new Copper Scroll reading.

Flint pp34–35 maps successive fragment publications onto a material reconstruction, distinguishing surviving pieces from the two lost opening columns. Pp35–37 distinguish the 622 variant entries against multiple sources from the smaller comparison with Masoretic witnesses; variant units may include more than one word or change. Pp51–52 expressly account for two entries duplicated between categories, so their category sum is not an independent-witness count. The text gives 115 minor variants on p46 but 117 in its concluding discussion pp51–52; preserve that inconsistency rather than silently harmonizing it. P52 extrapolates a whole-scroll variant count from approximately25% survival under an explicit same-pattern assumption; it is an illustrative extrapolation, not an observed total. Classification: original catalogue/reanalysis, useful counting control; no Copper Scroll site evidence.

### 1Q5: Stökl Ben Ezra (pp247–257)

Pp247–248 identify fifty assigned fragments but emphasize that many carry very few letters. The author reports preliminary access to the original pieces while stating that their versos could not be examined microscopically; material joins therefore remain untested. P249n11 identifies the displayed letter drawings as derivatives of scans of archived Orion photographs. Pp249–252 compare differently enlarged drawings of 1Q5 and 1Q4; damaged or partial letters limit comparisons. Pp252–255 propose separating fragment1 as1Q5a and possibly associating fragment2 with1Q4, while retaining multiple hands within one scroll and quotation as alternatives. A hand difference does not by itself establish a separate physical scroll. Pp256–257 offer earlier dates for the main hand but also analogize palaeographic date ranges to radiocarbon sigma intervals. That analogy is an authorial proposal, not a measured calibration of palaeographic uncertainty. Classification: independent palaeographic observations, tentative reconstruction, and methodological caution. No transfer to the Copper Scroll's reserved letter-images has been attempted.


### Festival Prayers: Hamidović (pp259–275)

Pp259–261 trace reassigned fragments and overlapping copies, distinguishing an opistograph's recto/verso relationships from unjoined fragments placed by a reconstructed annual festival order. P261 states that the isolated fragments have no textual join establishing that proposed sequence. Pp265–267 test proposed feast-identifications against alternative feasts sharing rejoicing, dispersed people and congregation language; these features are not uniquely diagnostic. P271 expressly identifies “seed” as a restoration absent from all three considered manuscripts, chosen to balance the following expression. Pp273–275 keep a general calendar introduction and a quarterly prayer as competing explanations. Finding a Day of Atonement prayer after these fragments does not itself identify the preceding prayer as autumn New Year. Classification: independent philological comparison and correction of specificity; no newly excavated ritual context.

### Genesis Apocryphon group (complete native reading)

Falk pp193–203 compares four departures from Genesis: Noah's sons marry rather than are born at his five-hundredth year, Noah sacrifices before leaving the ark, dominion is promised before the Flood, and the surviving food-permission is limited to vegetation. Pp201–202 offer a possible resegmentation and different preposition that could justify the last departure, while expressly labelling the reconstruction illustrative rather than a demonstrated authorial procedure. Vegetarian practice and social context remain suggestions, not independently observed community behaviour. Classification: original literary comparison and interpretive hypotheses, no new field evidence.

Machiela pp205–221 reports readings from his own photographic study (p206; p208n12) and argues for the text's apocalyptic character. Pp212–213 dispute using an assumed first-century-BCE composition date to establish priority between Genesis Apocryphon and1Enoch; common source and direct dependence remain alternatives. P217 distinguishes a manuscript's added geographic detail from the Genesis account. This is an ancient literary geography, not a survey confirming a Copper Scroll landmark. The project already records the separate Machiela/Fitzmyer Shaveh/BethHakkerem connection; this chapter does not supply a new independent candidate observation.

Peters pp223–232 compares Noah's priestly portrayal with Aramaic Levi and Jubilees. P224 explicitly notes that the Book-of-Noah instruction survives in the Greek MtAthos version, not in the extant Aramaic Qumran/Genizah fragments; its presence in an earlier Aramaic form is inferred. P228n14 preserves disagreement over whether a Book-of-Noah heading proves a separate lost source. P231n22 cites physical support for4Q252 beginning at its surviving column: tie remnants and reverse discoloration, reported fromDJD22p190, not newly inspected here. P232 assumes copies were sealed together in Cave1 at the turn of the era. That closing formulation must not override García Martínez's explicit deposit-date objections on pp8–10 in the same volume. Classification: literary source comparison and an internal disagreement over deposition; the shared volume is not uniform independent support.

Ego pp233–243 synthesizes Abraham's dream, instruction, prayer and healing. P236n7 contrasts Falk/Machiela's “wisdom” with Fitzmyer's “wife” in1QapGen19:24; these are alternative readings of one damaged passage. Pp237–238 propose identifying the taught wisdom with astronomical/astrological tradition by a parallel inPseudo-Eupolemos; that content is inferred, not preserved explicitly in the damaged passage. P241 compares laying-on-hands traditions through earlier scholarship; this is no independent observation of ancient practice. Classification: interpretation and source-dependent synthesis; no new Copper Scroll geography.


### Hodayot: Harkins (pp101–134)

P101 and p133 reproduce Sukenik's description of the material when opened: separate sheets irregularly folded/rolled together, with a third sheet forced into the folds, contrasted with the neatly rolled War Scroll. This is a report of Sukenik's opening, not an in situ excavation record observed by Harkins. P105 distinguishes damage patterns and unattached sheets from a single reconstructed literary sequence. Pp122–123 compare orthographic tendencies while explicitly declining to treat them as a test of Qumran composition; the same scribe can copy different orthographic exemplars. Pp126–130 challenge a reconstructed4Q428 of roughly68columns/9.5m because a large part of its length depends on two tiny, uncertainly located fragments and an assumed unchanged hymn order. Pp133–134 propose that an independently transmitted Community Hymns sheet was inserted into an earlier Teacher Hymns/Community Hymns collection. The insertion explains reported material disorder but remains the author's reconstruction. Classification: original analysis of published material and literary patterns; a concrete control against equating same handwriting, reconstructed ordering and demonstrated physical continuity.

### War Scroll: Schiffman (pp179–189)

The author p180 states there are no Cave4 parallels for the conscription passage under examination. Pp186–187 distinguish shared exegesis from a report of actual military actions. P188 explicitly says the later War Scroll manuscripts and recensions provide no new material for this particular analysis, despite their relevance to the composite nature of the wider work. Classification: largely repeated legal interpretation and a specific limit on independent-source credit. No new Copper Scroll association follows.

## Further chapter comparisons

Nitzan, pp57–75 (viewer 32right–41), proposes a ten-principle catalogue and tabulates its elaboration against several manuscript witnesses. P58 explicitly allows the number ten to be coincidental; the argument rests on subsequent order and content. P70n51 cautions that a conservative scribe could reproduce an earlier palaeographic style. Classification: original literary comparison and explicit uncertainty, not a numerical key or a newly observed deposit context.

Ginsburskaya, pp77–90 (viewer 42right–49left), distinguishes physical exclusion from a particular holy assembly from loss of membership or the right to offer counsel. Pp81–84 retain mediated counsel for physically impure members and distinguish special-event rules from general membership; pp87–90 contrast sinful members who may attend silently with physically impure members excluded from the gathering. Classification: correction of an overly broad interpretation of purity rules. It does not identify a specific bath, watercourse or cave.

Loader, pp91–98 (viewer 49right–53left), contrasts proposed expansion of1QSa with accidental omission at repeated wording in4Q249d, and separates supralinear additions from the first text of4Q249e (pp94–98). He proposes that the shorter witness may be defective and the longer text earlier, while making its historical date conditional on Pfann's palaeographic date. His alternative explanation of the missing sexual-conduct language also challenges an inference of permanent celibacy. Classification: independent textual comparison and competing reconstruction; short text does not automatically equal early text.

Chazon, pp135–149 (viewer71right–78), interprets the hymn collection liturgically but distinguishes what survives in1QHa from what is reconstructed through4QHa. P144 notes that the Self-Glorification Hymn's distinctive wording is certainly preserved in4QHa but reconstructed in1QHa, where only three non-distinctive words survive in the relevant fragment. P146 identifies another missing connector supplied by the parallel manuscript. Her broader liturgical inference therefore must not be counted as two independent observations of the same words in both copies. Classification: source-qualified literary reconstruction. Her emphasis on thematic unity across all three sections and Harkins's emphasis on their discontinuity are distinct arguments, not a uniform conclusion from one volume.

Schultz, pp153–164 (viewer80right–86left), distinguishes two stages in the imagined war from stages in the composition of the text; apparent differences need not automatically establish separate sources. Pp162–163 identify4Q491A/B as two documents under Abegg's palaeographic/orthographic analysis and compare their different content; the proposed common source remains a reconstruction. P164 suggests Roman conquest as the trigger for rewriting from relative manuscript dates and analogy to another text. It is an historical hypothesis rather than a direct dating observation or an attested reason for depositing the Cave1 copy. Classification: original textual synthesis and qualified historical interpretation.

Batsch, pp165–178 (viewer86right–93left), distinguishes priestly command from priests actually fighting, citing1QM9:7–9's requirement to remain distant from the slain (pp174–175). The interpretive diagram on p177 organizes political choices rather than mapping empirical sites, and p178 warns that its four positions simplify a more varied situation. Classification: historical interpretation with a source-based distinction between roles, no new field observation.
