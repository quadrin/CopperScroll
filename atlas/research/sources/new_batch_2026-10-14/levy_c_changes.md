## What changes in the evidence

**A metal source does not determine its transport route.** Klassen and Danielson printed 1207–1211,1220–1221 distinguish Arabah copper provenance from the proposed northeastern route through Moab. They compare local ceramic production and settlement sequences with the stronger western exchange evidence, while retaining the possibility of limited movement through Moab. Their Figure 1–2 roads are explicitly inferred from named earlier studies. They supply a useful control against treating a line on a modern map as a separately excavated ancient road.

**Survey phases can manufacture contemporaneity.** The same chapter printed 1209–1211 discusses sites assigned an early phase in survey whose excavated contexts proved later, and the danger that a broad survey period combines successive short occupations. This is a methodological control on the existing phase requirements; it adds no dated Copper Scroll landmark.

**Date road fabric separately from route use.** Ben David printed 1231–1234 distinguishes possible corridors, cairn lines and platforms, including differing proposed platform chronologies and secondary burial use. Printed1245–1248 traces the successive Nabataean and Roman interpretations of the Negev road, with the 2018 milestone discoveries providing a later, specific observation. An early route and a paved road or milestone station can belong to different phases.

**Keep similarly named ascents separate.** Printed1241 distinguishes Naqb Sfai, beneath the modern vehicle road, from the western Naqb Sfar, and separates the records of Rujm Sfei and Qasr Sfar/Horvat Zafir. The reported destruction of structures during the 1950 road construction limits modern field checking. These are regional identity controls, not a new proposed scroll-site match.

**A rare object in fill does not date its fill or define a community.** Jones printed 1259–1266 explicitly places the decorated bowl sherd R. 31048 in a mixed Islamic-period fill, with older material potentially washed from above. The proposed earlier personal inventory and religious associations remain hypotheses. His comparison of the South Cemetery chronology with the mining workforce also prevents later burials from silently defining the earlier workforce.

**New analysis can reuse an old find event.** Eshel and colleagues printed 1290–1298 first publish 2010 isotope analyses of bronze fragments from the previously reported Dead Sea coastal finds. These are new laboratory observations on an existing assemblage. Printed1298 retains ritual disposal and shipwreck/recycling explanations; the isotope evidence does not decide the deposition history. Figure 7 is explicitly a proposed reconstruction of Khirbet Mazin. The Elʿad silver set is separately located in Room 40 in Figure 6/printed 1297. No scroll-entry association is proposed here.

**Chemistry requires a preservation control.** Erel printed 1277–1283 distinguishes tooth enamel and petrous bone, and screens possible post-burial metal additions before comparison. It supplies an analytical-method control, with no independent dated landmark or cache context.

**An ingot shape does not fix a weight, material or function.** Kassianidou printed 1324–1331 compares several contemporary ingot forms and explicitly discusses the wide range of oxhide-ingot weights. This prevents importing a Bronze Age shape-to-talent equation into the scroll. The chapter is a distant material comparison, not a Copper Scroll metrology source. Its Table 1/printed 1335 has composition/category tensions with prose at 1332–1337; no corrected chemical value is inferred here.

**Test a caching description against formation evidence.** Papasavvas printed 1356,1360 questions the received claim that the Ingot God was cached between successive occupied floors. His 1975 radiographs, newly published here, are a separate observation about manufacture/alteration, not a new excavation of its deposit. Figure 6 is a hypothetical composite reconstruction, and Figure 9 distinguishes surviving parts from the reconstructed leg. Knapp's printed 1315–1316 hoard categories likewise remain interpretations of Bronze Age deposits, with older find events deduplicated.

The Oman and Andes chapters provide comparative background on production and social organization. Their sites and periods cannot supply local rarity, a Judean construction date or a Copper Scroll cache model. No region-wide archaeological inference is drawn from these distant parallels.

**Separate an experiment’s demonstrated result from its reconstructed archaeology.** Eliyahu Behar printed 1457–1464 explicitly separates the successful controlled shaft-furnace experiments used for osmium provenance from the exploratory bowl-furnace attempts, which did not yield a consolidated bloom. Similar-looking vitrified debris helps explain preservation, but does not establish the original furnace form or ancient ore source. This is a direct methodological comparison, without reopening a scroll test.

**A pit’s shape and its latest fill need not identify its first function.** Tsiafaki printed 1473–1477 separates irregular waste pits from beehive structures, preserves uncertainty about their original function and phases, and describes later reuse for workshop waste. Figure 2 is a multi-feature plan; the text does not establish that every plotted pit operated together. These are distant controls on functional and chronological inference.

**Check a proposed hydraulic landscape with dated elevations.** The Dor chapter's first part, printed 1493–1502, evaluates a proposed inland anchorage against sediment cores, deposition environments and sea-level relationships. It reuses named previously published cores and a geophysical dataset; it is not an additional independent field campaign by virtue of this synthesis. Figure 6 contains reconstructed shorelines. Printed1499's age wording for F5b and Figure 5/printed 1500's generalized range are not identical and should be checked against the cited dated sequences before numerical reuse. This reading contributes a method comparison; the chapter continuation remains the other reader's responsibility.
