# Regional source changes, 14 October 2026 UTC

Obtained original observations and publication corrections from scoped readings of regional excavation reports. These passages add comparative evidence for function, phase and detection limits. They supply no registered target observation for Atara, Siloam, Hyrcania or entry 25.

## Changes that matter

Leibner 2018, *Khirbet Wadi Hamam*, pp. 246–251, records an underground chamber beneath Courtyard C9, 2.5 m deep and 2.5 m in diameter. Two basalt beams and a fill of earth and small stones sealed its shaft. Chamber L4C008 contained neither eroded soil nor finds; the excavator judged it apparently sealed since antiquity. Four chambers lacked plaster, and the excavator proposed hidden storage or emergency hideouts. Their physical existence provides non-water underground-storage comparanda for the entry-46 function audit. The tentative comparison with rabbinic דות in p. 253 note 7 establishes no Copper Scroll word equivalence. C9’s floor is Early Roman but its precise construction date is unresolved. F3’s chamber was only partly excavated, so its total depth and contents remain unknown (pp. 223–227).

Meyers and Meyers 2009, *Nabratein*, pp. 113 and 116–120, documents an Early Roman rock-cut room cutting an older conical pit. Figure 31 on p. 118 includes a plan and four sections. The publication calls the pit a cistern on p. 113, a storage pit on p. 117, and a cistern or storage pit on p. 120. Room dating supplies a relative sequence; pit cutting has no independent construction date. This case should retain both function alternatives.

Omrit II, pp. 218–222 and Figs. 7.9–7.11, records two pipes beside a niche, a pipe cut in the temple’s basal molding, and a channel crossing behind the third stair to the southern wall. The author infers north-to-south flow and paired fountains but explicitly allows reverse flow (p. 218 n. 34). The proposed Echo dedication and association of inscription with fountain remain interpretations. The inscription’s calendar conversion varies internally between pp. 216 and 222; retain that variation. These pages give a hydraulic contact comparandum, without resolving another site’s junction.

Arubas and Leibner 2024, *Invisible Synagogues*, in *A Vision of the Days*, pp. 531–538, adds the Merot case. Six connected underground spaces include a bath and a cistern. Some entrances were blocked beneath the synagogue floor, while rear access and use continued into the Middle Ages. Page 535 n. 56 states that the pottery used to date the first synagogue to the late fourth century came mainly from bath L90, rather than sealed floor deposits. Figure 24.19 (p. 532) shows the complex beneath the synagogue; Fig. 24.23 (p. 537) gives the bath plan and section. This case clarifies why a blocked entrance and dated nearby building cannot establish a sealed, dated cavity.
