## New evidence and limits

1. **Pottery provenance is mostly visual.** Sandhaus pp. 31–32, 34, 36: fabric names and proposed local/regional origins rely on visual comparisons; she expressly requires NAA to distinguish several Galilean cooking wares. Gritty cooking ware's origin remains disputed, and the Tan Gray Marl attribution at Omrit is insecure. Project inference: material labels must retain this uncertainty; form alone cannot establish a production locality.
2. **Ceramic horizon versus construction date.** Pp. 32, 35, 42–43: Sandhaus places first temenos activity in the second century BCE; the earliest unguentarium parallels can reach the late fourth/third century, but those comparative form dates are not an independently dated construction context. Early Roman pottery dominates. Second/third-century material is limited. Fourth/early-fifth-century forms cannot be separated without lamps/coins; later Byzantine, Abbasid and twelfth/thirteenth-century pottery occurs in other contexts. Project inference: preserve locus/deposition relationships and avoid using a generic pottery horizon as an exact building date.
3. **Storage forms provide weak geographic discrimination.** P. 39: jars once described as Galilean recur in Judea, the Jordan Valley, Transjordan and Golan; production fabrics vary. Handmade piecrust pithoi are no longer geographically exclusive to an Iturean attribution. These are author comparative conclusions, not a new source provenience analysis.
4. **Lamp forms cannot distinguish T1 from T2 by themselves.** Rosenthal-Heginbottom p. 66 explicitly says the predominant volute lamps and Levantine derivatives did not change across Augustan–Flavian times. Pp. 67–69 require correlation with coins, other ceramics, figurines, glass, organic remains and deposit composition. Project inference: these lamps cannot independently date the choice between those temple phases.
5. **Counts reflect redeposition and possible duplicate fragments.** P. 67 n. 9 explains that tiny fragments, few joins and redeposition can cause fragments of the same lamp to be counted twice. The 720 total is not exact. Table 3.1 p. 68 visibly prints recorded quantities 12, 53, 29, 25, 381, 61, 83, 36, 19, 13, which sum to 712; its header and p. 69 state 720. The illustrated quantities sum to 108. Retain the source inconsistency; no corrected group quantity is assigned here.
6. **Earthquake date is a historical division.** P. 69 n. 21 expressly states that no specific archaeological evidence establishes the 363 CE earthquake at Omrit. Project inference: the chapter's before/after-363 grouping does not establish a site destruction deposit.
7. **Altar assemblage contains chronology and deposition uncertainty.** Pp. 78–79 record at least 46 complete/fragmentary lamps near the altar and three statue-base plinths. The two principal lamp groups are not unequivocally contemporaneous. The author considers a single deposition plausible, dates Phoenician-Palestinian lamps broadly from the last third of the first through second/third centuries, and proposes prior fire exposure followed by secondary deposition. These are author interpretations. Broken discuses alone cannot securely identify users or deliberate breakage; accidental breakage remains possible. No new concealed-hoard context is established.
8. **Source cross-reference inconsistencies.** Native p. 59 Figs. 2.66–2.67 call PT7.2/PT7.3 neckless jars; Table 2.7 p. 58 calls them necked. P. 40's Italian sigillata discussion cites Figs. 2.80–2.81, while Table 2.8 p. 60 and native captions p. 61 identify them as ESA plates. P. 42 points to Fig. 2.101 for an ointment container, while native p. 65 Fig. 2.101 is an unguentarium; Table 2.10 p. 64 assigns the ointment container PT10.5 to Fig. 2.102, whose native caption instead says PT10.4. Preserve these disagreements instead of joining catalogue entities solely by one figure reference.
9. **Illustration scales.** Each recovered pottery figure page preserves its captions and bars. General pottery plates carry 0–10 cm bars. P. 59 Fig. 2.71 has a separate 0–2 cm bar, and p. 65's large unguentarium drawing has a 0–5 cm bar in addition to the lower 0–10 cm bar. Do not impose a single displayed scale across the page. No object dimensions were newly measured.

No candidate identification or formal outcome test was run. These observations are exploratory reading/provenance corrections and comparative constraints.
