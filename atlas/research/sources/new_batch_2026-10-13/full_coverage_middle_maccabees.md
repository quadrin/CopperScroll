## Findings that affect interpretation

- **Construction and subsequent fills are separate dates.** Chapter2, pp.23–29, reassigns W1209 from an earlier Iron Age interpretation to an early Hellenistic structure. Building110's initial construction and later floor remodelling are distinct. The AntiochusIII coin supplies a terminus for the floor context, not every wall. Fig.2.4 marks unexcavated ground; Fig.2.12, p.32, shows installations on different floor levels.
- **Military remains do not independently establish a historical name.** Chapter3, pp.39–52, distinguishes the salient, ashlar collapse, gravel system and later pottery system. Figs.3.4–3.5, pp.43,46, show their sequence. Identification as the Akra and assignment to particular events are the author's interpretation. Six Roman coins from an upper fill are treated as intrusions (p.45 n.6), rather than securely dating the earlier works.
- **Survey continuity has a published numerical disagreement.** Chapter4, p.66 / viewer89: Fig.4.4 labels the total as127/148 (86%); prose says93%. The southern subset is86/93, labelled92% in one panel and93% in the other. Preserve the display and prose separately. Early ethnic attribution and reconstructed boundaries are arguments; later ritual markers do not prove earlier inhabitants' identity (pp.54–59).
- **Underground assemblages can be abandonment deposits.** Chapter5, pp.83–85, describes Maresha subterranean units filled with refuse during the upper settlement's abandonment. Such pottery is not automatically a clean use deposit. Preliminary reports and personal communications for neighbouring sites are not independent published excavations read in this volume. Ancient road alignments in Chapter6 are suggestions, not excavated Hellenistic road segments (p.94).
- **Elʿad's two coin groups remain two finds.** Chapter7, pp.114–118, describes groups of five and four silver coins in different areas; die links do not create nine independent events. Concealment of the drinking set with an intention to return is interpretation. The pool dimensions and the reported430m² value are inconsistent in units (p.114); the building's18m² value is also retained as printed (p.111). The relevant plans are missing from this copy.
- **Abandonment dates and perpetrators require separate support.** Chapter8, pp.130–143, leaves the reason and exact date of replacement of a large structure by a7×7m watchtower unresolved. Surface coins and competing earthquake/war interpretations cannot be collapsed into one sealed destruction event. Chapter9, pp.158–160, distinguishes Kedesh abandonment from a later localized archive fire and infant burials; actor and ritual explanations remain inferred.
- **The Kokhim cliff assemblage is a retrieval lead.** Chapter9, pp.162–163, describes rope access in2018 and a pottery group assigned to the middle second century BCE. Stored supplies, vessel contents and a crisis explanation are interpretations. The bibliography, p.465, cites forthcoming Shivtiel/Syon and Shivtiel/Syon/Berlin reports; neither was independently opened here. No exact cave plan or Copper Scroll identification follows.
- **Minting is not circulation or deposition.** Chapter10, pp.178–190, uses coin distributions to argue for early settlement. The source distinguishes coin issue dates from movement and loss. The ʿAqrav group has a latest coin120/119 BCE and a proposed later burial; the latter is inferred from wear. The Magdala table/body count disagreement remains a source correction requiring the original catalogue.
- **Amphora chronologies are modelled dates.** Chapter11, pp.193–214, explains chronological approximations, omitted years, alternative dates and revised stamp identifications. Zero or empty graph slots do not demonstrate no imports. Proposed historical links between import interruption, sieges and raids remain interpretation. Chapter12's coin seriation and absolute date argument (pp.215–239) are separate from direct mint-die or stratigraphic observations.
- **Market provenance is qualified.** Lorber, Chapter17, pp.312–325, distinguishes securely reported finds from dealer photographs, rumored origins and reconstructed groups. Additional market coins may belong to another assemblage (p.313 n.9). The original Bethlehem group and a later reconstructed group have different sizes and latest dates (p.319 n.18); do not pool them. Labels such as Hebron or Hebron area can reflect a dealer route rather than a findspot (p.325 n.24).
- **Literary evidence has its own dependence and dating.** Chapters13–16 offer competing historical models rather than independent corroboration of each archaeological observation. MacRae, pp.331–346, requires deduplication of petitions and decrees; recognition does not establish enforcement. Eckhardt, pp.347–362, separates dynastic framing from territorial control. Jokiranta, pp.363–378, preserves manuscript dating and group-identification alternatives; reconstructed4QSE wording is not an extant passage (p.370 n.14). Gruen, pp.379–390, distinguishes context-specific literary labels and minority positions from population-wide opinion.
- **The conclusion is synthesis of earlier evidence.** Chapter22, pp.391–407, cannot count as another excavation. It acknowledges limits on fine dating of early settlement (p.403) and poses selective territorial expansion as a question (p.404). A Har Yona coin group added in p.402 n.3 is reported from supplied identifications; rapid deposition is inferred from coin condition. Its original field record and catalogue remain a retrieval dependency.

The source-integrity gaps require a sound copy of the same edition. Re-rendering this damaged PDF cannot recover missing objects. Earlier targeted review remains historical; this audit does not reopen frozen outcomes or supply a reserved observation.
