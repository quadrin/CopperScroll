# Entry 29: reading constraints for the Jericho pool test

I inspected the actual images of Émile Puech, *The Copper Scroll Revisited* (Brill, 2015), printed pp. 62–65, including Hebrew, translation, apparatus and footnotes. This checks an edition; it does not constitute a new inspection of the copper or its fragment photographs.

Puech's entry concerns a water conduit associated with a northern reservoir and a four-sided description. Its damaged collection-word is conjectural. The northern suffix and the sides-word ending are partly restored. **Jericho is entirely a restoration.** Puech also mentions Kohlit, possibly Tell es-Sultan, as an alternative gap fill (p. 64, note 252).

The proposed starting datum deserves particular caution. Puech considers the reading of the word for large assured, based on traces and the earlier editor, but relocates it from line 4 to line 5. He supplies a missing expression linking it to the side and starting point (p. 64, notes 253–255). Thus the model “24 cubits from the long side” tests his reconstruction. It does not follow from an entirely preserved instruction. Equating large with longer is a further practical interpretation.

Twenty-four cubits is Puech's preferred partly restored numeral. His apparatus discusses competing published reconstructions, including a twenty-seven reading attributed to Wolters. The expression before the unit can mean either measure or distance; Puech prefers the latter (p. 64, notes 256–258). The entry gives no independent digging depth and no cardinal direction for the displacement. Northern qualifies the reservoir.

The plan test should retain both long sides, both inner and outer faces where their positions are recoverable, and more than one measurement convention. A perpendicular offset is geometrically convenient but unprescribed. Distance along a channel is another conditional model; its starting connection to the wall must be specified rather than chosen to produce a fit. Neither convention can acquire authority from a successful numerical result.

Puech's archaeological suggestion is broad: installations at Hasmonean and Herodian Jericho or near Ain Doq (p. 65). Note 259 cites Ehud Netzer, “The Swimming Pools of the Hasmonean Period at Jericho,” *Eretz Israel* 18 (1985), pp. 344–352, and says this identification preceded Puech's restoration. That chronology prevents claiming a simple restoration-first circular argument, but this page supplies no named pool, inlet or phase for entry 29. The specific northern-pool hypothesis is our assessment choice.
