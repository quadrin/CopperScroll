## Water route: observations and author reconstruction

Pp. 224–225: point A in Fig. 2 is the intake in a small wadi northwest of the fortress; Amit gives the source grid string `1906 1426`. The wadi flows west to east toward the Jericho valley. An approximately 2 m fall in bedrock and two small built walls approximately 1.2 m apart are described as remains of a chute linking the natural bed to the canal. The view that the builders collected the full runoff, rather than tapping a small share behind a low dam, is Amit’s hydraulic interpretation.

P. 225: the canal continues generally south, receives two more channels at B, then turns north/east and encircles the fortress’s northern side, before turning south onto its eastern side at C. Its measured length from intake to cistern 2 is exactly half a kilometre; interior width varies from 60 to 80 cm. It alternates built, entirely rock-cut, and partly rock-cut/built segments. White plaster with small stone particles is reported, along with repairs in places. Amit attributes the plaster to the Hasmonean period and treats the repairs as contemporary rather than evidence for a late use phase. These are material/chronological interpretations, not independently dated junctions.

P. 226: the canal reaches cistern 2 higher than cistern 1, and continues south at that level. A surviving steep chute branches between cisterns 2 and 3 and reaches cistern 3’s opening. This observed chute supports Amit’s reconstruction that the main canal passed above the cistern row and filled individual cisterns through branching chutes. Extending that layout to every cistern is his reconstruction; he explicitly says this is the sole chute preserved well. Remains farther south are sparse because the supporting cliff collapsed. A settling basin and stone sill are described at cistern 9.

## The new C-area comparison

P. 225 reports two small pools on a platform alongside the canal, below its level, shortly before its southward bend at C. Their construction combines cutting and masonry. The western pool retains some plaster and possibly steps; its approximate dimensions are 1.5 × 1.5 m. The eastern pool is poorly preserved: its cut eastern wall and part of its floor plaster remain. No length/width of the eastern pool is given. These are pool positions and preserved surfaces, not identified guard-room walls.

Amit suggests these pools served residents of nearby buildings and soldiers stationed there. P. 228 note 5 records December 1982 test excavations directed by Ehud Netzer and involving Rachel Bar-Nathan and Rivka Birger. Remains attributed to the Herodian period occurred down the fortress’s northeastern spur, near the pools. The cited detail supplies an archaeological building context; it supplies no individual guard-post plan, room function, eastern building corner or corner surface. Zeev Meshel’s alternative is that the pools may have been miqvaot, on account of their size and the ability to immerse in collected non-drawn water. Neither interpretation selects the Scroll’s disputed word.

**Source-plan candidate area:** retain the platform immediately upstream of C, beside and below the canal on the northeastern/eastern approach, as an inspection area for the guard-post branch. Its location follows Amit’s route and paragraph, not a surveyed pool footprint in Fig. 2. The plan does not outline the pool pair or the excavated Herodian buildings. Do not turn C into a point coordinate for either pool, a room, or a deposit. The tower symbols elsewhere in Fig. 2 are likewise unphased outlines/symbols in this chapter, without a documented guard-room function or required corner.

**Drying-room branch:** no room is identified as a fruit-drying room on pp. 223–228. The small pools do not supply that function. Keep the summit/building candidate broad pending a dated room plan and functional evidence; the summit’s eastern edge cannot substitute for a room corner.

## Cistern identities, datums and phases

Pp. 226–227 and Fig. 7 describe nine rectangular/trapezoidal cisterns cut in hard Turonian chalk along the eastern cliff. Cisterns 1–9 form the water-system row. Most openings occur on the eastern side, apart from 6 and 9. Cistern 2 lies above most of the row; cistern 4 has an opening higher than its floor. Spacing is generally about 20 m, but approximately 70 m from 2 to 3 and 35 m from 3 to 4. Nine cisterns total 2,090 m³ in Amit’s table. The table is more specific than the prose range: cistern 2 has a 14 m length and cistern 8 a 5.5 m width, outside the paragraph’s generalized 7–11 m length and 3–5 m width. Preserve the individual table entries rather than normalizing them to that prose summary. Cistern 10 near the summit has a bell shape and is treated separately as a Byzantine installation.

The row’s interior floors/heights are hydraulic measurements. They do not supply the ancient surface of the entry 31 room. P. 227 describes entrances cut down to floor height to remove debris during construction, with the lower portion subsequently closed in masonry and plastered. A plaster trace on an entrance wall supports filling to about 1.5 m below a cistern’s roof. This describes cistern water level, not a seven-cubit depth below an eastern building corner.

P. 227: Amit regards the water system as probably founded with the Hasmonean fortress, cites Herodian-period remains, sees no additional Herodian water-system phase, and infers that the Hasmonean system continued in use. The absence of a distinguishable second phase does not establish the construction/use date of either small pool or a drying/guard room. Pp. 227–228 also document later water-system use by Byzantine monks, nineteenth-century monks after the renewed monastery from 1874, a repaired summit cistern, Greek lettering dated 1898 in cistern 1, and crosses/other cuts at cistern 6. Do not collapse these into a sealed first-century context or a single dated room surface.
