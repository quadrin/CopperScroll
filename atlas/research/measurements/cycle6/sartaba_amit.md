## Result

Amit distinguishes two parallel saddle-crossing installations. Printed217 reports the original aqueduct embankment as187m long, approximately10m maximum height and2–3m basal width. The adjacent siphon, built to its north, is192m long and12.5m maximum height; the elevation difference between its beginning and end is2.11m. The siphon channel is expressly60cm wide and80cm high. Thus the1994 account's190m and60×80cm approximately agree with Amit's siphon description. These numbers should remain source-specific.

The later survey's approximately250m and60×60cm remain unreconciled. Amit distinguishes the installations, but the inspected chapter supplies no shared endpoint register or section location that maps the2017 values onto his measured192m extent. It therefore cannot establish that250m includes approaches, that60cm reflects another section, or that physical alteration explains the differences. All remain hypotheses. The187m embankment is a separate structure, not a second measurement of the siphon.

## Observed fabric and interpreted sequence

Printed217 describes the siphon built beside and north of the embankment, with heavy stone covers and grey plaster. Fig.4 shows the composite relationship at a surviving location: the lower north-side siphon fabric is depicted, while the former aqueduct on the embankment's top is reconstructed with dashed lines. Preserve that distinction when reusing the section. Printed217 explicitly says the two installations operated in different periods, interpreting the embankment as Hasmonean and the siphon as Herodian from historical fortress chronology and plaster evidence. This is a published interpretation, not a newly inspected dated foundation contact.

Printed221 note5 reports two plaster layers at several locations: lower white original plaster and later grey coating; similar types occur on the aqueduct between the saddle and cisterns. Note7 proposes that grey plaster on the embankment's top could indicate Herod first repaired the older system, then replaced it after failure, perhaps in the31BCE earthquake; it places siphon construction in the early reign of Herod (37–4BCE). The author uses tentative language. The earthquake attribution and construction date are not demonstrated through a sealed archaeological contact on these pages.

Printed217 also reports the RasKuneitra open channel as trapezoidal, average60cm bottom width,80cm top width and60cm depth. These are **another channel's measurements**. They must not be relabelled as the siphon's60×60cm section. This offers a concrete potential route for descriptive conflation; it does not establish that the2017 authors made that conflation.

Printed220 describes an open rectangular pool near northern cistern4, preserving about20m of a long side, with an approximately1m wall remnant at its northwest corner. Amit interprets it as an initial collecting basin for RasKuneitra runoff before cistern filling. This northern feature is distinct from the later survey's divided20×7m expansion at the western saddle siphon. No identification between them is justified.
