# Kowalewska 2021: bathhouses for entries 46 and 49

**Labels:** **Evidence** = the book states it. **Inference** = drawn here. **BK** = background not from this book (none used).

## Why this book was read

Entry 49 depends partly on Milik's reading of a "bathhouse" (בית חמים) at Siloam. Entry 46 places a reservoir at Beth ha-Kerem, for which Ramat Raḥel is a candidate. The question was whether any Roman-style bathhouse is recorded at either place, or near Jerusalem, in the window (50 BCE–70 CE, extended to 135 CE).

## Jerusalem before and after 70 CE (§2.5, "Jerusalem (Aelia Capitolina)")

- **Evidence.** "The Jewish city destroyed in 70 CE was characterized by a lack of public bathing complexes." Some wealthy residences destroyed in 70 CE had private bathrooms, with an immersion or sitting tub and sometimes a miqveh (citing Hoss 2005, 152–4).
- **Evidence.** The first Roman-style bathhouse of Jerusalem is assigned to Aelia Capitolina. The book gathers two Jerusalem bathhouses with a Roman-period phase and six that functioned only in the Byzantine period.
- **Evidence.** The Bathhouse West of the Temple Mount [41]: phase I begins in the 2nd century CE, probably built for or by the Tenth Legion (Mazar 2011). The Mount Zion Bathhouse [111]: Late Roman–Early Byzantine, tenth-legion bricks reported by its 19th-century excavators.
- **Evidence.** The Bathhouse of the City of David [131] is known only from an inscription (CIIP no. 796) that commemorates rebuilding and mosaic paving of a building for bathing. A coin under the mosaic dates it to the second half of the 6th century CE (Crowfoot 1929, 16; Baruch and Reich 2001, 170–1).
- **Evidence.** The other Jerusalem entries are Byzantine: Jewish Quarter Area C [54], Mamilla [49], Mount Scopus Monastery [29], Notre Dame [48], the Third Wall bathhouse(s) [110].

**Inference (entry 49).** The book records no Roman-style bathhouse at Siloam or anywhere in Jerusalem before 70 CE. The only bathing building in the City of David is 6th century CE. This supports the existing rating text that the bathing function needed for Milik's "baths" is not established. It does not exclude a private bathroom or a miqveh, which are a different kind of installation.

## Near Ramat Raḥel and the Jerusalem–Bethlehem road (§2.5)

- **Evidence — Ramat Rahel [65].** In the Late Roman and Byzantine periods the site held a villa with a small bathhouse. It had several restructurings; its layout was never fully recorded. The hypocaust used bricks stamped by the Tenth Legion (Aharoni 1962; Ciasca 1962; Gadot, Tal and Taxel 2016; Taxel 2016).
- **Evidence — Givat haArbaa [72].** A small bathhouse on a hill east of Mar Elias on the Jerusalem–Bethlehem road. Construction 2nd–3rd century CE from pottery and tenth-legion brick stamps; abandoned at the end of the 4th or start of the 5th century. The excavators suspect the army built it on a Jewish village destroyed in 70 CE (Baruch and Ganor 2014).
- **Evidence — Ein Yael [39 and the Lower Bathhouse].** Two small baths in a 2nd-century CE villa in Nahal Refaim.

**Inference (entry 46).** All bathhouses recorded at or near Ramat Raḥel postdate 70 CE. None supplies a window-period reservoir with an entrance. The entry 46 ratings (F 1, D 2) do not change.

## Other Judean bathhouses in the window (§2.5, §3.1)

- **Evidence.** Bathhouses confidently dated to the 1st century BCE are those of Herod's palaces and their vicinity (17 constructions, 14 sure). Small bathhouses in Jewish settlements and the first public city baths are assigned to the second half of the 1st century CE–early 2nd century CE. No military bathhouse is dated with confidence (§3.1, Table 3.2).
- **Evidence.** Jericho: four Herodian palace bathhouses ([169] First Winter Palace, c. 35 BCE, "probably the first Roman-style bathhouse" in the region; [170]; [171], [172]) and an unpublished one [176]. Some stand on Hasmonean remains and incorporate Hasmonean pools. The First Winter Palace has an extra bathing suite with a miqveh and otzar but no hypocaust (Netzer). Fig. 2.42 shows a miqveh-like pool and a collecting pool attached to the Third Winter Palace bathhouse.
- **Evidence.** Herodion [167, 168, 177, 178]; Cypros [173, 174]; Masada [164, 165].
- **Evidence.** Shuafat, north of Jerusalem: Jewish bathhouse(s) [119] with a brick hypocaust and plastered pools, in a neighbourhood abandoned about the Bar Kokhba Revolt (Sklar-Parnes 2005; 2006).
- **Evidence (§1.8).** In the 1st centuries BCE–CE some affluent houses in Iudaea had what Netzer calls Judeo-Hellenistic bathrooms: a miqveh room beside a room with a bathtub, usually heated from the other side of its wall (Netzer 1999, 48–9; 2006, 255).
- **Evidence.** Qalandia, once published as a bathhouse, was later shown to be a winepress (Kloner 2006, 861).

## What the book does not say (in the parts read)

- No bathhouse, bath suite or "house of hot water" at Siloam or the Gihon in any period before the 6th century.
- No mention of the Copper Scroll.
- No Hasmonean–Herodian installation at Ramat Raḥel.
