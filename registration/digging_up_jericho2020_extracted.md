## (c) Caves, cavities, shafts, cisterns and tombs

- [Evidence] Nigro describes **“underground caves in the limestone bedrock plateau north-west of the site”**, converted into tombs by EB I newcomers. The resulting **“rock-cut hypogea”** held extended families of **“100 individuals or more”**, with disarticulated burial/skull groups. Named examples: Garstang **Tomb A** (lowest strata); Kenyon **A13, A84, A94, A114, A124, A130+A61, K1, K2**. References include Kenyon 1960 pp. 4–51 and 1965 pp. 3–32; Nigro p. 183/r. 197. Sizes, individual cave coordinates, geological origin/date and an undated karst-cavity survey are not supplied.
- [Evidence] Garstang's Tomb A lies **“west of the tell”** and continued in use through the EB; p. 183/r. 197. Montanari places Tomb A114 at the top of a slope leading to the wadi, with roof collapse, EB I use and later EB II–III deposits; F5 was damaged by modern roadwork and had an irregular chamber plan, EB III date and four recovered skulls (p. 117/r. 131). No dimensions or mapped individual positions are supplied there.
- [Evidence] EB IV **“2300–2000 BC”** tombs had rock-cut chambers entered through **“vertical shafts”** and usually individual primary burial. Nigro reports **“More than 360”** excavated by Kenyon (p. 194/r. 208). Prag instead gives **177** published Intermediate Bronze tombs plus **167** possibly of that period, a possible **344** total, and a cemetery stretching **“over 1 km to the north”** (p. 103/r. 117). ## (d) Refugee camp, ground coverage and pre-camp imagery

- [Evidence] Butler identifies the encampment discussed in Wheeler's account as **“Ein es Sultan”**, a consequence of the **“Nakba of 1948”**. Its location is explained by the perennial spring (p. 58/r. 72). These are Butler's historical statements and quotations of Wheeler, not independently checked camp-administration dates.
- [Evidence] Relief agencies supplied tents, food, stores and clothing; residents **“gradually built little houses”** (p. 58/r. 72). Wheeler describes a regular grid layout (p. 59/r. 73). The text does not date individual tent/housing phases or give successive camp boundaries.
- [Evidence] Latrine-trench digging repeatedly exposed tombs. Kenyon's team employed refugees to find/excavate tombs in the village while Jericho townspeople worked on the tell (p. 59/r. 73). Permanent houses subsequently obstructed access to tombs; one threatened house led to an excavation shaft being filled (p. 62/r. 76). This establishes burial archaeology beneath/among camp housing during Kenyon's work, without locating the episode in SULTAN-1.
- [Evidence] Butler figs. 9–10 (p. 63/r. 77) show Wheeler/workmen and tomb excavation beside housing; fig. 11 (p. 64/r. 78) reproduces the refugee village, credited to **“M. Wheeler 1958, 8”**. Carswell fig. 1 (p. 69/r. 83) shows him at a tomb-shaft entrance in the refugee camp; his chapter identifies his Jericho seasons as **“1954 to 1956”**, but the photograph itself is undated.
- [Evidence] Taha states that the UNRWA school at Suwwanet eth-Thiniya was built **“after 1948”**; archaeological strata were assigned to Early Roman occupation, construction of **“the Nueima refugee camp in 1948”**, and the post-war period (p. 277/r. 291). The Nueima account must not be silently substituted for the ʿAin es-Sultan camp's building history.
- [Evidence] Pre-1948 sources reproduced or cited: Phillips's 1867 Wadi Nueima bridges (Cobbing p. 5/r. 19), Conder's 1873 regional tracing (p. 9/r. 23), SWP road tracing (p. 13/r. 27), Garstang's 1933 M Building view (p. 14/r. 28), and the **“1918”** Jericho Plain aerial reproduced by Jennings, after Dalman **“1925, fig. 70”** (p. 265/r. 279; discussion p. 264/r. 278).

## Landscape and water context relevant to the northern ground

- [Evidence] SWP identified **four** Kanat Musa aqueducts north/west of Jericho; two led to Mafjar and one to Tawaheen es-Sukkar. The surveyors attributed them to the Crusader period based on arches/masonry, but Cobbing warns that early surveyors commonly misclassified Islamic remains as Crusader (pp. 12–13/r. 26–27). These are reported historical datings, not secure first-century channels.
- [Evidence] Jennings's Zone 6 lies between the spring's natural flow boundary and Wadi Nueima; historical maps show a direct aqueduct from ʿAin es-Sultan. Zone 8 is the higher limestone shelf, bounded by Nueima to the north, Wadi el-Mafjar to the south and Judean cliffs to the west; its Naʿaran offshoot conduit is undated. Zone 7 (Mafjar) is across Nueima and had an Umayyad water supply from the Naʿaran springs (p. 260/r. 274).
- [Evidence] Arce describes the Mafjar complex and eastern walled agricultural enclosure **“hayr”**, water bridges, aqueducts, reservoirs and mills (fig. 6, p. 157/r. 171; discussion pp. 165–168/r. 179–182). Source hypotheses propose shifting wadi branches, flooding/fertilisation inside the hayr, perimeter walls serving as aqueducts, and a west-wall **“water gate”** of **“almost 3 m span”** (p. 167/r. 181). The hypothesised sequence is not a dated survey of channels beside Tell es-Sultan.
- [Evidence] Nigro illustrates **“Canals with EB pottery found just east of ʿAin es-Sultan”**, recorded by the Italian–Palestinian survey in **“2009”** (fig. 7, p. 185/r. 199). [Inference] The caption supplies an eastern spring location and associated pottery, without proving a northern-strip position or an independent construction date for the canals.
