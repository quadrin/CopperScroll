## Note 24 (p. 208; note text p. 224): the long-sought target

- **Evidence.** The note marker is at the end of the paragraph on cave no. 2 (p. 208). Note 24 says pottery sherds and other finds from the survey indicate "a Roman phase of use in some of the caves".
- **Evidence.** In n. 24 the most important finds are from **cave 20**: "a round, Roman oil-lamp and a jug". A rectangular water basin was built there, coated with hydraulic plaster "similar in composition" to that of the basin in cave no. 2. An iron Roman arrowhead was found "along the path leading down to Jericho". The note refers to J. Patrich, *HA* (in press).
- **Inference.** Note 24 concerns caves 2 and 20 and the Jericho path. It does not mention the wall opposite the monastery, its lower culvert, any aqueduct outlet, or any stratified deposit. It cannot date the R03 target wall. It matches the ESI 7–8 p. 92 reading already recorded (a second-century lamp in Cave 20, the arrowhead on the path), so the cave numbering appears to be the same survey series. It adds the jug and the hydraulic basin in cave 20, and the plaster parallel with cave 2.
- **Inference.** Note 24 does not date either basin. The plaster parallel links the two basins to each other, not to a period.

## Cave no. 2, the Hasmonean aqueduct and the water basin (p. 208; Figs. 1, 3, 4)

- **Evidence.** Cave no. 2 (Fig. 1 no. 2) "is recognized by the closing wall" (Fig. 3). It is not part of "The Cells", although Byzantine potsherds were collected there. It lies in the cliff above, ca. 8 m deep and 4 m broad.
- **Evidence.** It contains an oval water basin (Fig. 4) that "might have been constructed originally previous to the Byzantine period", to serve people hiding in the cave in an emergency.
- **Evidence.** "The Hasmonean aqueduct runs some 15 m. below", so water could be drawn directly from the upper cave. Cave 2 differs in location and construction from the cells farther east (n. 24).
- **Inference.** This is the source's only explicit statement of a period for the aqueduct. It is an attribution with no evidence given. It places a walled cave with a water basin 15 m above the aqueduct, west of the cells and at a bend of the gorge (Fig. 1). It is a wall–water association, but not an outlet with a wall at its mouth.

## Water installations (cisterns, basins, aqueduct)

- **Evidence, p. 205.** "The chilly water running in the aqueduct from 'Ein el-Qilṭ" passes the monastery of St George.
- **Evidence, p. 206, n. 12.** Georgios supplied water to people passing on the Jericho–Jerusalem road "from the large water cistern situated above the monastery, near the road (Beit Jabr el-Fauqâni)". The note cites *Vita Georgii* 23 and Milik, *RB* 67 (1960) 550–555.
- **Evidence, p. 209.** In general each cell "had a stove (Ar. tabûn) and private water cisterns".
- **Evidence, p. 212; Figs. 9–10.** Cell 9: an "ancient water cistern 5 m. deep" lies to the west of the chapel entrance, under a probably recent building with two rooms. Fig. 10b shows it in section as a bell-shaped cistern (no. 4).
- **Evidence, p. 214.** Cell 10 has a water-cistern opening on its balcony. Cell 11 has a water cistern. Below cell 12, near the river bed, are the remains of "a circular, domed water cistern".
- **Evidence, p. 214; Fig. 13b.** Cell 13 has two water cisterns (Fig. 13 nos. 3 and 6, "water cistern under balcony"), an elongated basin "presumably for slaked lime", and a domed stove ca. 1 m across.
- **Evidence, p. 218.** Cell 16 has a water cistern near the entrance with "a large, red, painted plastercross" on its front. The cross "was purposely obliterated some time after the Byzantine period."
- **Evidence, p. 220.** Cell 17 is attested mainly by a water cistern between cells 16 and 18. Cell 18 has two cisterns, one opening in the work room and one on the balcony.
- **Evidence, p. 220; Figs. 17, 20–21.** A third, larger cistern is in a cave ca. 10 m below cell 18. The cave is closed by a barrier wall 0.8–0.9 m thick, strengthened outside by a second wall 0.55 m thick. The cistern measures 5 m long, 2.5–3 m wide and 3 m high, "thus containing ca. 40 m.³". Water came from the cliffs above through a channel. A large relief cross is made on the outer plaster of the barrier wall (Fig. 21). Eight steps lead down inside. The cistern "could have been drained through a ceramic pipe, inserted at the bottom" of the barrier wall.
- **Inference (entry 32).** The cell-18 cistern is the only place in the source with a possible outlet through a wall: a pipe at the foot of a barrier wall. Patrich gives it as a possibility ("could have been"), not as an observed pipe, and gives no date. The relief cross ties the visible plaster to Christian use. RUBRIC.md F counts a feature known only from a reconstruction one level lower; D needs the feature dated in the window. Neither condition is met.
- **Evidence, n. 24.** Cave 20 has a rectangular basin with hydraulic plaster (see above).
- **Evidence, Fig. 22 (p. 223).** "Installation with water basin below cell no. 18": a plan and section, 0–5 m scale. The text pages read do not discuss it.

## Sites, paths, gate, bridge (pp. 205–209; Fig. 1)

- **Evidence, p. 205.** The cells lie "About 1.5-2.5 km. to the east of the monastery, below the path leading to Jericho." The Survey of Western Palestine and Schneider do not mention them (nn. 1–2). Koikylides identified them in 1901 (n. 3).
- **Evidence, p. 208.** A marked modern path on the north bank leads east from the monastery to Jericho. At ca. 1.5 km are the remains of 14 cells, mainly along the north-bank cliffs (Fig. 1 nos. 6–19). They are "the only concentration of monastic ruins" near the monastery (n. 20).
- **Evidence, pp. 208–209.** After ca. 1.5 km a branch path leads down to a gate at the top of a steep staircase (Fig. 1 no. 3; Fig. 5). Beyond it, a precipitous cliff 5 m deep has a collapsed retaining wall that once carried the path.
- **Evidence, p. 209.** The path then follows the wadi's 90° bend eastwards to the first cells, which stand 5–10 m above the wadi. Opposite cell 7, a secondary path crossed the wadi on a bridge (Fig. 1 no. 4), with remains on both sides, to cells 11, 12 and 19 on the south bank.
- **Inference (R03).** The bridge no. 4 and the retaining wall are about 1.5 km east of the monastery. The SWP opposite-monastery wall and Jisr ed-Deir are at the monastery. The source does not correlate them.
- **Evidence, p. 216.** Below hermitage 13, steps lead down to the river bed and across to cell 12.
- **Evidence, n. 20 (p. 224).** St George's monks identify ruins near 'Ein el-Qilṭ, "some 4 km. to the west", with the monastery of St Passarion. Patrich says this tradition "lacks historical grounds".

## Cells described (pp. 209–221)

- **Evidence, p. 209.** Each hermitage is a cave with a built space in front. Ladders 6–10 m high were needed in several cases. Hermitages 6, 7, 8, 14 and 19 are in wide natural caves, with floors levelled by fill, walls coated in lime plaster, and terraces in front. Cave 5 has poorly built remains and "may reflect an earlier, short stage".
- **Evidence, pp. 209–212; Figs. 8–10.** Cell 9 is a private chapel: "The Church of the Cells" (Koikylides) and "The Laura of St. Anne" (Meinardus). Kallinikos restored it in 1897. It is ca. 8.5 × 3.75 m; the apse is 2.35 m across and 1.4 m deep; the altar is 0.85 × 0.80 × 0.95 m. Beside the altar is a section of Byzantine mosaic, damaged in 1917.
- **Evidence, p. 212.** The Joachim and Anna traditions at Choziba are post-Byzantine. They first appear in the Georgian Lectionary (8th–10th c.), in Epiphanius Hagiopolites (between 715 and the 11th c.) and in Abbot Daniel (1106) (n. 28).
- **Evidence, p. 214.** Cell 10 is 20–30 m east of the chapel. It has an arched gate 1.15 × 0.70 m, a cave of 6 × 3 m, and a rock block carved like a column shaft, 0.42 m in diameter.
- **Evidence, p. 214.** Cell 11 is on the south bank, in three small caves. The text cites Fig. 12 for it, but the Fig. 12 caption reads "Cell no. 12 — plan" (see Figures).
- **Evidence, pp. 214–216.** Complex 13 covers ca. 6.5 × 17 m and is reached by ladders of 4 m and 2 m. The north-eastern corner is a prayer chapel with three niches; relics were kept under the apse niche.
- **Evidence, p. 216.** East of cell 13 on the north bank, a complex "with many dispersed bones" might have been the burial place of "The Cells". Some monks buried in burial cave no. 1 are identified as Celliotes (n. 30, Schneider's inscriptions nos. 28, 105, 138, 159, 178, 198).
- **Evidence, p. 216.** Cave 14 is almost inaccessible. Finds there show 20th-century resettlement, and "several Roman and Byzantine sherds" were collected.
- **Evidence, p. 217.** Cell 15 has a broad wall with a door aperture near its top.
- **Evidence, pp. 217–218.** Cell 16 was the hermitage of Jeromon Joan, who died and was buried there in 1960, and then of Yoannicios until 1973. Its modern cell is rebuilt on ancient foundations. Fig. 16a no. 4 is labelled "tomb".
- **Evidence, p. 220.** Cell 18 was inhabited by Paulos Sabaites from 1953 to at least 1966. It has Byzantine mosaic floors in black and white (Fig. 19). Locally it is called "Deir al Banât", the monastery of the Virgins.

## Inscriptions (pp. 205, 212, 214–216, 222, 224–225)

- **Evidence, pp. 214–216; Fig. 14.** The mosaic of cell 13 has a 1.45 m square around a medallion 1.20 m across, with a seven-line Greek inscription. The letters are 8 cm high, at ca. 50 tesserae per square decimetre.
- **Evidence.** Translation as printed: "For the salvation and deliverance of the past-donors and the present-donors whose names the Lord knows".
- **Evidence.** A second inscription reads φῶς ζωῆς with Α and Ω, "The Light of Life / Alpha and Omega". Both were published by Koikylides in 1901. Patrich gives no date for either.
- **Evidence, p. 212.** Koikylides saw two Greek inscriptions on the west wall of the cell 9 chapel.
- **Evidence, n. 27.** A Greek inscription above the chapel entrance records its restoration in 1897 under Patriarch Damianos by Kallinikos.
- **Evidence, p. 208.** Burial cave no. 1 near the monastery yielded 213 burial inscriptions (n. 23: Schneider *RQ* 29 [1931] 315–332; Koikylides 71–78).
- **Evidence, p. 222, n. 33.** A bilingual Greek–Arabic inscription above the ancient gate of the monastery suggests restoration by Manuel I Comnenus. Lagrange dated it 1234; Schneider's 1179 is "more convincing".

## Dates and history (pp. 205–206, 221–222)

- **Evidence.** From ca. 420–430, five Syrian monks lived at the site one after another: Promos, Elias, Gennaios, Aias and Zenon (n. 6).
- **Evidence.** Ioannes Chozibites founded the coenobium ca. 480 AD.
- **Evidence.** Georgios was born in Cyprus ca. 550 and died ca. 620. Antonius wrote "prior to 634 AD".
- **Evidence.** During the Persian invasion of 614 the monks abandoned the monastery; "The Cells" seem "never resettled".
- **Evidence.** Later sources do not mention "The Cells": the Commemoratorium (808), the Georgian Lectionary, Epiphanius, Daniel (1106) and an anonymous Greek pilgrim (1253). Phocas mentions monks living in caves after 1179.
- **Evidence.** The monastery was rebuilt in 1878–1901 under the hegoumenos Kallinikos.
- **Evidence, p. 208.** The cell above the monastery's present gate (Fig. 2) is assigned by local tradition to Ioannes Chozibites. It belonged to the Byzantine coenobium, and the monk Gabriel lived there from 1935 (n. 22).
- **Evidence, p. 208; n. 19.** The cells were explored in January–March 1986 and measured in winter 1986–87, as part of the Archaeological Survey of Caves in the Judean Desert (from November 1983, Hebrew University).
- **Evidence, n. 19.** The team was B. Arubas (surveyor), H. Kali and B. Agur (climbers). The Hebrew report is Patrich, Arubas and Kali, "The Monastic Cells of St. George Monastery", *Israel: Land and Nation* 4 (1986–7) 179–193.

## What the source does not say

- It does not mention the wall opposite the monastery (SWP pp. 227–228), its lower culvert, Jisr ed-Deir, or any outlet set in an aqueduct wall. It gives no wall section, ground datum, construction joint or stratified contact for any Qelt aqueduct feature.
- It gives no evidence for "Hasmonean" (p. 208), the only stated period for the aqueduct. Fig. 1 does not show the aqueduct's course.
- It gives no window date (50 BCE–135 CE) for any individual cistern, basin, wall or tomb. The Roman indications are "some of the caves" (n. 24), cave 20's lamp and jug, cave 14's "Roman and Byzantine sherds", and a path arrowhead. The cave 2 basin is only "might have been" pre-Byzantine.
- It does not say what Fig. 1 nos. 20 and 21 are, beyond the cave-20 finds in n. 24.
- It gives no grid references or coordinates.
- It says nothing on the name Koziba before the 5th century AD, on Cozeba of 1 Chr 4:22, or on ʿEin Kuweiziba.
- It does not mention the Copper Scroll, Qumran, Jerusalem tombs, the Kidron, Siloam or the Mount of Olives.
- It reports no Second Temple tombs at Choziba. The burials are of Byzantine monks (cave 1, 213 inscriptions) and of modern hermits (cell 16).
