# Patrich 1989, "The Aqueducts of Hyrcania": English extraction

**Citation.** J. Patrich, "The Aqueducts of Hyrcania" (Hebrew), in D. Amit, Y. Hirschfeld and J. Patrich (eds.), *The Aqueducts of Ancient Palestine* (Jerusalem: Yad Izhak Ben-Zvi, 1989), pp. 243–260.

**Pages read.** Printed pp. 243–260, all 18 scan pages (PDF p. 1 = printed p. 243 ... PDF p. 18 = printed p. 260). I read them from 150 dpi renders and checked the key passages and plan labels at 220 dpi. The chapter has no English summary in this scan.

**Conventions.** **Evidence** means the author states it; the printed page and figure follow. **Inference** means my own reading or deduction. Point numbers (1–49) are the author's station numbers on the Fig. 1 map. The Hebrew quoted below comes to about 13 words in total.

## A. Introduction and history of research (pp. 243–244)

1. **Evidence (p. 243).** Hyrcania lies in the northern Judean Desert, on the western edge of the valley called the "Buqei'a of Hyrcania" (Arabic el-Buqei'a). It was founded in the Hasmonean period by Alexander Jannaeus (printed "103–74 BCE") or perhaps earlier by his father John Hyrcanus (135–103 BCE), after whom it is named. It was one of the three fortresses that Shelamzion kept in her own hands, where she stored most of her treasures. Gabinius destroyed it in 57 BCE on Pompey's orders. It was rebuilt, held by one of the last Hasmonean pretenders, and taken from her by Herod after a long siege, around 31 BCE. Herod rebuilt it at great expense.
2. **Evidence (p. 243).** Hyrcania is not mentioned again after Herod's death, nor among the desert fortresses at the time of the Great Revolt, "and it seems the place was abandoned." In 492 CE St Sabas founded a monastery (Kastellion) among the ruins. The monastery outlasted the Muslim conquest and was finally abandoned only in the mid-14th century (n. 1).
3. **Evidence (pp. 243–244).** The British Survey noted the aqueduct reaching the fortress from the northwest and placed its head at Bir el-'Amra, east of Jebel Muntar (n. 2). Mader briefly mentioned two channels without describing their courses (n. 3). Wright thought there was a single aqueduct, coming from the direction of Jebel Muntar, and described the bridge below the fortress in detail (n. 4).
4. **Evidence (p. 244).** The main research was done by Yosef Feldman in 1971. He was the first to trace the southern aqueduct, which comes along a winding course from Nahal Qidron. He dug many test pits along it, and a Survey Department team made a detailed map for him. He measured and mapped the cisterns and pools on the southern and eastern slopes and dug soundings in some of them. He also traced the northern aqueduct and found that it begins at a dam in Wadi Abu Shu'ala, not at Bir el-'Amra further west. Beyond the maps, he published only a preliminary report (n. 5). Patrich re-examined the aqueducts and cisterns in 1983 for the Map of Mar Saba of the Archaeological Survey of Israel, using Feldman's test pits (n. 6).

## B. Courses and history of the aqueducts (p. 244)

5. **Evidence (p. 244, Fig. 1).** In the Second Temple period two aqueducts brought runoff and floodwater to the fortress. The **northern aqueduct** is the shorter one, 1,950 m long, and is "almost certainly also the earlier." The **southern aqueduct** comes from the Qidron and is about 9 km long. Both are plastered along their whole length with white plaster made of lime and grits (*חצצים*). Depending on terrain and rock, the channel is built, rock-cut or dug. It is about 0.5 m wide.
6. **Evidence (p. 244).** The southern aqueduct runs mostly through hilly country of soft, crumbly Senonian chalk, where its remains have sometimes vanished entirely. Remains are clearer where it crosses gullies cut into hard Turonian limestone. There the channel is rock-cut, and sometimes high supporting walls were needed to bridge the gully. The northern aqueduct also runs mostly through soft Senonian chalk, close to gullies cut in hard limestone.
7. **Evidence (p. 244).** The two aqueducts join about 750 m west of the fortress. About 500 m west of Hyrcania the shared course crosses young, crumbly beds of the Hatrurim Formation (*חתרורים*), which required a built channel. In the three saddles on the way, the aqueduct ran on thick supporting walls that served as bridges. The eastern saddle is about 48 m below the summit of Hyrcania, which stands 248 m above sea level. From there westward the aqueduct runs roughly along the 240 m contour. "The height of the eastern bridge thus fixed the level of the line of cisterns on the slopes."
8. **Evidence (p. 244): phasing.** All three periods of activity on the hill, Hasmonean, Herodian and Byzantine, are represented in the waterworks.
   - **Hasmonean.** The lower courses of the eastern bridge (no. 48) are Hasmonean. At that time the bridge rose only high enough to fill the four lower cisterns on the south side of the hill, and the northern aqueduct fed the cisterns. The Hasmonean bridge was probably destroyed with the fortress by Gabinius in 57 BCE.
   - **Herodian.** Herod's costly rebuilding included renewing and enlarging the water supply. "Now the southern aqueduct was erected," which greatly increased the water reaching the fortress. The eastern bridge was raised, and more cisterns were cut at a higher level on the south and east sides of the hill.
   - **After Herod.** When the fortress was abandoned, the Herodian eastern bridge partly collapsed.
   - **Byzantine.** The bridge was then too low to fill the upper cisterns, only the lower ones. The Byzantines apparently brought the northern aqueduct back into use without needing plaster repairs. Of the southern aqueduct, perhaps only a short stretch was reused, from point 42 onward. Clear Byzantine channel remains survive against the north side of the western bridge (46) and on top of the eastern bridge (48). Between them the Byzantine line runs parallel to the earlier one, slightly lower and to its north.

## C. The southern aqueduct (pp. 244–251)

9. **Evidence (p. 244): head of the southern aqueduct.** The aqueduct begins on the south bank of the Qidron. After about 1,300 m it crosses the stream and continues along the north side. "In the field we found no remains at all of a diversion dam (*סכר הטיה*) or other regulating installations at the starting point." There is also no bridge at the crossing point and no channel along the stretch between the two points. "Nevertheless the course is reasonable topographically." Remains first appear on the surface and in Feldman's test pits only on the stretch north of the stream, and then east of the Qidron's channel.
10. **Evidence (p. 244): point 1.** "A small test pit, in which a plastered stretch of the eastern wall of the channel was exposed." That is all the chapter says about point 1. It gives no dam, building material, finds or date for it.
11. **Inference (Fig. 1, p. 243).** On the map the stretch west of point 1, running upstream along Nahal Qidron, is drawn in the "aqueduct (estimated course)" style. It runs about 1.2–1.3 km by the map's scale, which agrees with the 1,300 m stated in the text. Point 1 carries no dam symbol, although the legend has one. The intake is therefore a reconstruction, not a recorded structure. The chapter does not mention the Small Lavra. Mar Saba (the Great Lavra) is marked about 3 km downstream, to the southeast.
12. **Evidence (p. 244).** Point 2: wall remains 1.95 m wide on the north bank of Wadi Abu el-Kalb, which served as the bridge carrying the aqueduct across the wadi.
13. **Evidence (p. 245).** Point 3: a test pit exposing the channel floor, about 50 cm wide, rock-cut and plastered. Point 4: a short rock-cut stretch among houses of the 'Ubeidiya tribe. Point 5: a test pit exposing a rock-cut, plastered stretch, 40 cm wide at the top and about 30 cm at the bottom. Beyond 5 the course shows as a horizontal quarrying line, with threshing floors below it. Further on are old terraces, none of which follows the line of the aqueduct wall. "These terraces are therefore later than the Second Temple period."
14. **Evidence (p. 245).** Point 6: near Bir Ibrahim, remains of the aqueduct's supporting wall. Above it is a square Byzantine cistern, plastered with reddish plaster mixed with sherds. About 50 m east of it, on the aqueduct line, is a round building (outer diameter 3.40 m, walls 0.7 m) of large field stones. Nearby runs the western fence of a terraced field, linked to a Byzantine building above Bir Baquq.
15. **Evidence (p. 245).** Point 7: the aqueduct crosses Wadi Baquq on a winding course and can be followed for about 200 m east of the vehicle road that crosses the Qidron, where Feldman dug test pits. From here it runs parallel to the Qidron gorge, along the contact between soft Senonian chalk and harder Turonian limestone. It is cut in Turonian rock in some places and carried on a built wall in others.
16. **Evidence (p. 245, Figs. 2–4).** Point 8: test pit, one wall rock-cut and one built, plaster remains, floor width about 0.5 m (Fig. 2). Point 9: about 50 m on, a fully rock-cut stretch, floor width about 0.5 m and wall height about 0.75 m (Fig. 3). The course is fully rock-cut only where a hanging rock step forces it. Where the slope is gentle, the builders usually cut a step and completed the second wall in masonry. Point 10: section similar to the preceding (Fig. 4); channel about 0.55 m wide, on a rock-cut step, with its built western wall on lower steps.
17. **Evidence (pp. 245–246, Fig. 5).** Point 11 is on the lip of a steep cliff and is fully rock-cut, preserved to its full height of about 0.75 m. It is 0.60 m wide at the top and about 0.35 m at the bottom. In the Byzantine period this stretch was blocked and re-plastered as a reservoir, with a settling pit built above it, fed by slope runoff guided by a dam wall (*קיר סוכר*). Below the aqueduct, on the rock step, runs a path to a Byzantine complex of dwelling cells belonging to the Great Lavra of St Sabas. These east-bank stretches appear on the Survey Department's 1:50,000 maps. Feldman was the first to connect them with Hyrcania.
18. **Evidence (p. 246).** Point 12: an old quarry above the aqueduct, with a path, partly paved, passing it to a larger quarry. Building stones were taken from these quarries and carried along the path to the building sites. Point 13: on Turonian rock the aqueduct sits on a built wall whose south side survives 3 courses (1.3 m) high (Fig. 6); about 10 m further west it reaches 2.10 m.
19. **Evidence (pp. 246–247).** Point 14: the aqueduct crosses a small gully. The lowest course has two drainage openings that served as culverts against collapse from runoff. The channel runs on top of a supporting wall (Fig. 7); a plastered floor survives on top of the 2.75 m wall, above a rock drop of about 15 m. Point 15: 30 m on, the supporting wall reaches about 7 m (Fig. 8), with a terrace wall about 10 m downslope to stabilise the ground and give the builders a working surface. Point 16: a gully crossing; the lowest course is massive, with three drainage openings, and alluvium has built up behind it. Point 17: a rock-cut stretch 3 m long, 0.5 m wide and 0.7 m high.
20. **Evidence (pp. 247–248).** Point 18: a 1.75 m wall descends along the spur, perhaps carrying a branch of the channel to a building that may have been a small watchtower. Its stones were apparently robbed for the Great Lavra cells below. A second wall (0.70–0.80 m) may be a fence wall of the Lavra, whose northern continuation was founded on the earlier aqueduct. Further on, the aqueduct winds through five gully crossings before turning north toward Hyrcania.
21. **Evidence (p. 248).** Point 19: a rock-cut stretch. Point 20: a lime-quarrying cave, identified by its white dump. Point 21: crossings on simple terrace walls. Point 22: a stretch with a rock-cut wall, the built wall collapsed. Points 23–25: test pits. After a further gully, the aqueduct turns east, then northeast, and at a low saddle crosses the vehicle road down to the Buqei'a. Points 26–28: terrace-wall crossings, about 2 m high at 26, and test pits.
22. **Evidence (p. 248).** Point 29: on Turonian rock a deep gully is bridged by a supporting wall about 7.5 m high to the channel floor and about 2.5 m thick at the base. It is the first of three similar bridges on the southern aqueduct before the junction. The channel is about 1 m wide at the north end of the bridge and narrows to the normal 0.45 m about 10 m on. A culvert at the base let floodwater pass without undermining the foundations. Near the south end, above the aqueduct, are the remains of a square building (3.20 × 3.20 m, door on the east), possibly for guarding and maintaining the bridge. Point 30: about 50 m on, a white dump, probably a quarry cave for making lime.
23. **Evidence (pp. 248–249, Figs. 9–10).** Point 31: a terrace-wall crossing. Point 32: a test pit; higher up, a dam wall protecting the aqueduct below. Point 33: a rock-cut, plastered channel 0.52 m wide. Point 34: the second bridge, over a deep gully in Turonian rock (Fig. 9). It is 2.5 m thick at the base and up to about 17 m high to the gully floor, with plaster on the rock at both ends fixing the channel level. The culvert type is unclear (arch or simple culvert). North of it the channel runs for about 150 m on a high supporting wall, with the rock face serving as its inner wall. Point 35: both channel walls rock-cut; floor 0.48 m wide, top 0.60 m, walls 0.40–0.50 m high (Fig. 10).
24. **Evidence (p. 250).** Point 36: a low terrace wall (no more than 0.8 m) across the gully, probably to divert gully water into the channel. Point 37: a plastered channel, one wall rock face and one built; floor width about 0.50 m; the section is shaped like an inverted Hebrew letter *het*. Point 38: a crossing on a terrace wall about 2.5 m high and 6 m long, possibly a dam wall feeding floodwater into the main line through a very short secondary channel. Point 39: plaster pieces mark the course.
25. **Evidence (pp. 250–251, Figs. 11–13): construction phases.** Point 40 is the third bridge, over Wadi 'Idrei. About 5 m of it survives at the gully floor. Its total width is 3.6 m: "at first the wall was 2.5 m wide, and at a later stage a further supporting wall, 1.1 m thick, was added on its east side" (Fig. 12). The chapter does not date the two phases. The reconstructed height is about 9 m to the channel floor and the length about 16 m. Whether the water passed under an arch or through a simple culvert is unknown.
26. **Evidence (p. 251).** Point 41: a gully crossing on a lower bridge, with a stretch of supporting wall parallel to it; another branch comes in from above the gully. Point 42: a dam that diverted the gully water into the channel. It blocked a stream that could have damaged the bridge and increased the water collected. "Possibly in the Byzantine period this stretch of the southern aqueduct, from the dam onward, returned to use." Beyond 41 the supporting wall skirts the spur on which the vehicle road descends to the Buqei'a. Further on, "the course is visible as a path," without a supporting wall, crossing several gullies on simple terrace walls.
27. **Evidence (p. 251).** Point 43: the junction of the northern and southern aqueducts lies near the western end of bridge 46, "but the junction point is not recognisable in the field."

## D. The northern aqueduct (pp. 251–252)

28. **Evidence (p. 251): point 44, the dam at the head.** The dam is 17.5 m long and built diagonally across Wadi Abu Shu'ala, so that the stream was turned into the aqueduct on the south bank. "The dam is built on the lip of a waterfall about 8 m high. East of here the stream deepens and takes on a canyon-like (*קניוני*) character." The dam is 4–5 m wide and built of large stones; only one course is visible.
29. **Evidence (p. 251): crosses and a trail mark at 44.** About 50 m from the dam a cross is incised on the rock face above the aqueduct. "At this spot there is also a black trail-marking (*סימון שבילים שחור*) on the rock face." A second incised cross lies further along the aqueduct's course. "This suggests that this aqueduct, built apparently in Hasmonean times, was in use also in the Byzantine period."
30. **Inference.** The black trail-marking is evidently a modern marked hiking trail (painted trail blazes) about 50 m from dam 44, that is, upstream of junction 43. It is the only trail the text records on the northern aqueduct upstream of 43. The text does not say whether the trail crosses the aqueduct or follows it.
31. **Evidence (p. 251, Fig. 14): point 45.** A test pit about 300 m from the dam exposed a plastered stretch of channel floor. The channel is about 1 m wide, roughly double the southern channel. As Feldman suggested, it was meant to catch a large volume of floodwater flowing for a short time. "The plaster is made of lime and grits, and is typical of the Second Temple period. Along the whole aqueduct no signs of Byzantine repairs were found." If it was used then, it was still sound and fit for reuse. Built and rock-cut stretches alternate, the course is visible for most of its length, and it is easy to follow.
32. **Evidence (p. 251): at junction 43.** The northern aqueduct reaches junction 43 at a much higher level than the southern one and loses height as it descends to the bridge. "It is reasonable to assume" that at the junction, which is also where flow onto the bridge begins, there was a regulating and settling device, "a kind of *castellum*," to catch and slow the water before it passed over the bridge. "On the surface, however, no remains of such an installation are visible."

## E. The three bridges of the shared course (pp. 251–255)

33. **Evidence (pp. 251–252, Figs. 15–16): western bridge, point 46.** The exact junction and the start of the bridge are not known, so the bridge's original height cannot be reconstructed. It crosses the saddle along the 240 m contour. It is a massive wall 2.35–2.75 m thick, built through its full thickness of large, partly dressed stones (Fig. 15); the top with the channel is missing. It is about 150 m long and bends slightly south about 80 m from its east end. In the gully north of the saddle are the remains of a lime kiln, probably built when the aqueduct was constructed.
34. **Evidence (p. 252, Fig. 16).** Against the north face of the earlier bridge runs a Byzantine aqueduct, channel about 0.4 m wide. It has one layer of "typical Byzantine plaster" with red crushed sherds, laid on stone chips set in mud. Its south wall, against the earlier bridge, is of flat brick-like stones; its north wall is faced with large stones probably taken from the upper part of the earlier bridge. Its position on the north side "suggests that the water collected in it came from the north, in the Wadi Abu Shu'ala aqueduct."
35. **Evidence (p. 252): path, downstream of 43.** From the western bridge two aqueducts continue on slightly different lines: "the earlier aqueduct runs at a higher level, while the path follows the level of the lower aqueduct, which is the Byzantine aqueduct."
36. **Evidence (p. 252): central bridge, point 47.** It spans the next saddle and is about 50 m long. Bedouin goat pens now abut both sides. "The path runs at a lower level." Here the Byzantine aqueduct does not abut the bridge. The centre survives 1.4 m above ground; the east end is 2.3 m higher than the centre, so the supporting wall stood more than 3.7 m high at the centre.
37. **Evidence (p. 252, Figs. 17–19): eastern bridge, point 48.** The bridge stands in a saddle created by quarrying away the rock until only a narrow rock strip remained, "over which the access path to the fortress also passes" (Fig. 17). Its upper part is built through its full width; its lower part, east of the saddle, is a rock-cut wall (Fig. 18). It is 112 m long in all. "Its western end lies west of the point where the path on its right crosses the aqueduct." Being next to the fortress, it is more carefully built than any other part of the aqueduct (Fig. 19).
38. **Evidence (pp. 253–254, Figs. 20–21): masonry phases.** In several places the lowest courses (exposed mostly on the north side; on the south side they are hidden by the path beside the bridge, which also holds Byzantine plaster fragments) differ in technique and dressing from the rest. "These lower courses can be attributed to the Hasmonean period, while all the rest can be seen as Herodian building, with light repairs in the upper parts made in the Byzantine period."
    - The four lowest courses above the pools are header-and-stretcher (stretchers about 75 cm, headers about 30 cm, courses about 40 cm), with drafted margins 8–10 cm wide and a boss projecting about 6 cm.
    - The higher (Herodian) courses are less strictly header-and-stretcher, with long, near-square and narrow blocks 25–80 cm long. They are nearly isodomic, with 40 cm courses and smoothed bosses, and set back slightly every 2–3 courses (Fig. 21). "The Herodian stones were dressed with less care than the stones of the Hasmonean courses," with irregular joints levelled by small flat stones and a bonding of mud and lime.
39. **Evidence (p. 255).** The building stone was quarried from the local Hatrurim rock, which is very hard to dress into complete ashlars. The saddle and the area west of it served as quarries for the aqueduct and the summit buildings. Quarrying left only the rock strip carrying the aqueduct and "created the pools north and south of the bridge."
40. **Evidence (p. 255): Herodian bridge height.** The Herodian channel ran higher than the top of the surviving bridge wall and is lost. At the bridge's western end, above the pools, the lower Byzantine channel with "the typical reddish plaster" is visible; it also survives rock-cut and plastered near the east end. Remains of the earlier aqueduct on the slope of the Hyrcania hill stand about 70 cm above the top of a rock outcrop. From this the author infers that the Herodian channel floor was 1.20 m above the present top of the bridge's west end (west of the regular footpath), and that the Herodian bridge's original height there was about 12.5 m. Today it is about 6 m high, 2.5 m wide at the base and 1.45 m at the top. The lost upper part may have been arcaded (see the reconstruction, Fig. 17). Alternatively, a metal pipe (*גיחון*, apparently a pressure pipe) at the top lifted the water to the required height.

## F. The pools beside the eastern bridge (p. 255, Figs. 17, 22, 23)

41. **Evidence (p. 255, Fig. 22): the double pool.** "North of the bridge there is a pair of pools, separated by a partition 4.80–5.40 m wide" (n. 7). Fig. 22 labels them *בריכה כפולה* ("double pool"). They were formed by quarrying stone for the bridge and the summit buildings. The outline is rectangular, but each side has a different length. The walls are not cut vertically to their full height and the floor is not levelled. The east side of both pools is not cut but descends inward in steps, as does the south side of the southern pool.
42. **Evidence (p. 255): which is larger.** "The northern pool is the more complete and the deeper of the two." The west side of the southern pool reaches at most 2.6 m. The northern pool sits lower relative to the saddle, and its west side reaches at most 5 m. Crosses are incised on the rock face in places. The northern pool measures **19 m on the south, 15 m on the west, 18.2 m on the north and 16 m on the east.** **No dimensions are given for the southern member.**
43. **Evidence (p. 255).** On that side (the east), three parallel rows of rock-cut benches on the hill slope above the pool "suggest that this reservoir also served as a swimming pool." Fig. 22 labels them "rock-cut benches." Feldman's soundings exposed large patches of lime-and-grit plaster on the walls and floor, "the hydraulic plaster typical of the Second Temple period."
44. **Evidence (p. 255, Fig. 23).** Northeast of the northern pool is a rock-cut cistern with a perfectly round outline and vertical walls, 3.05 m in diameter and 4.3 m deep; its purpose is unclear. Feldman found three lime kilns around the fortress.
45. **Evidence (p. 255): access path and use.** "Along the eastern edge of the pools passed an access path, which cut through the rock wall carrying the aqueduct in a rock-cut passage, whose cutting marks are still visible on its western side" (n. 8). The pools served as reserve reservoirs for the cisterns on the east and south slopes and on the summit: water could be carried from them by pack animals to fill the upper reservoirs.
46. **Evidence: how the pools were fed.** The text describes no inlet conduit or feeder channel for either pool. Fig. 22 draws none: the aqueduct (bridge) runs along their south side. The only conduit mentioned near them is the Byzantine channel at the bridge's west end "above the pools" (p. 255).
47. **Inference.** The pools lie directly beside and below the aqueduct bridge, in the quarried saddle. A spill from the channel or runoff into the quarry is plausible, but the chapter does not show how water got in.
48. **Evidence (p. 255): the southern pool.** South of the bridge is a deep area bounded on the west by a vertical quarried wall. No plaster is visible on it, though deeper parts may survive below the present surface. Like the northern pools, it also served as a moat cutting the fortress off from the spur approaching from the west, the side of easy approach.
49. **Evidence: dating of the pools.** The pools were formed by the quarrying for the bridge and buildings (p. 255) and carry Second Temple-type plaster (p. 255). Note 7 says that a pair of swimming pools appears twice in the Hasmonean palace at Jericho, which "may hint that the pair of pools at Hyrcania was already installed in the Hasmonean period." The summary (p. 259) links their use as swimming pools, with rows of spectator seats, to the abundance of water that reached the fortress at the system's Herodian peak. The chapter gives no separate date for either member.
50. **Inference (Fig. 22).** The double pool lies northwest of the summit, on the north side of the bridge that approaches the fortress from the northwest. The caller's "west of the summit" fits this.

## G. The cisterns (pp. 255–259, Figs. 22, 24–28)

51. **Evidence (p. 255).** Past the bridge, the aqueduct split into a northern and a southern branch around the summit, feeding cisterns cut in its slopes. The northern branch fed the eastern cisterns. On the south, Feldman found two aqueducts at two different levels, which fed the southern cisterns. In each period the aqueduct crossed the bridge at a single level.
    - The lower line was "almost certainly" linked to the Hasmonean bridge, which was lower.
    - The upper line was in use later, in the Herodian period, when the bridge was rebuilt much higher than its Hasmonean predecessor. This allowed the upper row of cisterns to be cut; these could take the extra water brought by the southern aqueduct, "which was also built at that time."
    - Presumably a channel ran down from the upper southern branch to the lower aqueduct and fed the lower cisterns in the Herodian period as well. That connecting channel has not been found.
52. **Evidence (p. 256).** Feldman counted 20 rock-cut cisterns and two built pools, with a combined capacity of about 20,000 m³. They are coated with thick hydraulic plaster of lime and grits, with cracks packed with small stones before plastering. Five cisterns are on the summit, eight in the upper row south of the fortress, four in the lower row, and three on the east slope.
    - The five summit cisterns (H, L, P, X, K1) were fed from roofs and courtyards, not by the aqueducts. P is well vaulted, with a wide flight of steps, and may have been a miqveh.
    - In K1 papyri in Arabic, Greek and Syriac of the 7th–9th centuries CE were found in 1952–53.
    - Cave K, lower down, was a Byzantine burial chapel. It has no upper drawing shaft, only a small opening, and no feeding channel, so it is doubtful that it was a cistern (Feldman's no. 21).
53. **Evidence (p. 257): Hasmonean cisterns.** The lower southern branch feeds cisterns N, O, E and J; J lies much lower than the rest. "These should be seen as Hasmonean cisterns." The upper southern branch feeds A, A1, M, B, C, D, F and G and ends in a pool with plastered walls and a thick east wall, now full of fallen debris and silt. A second, built, stepped pool lies southwest of cistern O (Fig. 24; n. 9). Between N and A the aqueduct runs through a built tunnel (point 49): 11.5 m clear, 1.25 m high, 0.75 m wide, roofed with a long-stone vault (Fig. 25).
54. **Evidence (p. 259).** The northern branch of the upper aqueduct feeds R, S and T on the east side. S is a small stepped cistern, perhaps a private miqveh (Fig. 26). F is a larger stepped cistern, perhaps a public miqveh (Fig. 27). On F's walls are crosses painted in black and several Greek inscriptions incised in the plaster, made when the cistern served as a hermit's cell (Fig. 28). The Byzantine aqueduct apparently fed only E and J; at E's entrance is a settling basin plastered with the reddish plaster typical of the Byzantine period.
55. **Evidence (p. 259): the Herodian coin in cistern C.** "In some test excavations Feldman made in several cisterns, and especially in cistern C (beside the steps), many sherds were found (lamps, large jars and other vessels), most of them of the Herodian period, and also a Herodian coin" (n. 10). "No vessels of a later period were found, and hence the upper cisterns were not in use later."

## H. Summary (p. 259)

56. **Evidence (p. 259).** Hyrcania's aqueducts are among the longest and most complex of the desert-fortress water systems.
    - They began in the Hasmonean period, under Alexander Jannaeus or his father John Hyrcanus, and peaked under Herod.
    - With so much water, the pools north of the eastern bridge could be used as swimming pools, and rows of spectator seats were built there.
    - The topography forced high bridges: three over deep gullies on the southern aqueduct, and three more in the saddles on the shared course.
    - The bridges were the system's weak points: the collapse of any one of them, especially on the shared course, would put the whole system out of use.
    - "It may be that the difficulties of maintaining the water supply were among the reasons that led to the abandonment of the fortress after Herod's days." Accordingly, it is not mentioned among the desert fortresses after his death, and even in the Great Revolt the Zealots did not seek refuge there.

## Answers

**1. Southern system, intake / station 1.** The chapter does not support a dam at station 1. Point 1 is "a small test pit" exposing a plastered stretch of the channel's east wall (p. 244). The author states that at the starting point "we found no remains at all of a diversion dam or other regulating installations." He also found no bridge at the stream crossing and no channel between the start and the crossing; the reconstructed course is only "reasonable topographically" (p. 244).
- The 1,300 m figure is the length of the (unrecorded) stretch on the south bank before the aqueduct crosses the Qidron. It is not the distance of a dam from the Small Lavra, which the chapter never mentions. On Fig. 1 that stretch is drawn as an estimated course upstream (west) of point 1, with no dam symbol (Inference).
- **Gorge, narrows, canyon:** the chapter is silent for station 1. The only canyon wording in the chapter belongs to the northern dam 44 ("canyon-like," p. 251).
- **Material:** the chapter is silent.
- **Date:** there is no plaster, find or phase evidence for point 1 itself. The southern aqueduct as a whole is dated Herodian (p. 244). The argument is one of phasing: the Hasmonean bridge was low and the northern aqueduct fed the four lower cisterns; Herod's rebuilding raised the eastern bridge, added the upper cisterns, and "now the southern aqueduct was erected." Corroboration: the upper cisterns it fed yielded only Herodian-period finds, including the Herodian coin in cistern C (p. 259, n. 10). The plaster along both aqueducts is the same white lime-and-grits type (p. 244), so plaster does not separate Hasmonean from Herodian here.

**2. Double rock-cut pool (Fig. 22, p. 256; text p. 255).**
- **Location:** north of the eastern bridge, in the quarried saddle northwest of the summit.
- **Sizes:** the members are separated by a 4.80–5.40 m partition. The northern pool measures 19 m (S), 15 m (W), 18.2 m (N) and 16 m (E), with its west side up to 5 m high. For the southern pool only the 2.6 m maximum height of its west side is given.
- **Which is larger:** the northern pool is called "the more complete and the deeper." The chapter does not state outright which is larger by area.
- **Feeding:** the chapter describes no inlet conduit for either pool, and Fig. 22 draws none. The pools are described only as quarry hollows used as reserve reservoirs, from which water was carried by pack animals to the upper cisterns.
- **Date:** Second Temple-type lime-and-grits plaster (p. 255). Note 7 compares the paired pools of the Hasmonean palace at Jericho and suggests a possible Hasmonean installation. The summary ties their use as swimming pools, with spectator benches, to the Herodian peak (p. 259). There is no separate date for either member.

**3. Northern system.**
- **Junction 43:** near the west end of bridge 46, not recognisable in the field. A *castellum*-like regulating device is assumed there but no remains survive (p. 251).
- **Dam 44:** 17.5 m long, built diagonally across Wadi Abu Shu'ala on the lip of an 8 m waterfall, below which the wadi becomes "canyon-like." It is 4–5 m wide, of large stones, with one course visible (p. 251).
- **Station 45:** about 300 m from the dam; channel about 1 m wide, plastered in Second Temple-type lime and grits, with no Byzantine repairs (p. 251, Fig. 14).
- **Station 48:** the eastern bridge, 112 m long, in a saddle quarried down to a narrow rock strip that also carries the access path; Hasmonean lower courses, Herodian above (pp. 252–255, Figs. 17–21).
- **Trails upstream of 43:** the only one is a "black trail-marking" on the rock face about 50 m from dam 44, beside an incised cross (p. 251). Its relation to the aqueduct (crossing or following) is not stated. Fig. 1 also shows undefined thin dashed tracks near 45–46 (Inference).
- **Paths downstream of 43:** the path follows the lower, Byzantine aqueduct line from bridge 46 (p. 252). It runs lower than bridge 47 (p. 252), crosses the aqueduct at bridge 48 (p. 252), and runs along the pools' east edge through a rock-cut passage (p. 255).

**4. Dating overall.**
- **Hasmonean:** the northern aqueduct ("almost certainly the earlier," p. 244; "built apparently in Hasmonean times," p. 251), the lower courses of bridge 48 (identified by their different masonry and dressing, pp. 253–254), the lower aqueduct line, and cisterns N, O, E, J (pp. 255, 257).
- **Herodian:** the southern aqueduct, the raising of bridge 48, the upper channel, and the upper cisterns (pp. 244, 255). The Herodian sherds and coin in cistern C, with nothing later, show that the upper cisterns went out of use (p. 259).
- **Plaster typology:** white lime-and-grits means Second Temple, in both aqueducts and the pools. Reddish plaster with crushed sherds means Byzantine (pp. 244, 251–252, 255).
- **Construction joins:** the 1.1 m addition to the Wadi 'Idrei bridge (pt 40) is a later phase but is not dated (p. 250).
- **Byzantine:** reuse of the northern aqueduct (crosses near 44, the channel along bridge 46) and perhaps of the southern line from 42 on (pp. 244, 251–252).
- **Abandonment after Herod:** stated explicitly. Hyrcania is not mentioned after Herod's death or in the Great Revolt; the bridges were weak points, and the difficulty of maintaining the water supply "may have been among the reasons" for the abandonment (pp. 243, 259).
