## Project questions and the principal findings

**[BK—project premise]** The project associates Entry 28 with a “cairn” at the High Priest’s ford/bridge, compares a lower-Jordan location with Wilson’s Arch, and compares Entries 57–59 with Zertal’s B7 and the candidates Upper/Lower Kh. Ibziq and Kh. Salhab. Those identifications and readings were supplied by the project; this extraction does not independently verify them.

**[E]** Dorsey’s relevant northeastern road is **S12**, through Tubas/Kh. edh Dhuq, Kh. Salhab and Kh. Ibziq toward Rehob and Beth-shean. His **S13** describes the main Roman Neapolis–Beth-shean-valley road as taking a more southerly course through Tayasir, Qasr esh Sheikh Ghazal and Qaʿun. For Kh. Ibziq he expressly writes “**possibly not biblical Bezek**” (p. 173). The distinction between the two roads is material to the project.

**[E]** The Jericho road accounts name **Makhadat Hajla**, **el Ghoraniya** and **Makhadat Mindisa** (pp. 202, 205). Adam/T. Damiya and Makhadat Abu Sidra belong to the farther-north Shechem/Tubas corridors (pp. 150–151, 173–174, 176–177). Dorsey does not identify a High Priest’s ford in these accounts.

**[I]** Dorsey provides a network within which the project can test candidates. He does not supply a Copper Scroll itinerary, an identification of Entry 28, or a demonstration that Zertal’s B7 is identical to S12 or S13. In particular, the project’s Bezek–Tubas sequence should not silently replace Dorsey’s Tubas–Ibziq ordering.

## (c) Shechem–Beth-shean, Bezek and the Roman road

1. **[E] Northeastern route:** S12 explicitly starts in the Shechem area, proceeds to Tubas/Kh. edh Dhuq, through Kh. Ibziq to Rehob and Beth-shean. Its evidentiary chain includes Kh. Salhab. Dorsey supplies one Kh. Ibziq location and **does not divide it into Upper and Lower sites** (pp. 171–173).
2. **[E] Main Roman route:** S13 follows the more southerly Tayasir–Medio–Conas–T. Malqoah corridor. Bronze/Iron occupation at the foot and Iron I finds at Tayasir suggest an earlier route, but the explicit Roman description is S13, not S12 (p. 173).
3. **[E] Shechem–Tubas approach:** N22 runs from Shechem along Wadi Beidan’s western edge to Tirzah, then toward Tubas; Dorsey describes Roman as well as Iron Age evidence. He also supplies a western Tirzah–Tubas alternative via Kh. Kashda (pp. 144–145).
4. **[E] Bezek qualification:** Aharoni’s reconstruction quoted on p. 119 uses “Tirzah and Bezek.” The detailed S12 account calls Kh. Ibziq “possibly not biblical Bezek” (p. 173). These should both be recorded rather than converted into a firm Ibziq = Bezek identification.
5. **[I] Relation to Zertal B7:** N22 plus S12 supplies a plausible Shechem–Tubas–Ibziq–Beth-shean network for comparison. It does **not** establish identity with B7, prove a route over a named “Bezek pass,” or show that the Roman Neapolis–Scythopolis road passed Salhab/Ibziq. The book supports treating S12 and S13 as alternatives whose detailed alignments need separate testing.
6. **[E—audit] Gerizim:** no separate Gerizim index entry or named Gerizim ascent was found in the checked material. The national-highway account explicitly discusses **Mount Ebal**, the Shechem pass and Makhana valley (pp. 136, 140, 143). **[I]** Shechem’s road junctions cannot therefore be turned into a documented Gerizim itinerary from this book alone.

**[E] Physical features:** Iron Age open-road preparation is reconstructed principally as clearing stones/obstructions and levelling where necessary; Dorsey distinguishes this from paved city streets (pp. 26–32). He gives **no clear evidence for stair-equipped ascents or tunnelling in Iron Age Israel**. Later Roman ascents could use numerous switchbacks and, at Maaleh Aqrabbim, stairs; a possible man-made pass near Adami-hannekeb is discussed as a suggestion (pp. 40–41). Foreign Mesopotamian/Egyptian rock-cutting and Persian/Greek road-building examples illustrate available techniques, not identified road cuttings at the project’s sites (pp. 28–30).

**[E] Crossing conditions:** fording was the normal biblical-period solution in his reconstruction; depth, swift currents, bad footing and winter/rainy-season floods could obstruct crossings (pp. 37–39). Josephus’s Peraeans fleeing Vespasian found the ford opposite Jericho swollen and unfordable (*War* IV:433–435; p. 38). Roman-period paving of some fords is noted generally (p. 39), without identifying Hajla, Mindisa or Ghoraniya as examples.

**[E] Later bridges/ferries:** Persian and Hellenistic boat bridges are attested outside Israel, notably in the examples drawn from Xenophon; Roman permanent bridge technology is discussed (p. 35). The Byzantine Madaba Map depicts two Jordan ferries, one opposite Jericho and one upstream. Dorsey finds no clear biblical or contemporary extrabiblical evidence for Iron Age ferries in Israel (pp. 36–37). These are period-specific observations, not continuity evidence for a named project road.

**[E] Markers:** no evidence for **inscribed Iron Age road signs** is supplied; Roman inscribed mile markers are contrasted with possible earlier uninscribed markers. Jer. 31:21 is positive literary evidence for marking a route for return; the practice’s extent is unknown, and Dorsey suggests a particular usefulness on desert tracks (pp. 50–51).

## What the book does NOT say about (a)–(e)

- **(a) Roads and dating — [E]** It does not establish continuous use of each reconstructed Iron Age alignment through the Hellenistic and Roman periods.
