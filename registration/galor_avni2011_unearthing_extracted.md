# Unearthing Jerusalem 2011: Temple Mount cisterns, the Siloam pool, a Tyropoeon house

**Labels:** **Evidence** = the chapter states it (an author's view stays the author's interpretation). **Inference** = drawn here. **BK** = none used.

## Chapter 12. J. Patrich, "The Location of the Second Temple and the Layout of its Courts, Gates, and Chambers: A New Proposal" (pp. 205–229)

### Cistern 5 and the laver (pp. 207–213)

- **Evidence.** The laver had a *mechane*, which Patrich takes as a geared water-lifting wheel of the sakiya type (pp. 209–210 and n. 14). From m. Tamid 1:4 he concludes "that a water cistern was located near the laver" (p. 210).
- **Evidence.** "Our proposal is that the cistern that fed the laver is cistern 5 on Wilson's map of 1876" (p. 210). Note 16: this is "cistern no. 28 in Gibson and Jacobson 1996: 288"; "it is impossible at present to descend into the cistern". The Fig. 1 caption cites Gibson and Jacobson 1996: 137 (p. 207).
- **Evidence — dimensions (p. 210).** 15 m deep. The main gallery is a corridor 54.5 m long and 4.6 m wide. At the east end two arms 9 m long and 3.5–4 m wide project north; the eastern one also runs 18 m south. At the west end an arm 17 m long, narrower and irregular, runs south; Patrich takes it for a natural fissure. A staircase about 1.5 m wide comes down into the corridor from the east and turns along the east wall of the north-east arm, where it is about 2.1 m wide.
- **Evidence — ceiling (p. 211 and n. 18).** A stone ceiling covers the south end of the south-east arm; most of the ceiling is plastered, which Patrich reads as masonry. So the cistern "when cut, was thus open to the sky" (citing Wilson 1866: 43; Schick 1896: 293–304, pl. IX; Gibson and Jacobson 1996: 135–40, fig. 65). Wilson gives that arm a rock ceiling; Gibson and Jacobson mark only its south end so.
- **Evidence.** The altar ramp is placed in the 11 m between the two north arms; the cistern's long axis, 9.7° south of east, is read as the temple's orientation (pp. 210, 213).
- **Evidence — identifications (pp. 215–217, 222, 225).** The south-east arm marks the Water Gate and the south-west arm (the fissure) the Gullah Chamber; "cistern 5 can be identified with the Gullah Cistern" (p. 217). Because it "is the only candidate that can be identified with the Gullah Cistern", the Gullah Chamber lay on the south of the court and the order of m. Mid. 5:3 is preferred to b. Yoma 19a (p. 222).
- **Evidence — phases (p. 216 n. 36; p. 217 n. 37, "cautiously").** An inset in the south wall of the main gallery "can be cautiously suggested" to mark the corner of an earlier cistern of the "Hellenistic/Hasmonean phase"; the gallery was later extended to join the fissure. Note 36 says the laver was moved "simultaneously with the cutting of cistern 5 from the fissure eastward". No finds, plaster sequence or section are cited for either phase, and the two notes describe the sequence differently.
- **Evidence — other cisterns.** Cistern 3, at right angles to cistern 5, "marks the site of the Chamber of Immersion" (p. 219 and n. 48, also Ritmeyer 2006: 345). Wilson's cisterns 2, 34 and 36 lie outside the court to the north (p. 222 n. 52). Qoren's Gullah Cistern is Wilson's no. 1 (= no. 31 of Gibson and Jacobson), "ca. 40 m long and 7.3 m wide, with a masonry vaulted ceiling" (p. 222 n. 53).
- **Evidence — the rock (p. 206).** The top of the Rock is 743.7 m a.s.l., 1.3–1.5 m below the level of the Herodian upper court; the lower platform of the Haram is at 738 m.

### Wilson's cistern 8 and the aqueduct (p. 211 n. 19)

- **Evidence.** The low-level aqueduct reached the Temple Mount over Wilson's Arch at about 735 m a.s.l. (A. Mazar 2002: 221) and could not feed the upper platform. "It fed cistern no. 8 on Wilson's map (cistern no. 3 in Gibson and Jacobson 1996: 33–41), on the southern side of the Temple Mount." Its top is at 734.9 m, "ca. 1.5 m lower than the floor level of the Haram at that place". The aqueduct "was apparently constructed under John Hyrcanus or Alexander Jannaeus" (Patrich 1981: 25–39; A. Mazar 2002: 217–23, 230–38).
- **Evidence.** The miqvaʾot on the Water Gate and the Parwah Chamber were, he says, fed by roof rainwater, not by the aqueduct (pp. 211–212 n. 19, p. 222 n. 54).

### The Wood[en] Chamber (pp. 220–225)

- **Evidence.** m. Mid. 5:3 names six court chambers: Salt, Parwah and Rinsing on the north; Wood[en], Gullah and Hewn Stone on the south (p. 220). Patrich puts the Wood[en] Chamber nearest the Water Gate, to its west (p. 222).
- **Evidence.** He takes the name from a chamber "built of wood", not from wood storage, because R. Eliezer b. Yaʿaqov "forgot what was its use" and Abba Saul called it the chamber of the High Priest (p. 222). The high priest's miqveh on the Water Gate (b. Yoma 31a) drew on the cistern within the gate (p. 222 and n. 54). "The Wooden Chamber that served the High Priest was attached to the Water Gate" (p. 225).

### Inference for the entries

- **Entry 3 (jer_temple).** The repo's candidate is Wilson's No. 8, the "Great Sea". This chapter gives two facts the repo lacked: its Gibson and Jacobson number (their no. 3, pp. 33–41) and its top level (734.9 m, from them). Patrich also links No. 8 to the low-level aqueduct, which he dates to John Hyrcanus or Alexander Jannaeus. That link is his inference from levels. It dates the supply, not the cutting, and gives no contact. F stays 1 (no cistern recorded in relation to a peristyle court) and D stays a site rating.
- **Entry 3, inner-court branch.** Milik's "peristyle" is the inner court. In Patrich's model cistern 5 lies under the inner Temple Court, but he names it the Gullah Cistern, which m. ʿErub. 10:14 lists apart from "the Great Cistern" (p. 210). The placement rests on his own reconstruction, and his early phase is offered "cautiously" without data. It is a new candidate cistern for this branch, not a recorded feature in a dated court.
- **Entry 8 (jer_temple, "wood stores").** Patrich's "Wooden Chamber" works against Milik's 'wood stores' sense, and places that chamber by the Water Gate above cistern 5's south-east arm. It is a textual reconstruction; it gives no dated chamber with a cistern opening in its floor. F stays 1.
- **Entries 9–10.** Nothing in pp. 205–225 places a cistern near the Golden Gate or under the east wall.
- **Next source.** Gibson and Jacobson 1996 is still the decisive source. Patrich gives its numbers for Wilson 5 (no. 28 per n. 16; caption p. 137, fig. 65), Wilson 8 (no. 3, pp. 33–41) and Wilson 1 (no. 31).

## Chapter 14. R. Reich and E. Shukron, "The Pool of Siloam in Jerusalem of the Late Second Temple Period and Its Surroundings" (pp. 241–254)

### The pool, its phases and its date (pp. 241–246)

- **Evidence.** A sewer dig at the south end of the Tyropoeon found steps; the excavation exposed the north-east side of a large stone-lined stepped pool, about 70 m south of the small pool at the end of the Siloam Tunnel (p. 241). The whole north-east side was exposed, "some 50 m"; the steps run in sets of five steps and a broad landing, three sets exposed; the bottom lies under the private orchard (p. 244). The pool was "probably trapezoidal", about 50–60 m × 50 m (p. 244).
- **Evidence — two phases (p. 244).** The earlier steps were rubble in gray cement, plastered with gray plaster, which "recalls features known from contemporary ritual baths"; later the steps were faced with ashlar blocks.
- **Evidence — date (p. 245).** A metal detector found coins "embedded in the gray cement of the earlier construction phase". "All coins were minted under Alexander Jannaeus". This is a terminus post quem; the authors put the first phase "sometime during the middle of the 1st century B.C.E." and the second "in the earlier part of the 1st century C.E."
- **Evidence — end (p. 246).** Collapse on the esplanade held 1st-century CE pottery and stone vessels and revolt coins (years Two, Three and some Four, 67–69 CE); the area, the pool included, "went out of use and fell into neglect with the sack of Jerusalem in 70 C.E."
- **Evidence — esplanade (p. 245).** North of the pool a paved esplanade ran across the valley, with a stylobate about 2.5 m wide, column drums and Doric capitals; one drum has the mason's mark ḥet, ṣade and three strokes.

### The feeding channel and its sluice (pp. 247, 250)

- **Evidence.** The channel at the foot of the City of David scarp, long called the "overflow channel" of the Siloam Tunnel, "was constructed at the same time as the pool, constituting in fact the channels feeding the Pool of Siloam" (p. 247).
- **Evidence.** "Close to the southern end of this channel, just before it crosses the southern damming wall of the Tyropoeon Valley, a short channel branches off and heads toward the stepped pool. This branch was found blocked." Clearing it exposed "two vertical and narrow grooves", which "would have accommodated a wooden plank that could have served as a sluice, controlling and regulating the water-flow from the main channel into the pool" (p. 247).
- **Evidence (p. 250).** A shallow channel cut into the bottom of the Byzantine pool runs south from the tunnel outlet; under the paving "the active channel branches off to the east", and it is this channel that feeds the stone-lined pool. A second channel along the cliff is a rainwater drain, cleared for about 40 m.
- **Evidence — the dam (pp. 247–248).** The damming wall is of small, roughly squared stones in yellowish clay mortar, "probably Iron Age II". Under the pool, abutting it, are 3–4 plastered steps descending south-west, earlier than the stepped pool; "Additional data for dating these steps more accurately is not yet available."

### Use (pp. 248–249)

- **Evidence.** The authors argue that the pool, or part of it, was used for ritual immersion: spring water from the Gihon, steps in the miqveh technique (rubble and gray cement), the five-steps-and-landing rhythm, and parallels with steps all round (south of the Triple Gate; Jewish Quarter Area Q). Holes in the landings "may have accommodated wooden poles to which mats were attached to close off an area for ritual bathing in the nude" (Fig. 3, p. 246; pp. 248–249).

### The stepped street (pp. 250–254)

- **Evidence.** A stepped street ascends north from the esplanade over a drain; the street went out of use in 70 CE. They date its paving "most probably" to Agrippa II in the 50s CE, from the same street along the Temple Mount, where the latest of about 15 coins under the paving is of Pontius Pilate (p. 253). Breaches in the paving above the drain are read with Josephus as refugees' entries (pp. 253–254).

### Inference for entry 49 (R06)

- **New for the repo:** a named conduit junction at the main (stone-lined) pool that is not L103: the short branch with sluice grooves near the dam (p. 247). The repo had no record of it.
- **It does not raise F.** The entry's landmark is a trough (השקת). The junction is a branch channel with a sluice, not a trough, and no plan, levels or contact are printed. Its phase rests on the authors' statement that the feeding channel was built with the pool, whose first phase is post-Jannaeus. F stays 1, D stays 2.
- **What it changes.** The R06 next test asks for "a named, surveyed main-basin conduit/trough junction tied to its ancient boundary/floor and phase". This junction meets "named" and "main-basin". It lacks the survey and the tie to the floor and phase. The primary report of the sluice (Reich and Shukron's excavation reports, or Szanton's files) is the source to ask for.
- **Bathing.** The authors' case for ritual immersion in the pool bears on Milik's "baths". It is an interpretation from step technique and the landing holes, not a dated bathing installation, and immersion is a different function from a bathhouse. It does not settle the installation word.

## Chapter 15. Z. Greenhut, "A Domestic Quarter from the Second Temple Period on the Lower Slopes of the Central Valley (Tyropoeon)" (from p. 257; references from p. 290)

- **Evidence — place (pp. 257–258).** IAA rescue excavations in 2005–2006 (and 2009) at the foot of a terraced, quarried cliff 13 m high on the lower west slope of the Central Valley, "facing and above the Siloam Pool to the east", about 650 m a.s.l.; about 175 m² plus a strip 28 m long (p. 258).
- **Evidence — the building (pp. 262–268).** Rock-hewn rooms on three storeys, terraced north to south, with beam sockets and vault grooves (Rooms 101, 102, 105, 107, 109, 121, 131). Room 109 is entered through three hewn steps, "the middle step cut by a hewn channel" (p. 265); its west wall has a tall plastered niche (L111) and four plaster layers with grooves read as Herodian panel stuccowork (pp. 265–267).
- **Evidence — cellar L117 (pp. 267–268).** "A hewn staircase, L-shaped and consisting of five steps, descends ... to the house's cellar (L117)", a rock-cut room 3.40 × 2.90 m with convex walls, the start of a hewn arch, and "typical Second Temple Period gray plaster with charcoal inclusions" (p. 267; Fig. 9).
- **Evidence — cellar L125 and miqveh L127 with otzar L130 (pp. 269–272).** Cellar L125 (4.15 × 1.70 m) has a conical depression in its floor, 0.50 m across and 0.40 m deep, fed by a short channel, read as a refuse or spill pit of a storeroom (p. 270). East of it, miqveh L127 (2.40 × 1.20 m), with four plastered steps and three layers of gray plaster; north of it the otzar L130 (2.60 × 0.80 × 0.90 m), with a reconstructed pipe through the partition (pp. 271–272; Fig. 12).
- **Evidence — date (pp. 268–269, 278).** "Nothing was found in situ"; the finds come from fill and "cannot be used to date the complex". Of 673 items, 70.13% are Late Hellenistic–Early Roman, 21.25% Iron II, 8.62% Late Roman–Byzantine (Table 1, p. 278). The building is assigned to the late Second Temple period by its plaster, stuccowork and marginally dressed stones (pp. 267, 269, 274–276).
- **Evidence — street (pp. 274–277).** A side road on a leveled fill east of the complex, drained by five plastered downspouts into channels L151 and L156; the latest finds in the fill are Early Roman (pp. 274–276).
- **Evidence — discussion (pp. 289–290).** Greenhut sees a wealthy household overlooking the pool and argues against a poor "Lower City" on this slope. He notes the Weill cistern near the Theodotus synagogue, north-east of the pool (Weill 1920: 174–90; p. 289).

### Figures (viewed)

- **Fig. 1 (p. 258), location plan, north at top, scale 100 m.** The excavation lies on the west side of the valley mouth, between Kenyon's Areas O and F, west of the Siloam Pool excavation.
- **Fig. 4 (p. 260), general plan.** A narrow strip trending NNW–SSE along the cliff; rooms L101–L109 at the south, cellar L125 and miqveh L127/otzar L130 in the middle, the road and W9 to the north. The labels are barely legible at book resolution.

- **Fig. 12 (p. 271).** The plastered miqveh with its steps, looking north, with the otzar behind.

### Inference for entry 6 (jer_tyropoeon)

- The Tyropoeon slope has a recorded rock-cut house with a stair down to a cellar, a pit fed by a channel in a cellar floor, and a stepped miqveh. None of these is a cistern below steps: the L117 stair leads into a cellar, the L125 pit is 0.4 m deep, and the miqveh's steps lead into the pool. The house is dated by style, not by sealed deposits. This adds district-level features of the kind; F stays 1 and D stays 2. The entry's weakest part is still R (המלה against המלח).
