# Allegro 1959: Cave 3, the cutting, and the Qumran waterworks plates

**Labels:** **Evidence** = Allegro states it (his views of 1959 are his interpretation). **Inference** = drawn here. **BK** = none used.

## Cave 3 and the find (plates 70–76)

- **Image (pls. 71–73).** Pl. 71: a woman points at the line of the fallen roof above a dark hollow at the foot of a slope of broken rock; the plain and the Dead Sea are in the left background. Pl. 72: a person bends under the overhang at the back of the cave. Pl. 73: the two rolls lie side by side on the cave floor beside a scale bar of five alternating blocks; the scale's units are not stated.

- **Evidence.** After the third Bedouin find, the archaeologists examined "some 230 caves" near Cave 1 (pl. 70). Cave 3 lay "toward the northern limit of the area covered by the search"; its roof had collapsed in antiquity and hidden the entrance (pl. 71). Broken scroll jars and fragments lay in the dust; the copper scroll lay "hard up against an inner wall" (pl. 72).
- **Evidence.** Three strips of beaten copper, about eight feet long in all, riveted in two places; the rivets of the second join had given way (pl. 74). The metal was fully oxidized and too brittle to unroll (pl. 75).

## The cutting at Manchester (plates 77–85)

- **Evidence.** A machine was built at the College of Technology, Manchester (pl. 77). A spindle ran through each roll, which was blocked against wooden discs with plaster of Paris and set in a cradle on a trolley under a 1¾-inch circular saw on a spring-loaded arm, for a controlled depth of cut (pls. 78–79). An aircraft adhesive was painted on and baked (pl. 79).
- **Evidence.** Before each cut the saw line was chosen to avoid as many visible letters as possible. "At one point it was found necessary to change the direction of the cut halfway along its length" (pl. 80). Plaster was ground away with a dental drill (pl. 81).
- **Evidence.** The engraving was sometimes deep, sometimes "light and hurried". Allegro estimates the scribe's tool edge at about ⅛ inch (pl. 84). Plate 85 shows the first line of rivets on an inner segment.
- **Image (pl. 80).** The photograph shows the operator guiding the saw along the top of a plaster-blocked roll in the cradle. It does not show which cut is being made or where a change of direction falls.
- **Inference (R11).** Plate 80 states that one cut changed direction partway. It does not say which cut. The project's open question about a proposed rotation at cut 10 needs the cut number and the original photographs; this caption is a lead, not a crosswalk.

## Allegro's reading of the scroll (plates 86–93; chapter 9)

- **Evidence.** "Written in a colloquial Hebrew of the first century a.d." (pl. 86); twelve columns; deposits "mainly around Qumran and in the vicinity of Jerusalem" (pl. 87). He links the bullion and vessels to the Second Temple (pl. 88; ch. 9) and calls the medicinal essences "peculiarly Essene" (ch. 9).
- **Evidence (ch. 9).** He says letters were substituted and words run together, "clearly with the intention of making the reading more difficult". This is his interpretation.
- **Evidence.** The Vale of Achor "has been recently identified as the modern Buqei‘a" (pl. 90). He asks whether the Essenes identified the Kidron monument with Absalom's (pl. 91), and places one deposit in a pit or well on Gerizim, "of which a number are to be seen on the top" (pls. 92–93).
- **Evidence (ch. 8).** He infers that a Qumran metalsmith prepared and riveted the copper strips.

## Qumran waterworks plates (plates 140–159)

- **Evidence.** Rainwater "was first collected in a great natural basin halfway down the cliff face at the head of the great gorge" (pl. 143). From there a conduit ran around the cliffs and "in one place through" them (pl. 144); a rock-cut tunnel (pl. 145) opens into a carved, cemented conduit (pl. 146); collapsed stretches leave cement and plaster on the cliff face (pl. 147); an open aqueduct crosses the plateau (pl. 148); a sluice of rough stones breaks the flow at the main settling tank (pl. 149, plan pl. 101 location 1).
- **Evidence.** Plates 154–155: the deep round cistern, which Allegro dates to the 8th–7th centuries BCE, fed by "a great duct near the rim". Plate 156: an overflow drain. Plate 157: plaster partitions on cistern steps. Plate 158: steps cracked by the earthquake of 31 BCE (near pl. 101 location 14). Plate 159: a possible bathhouse west of that cistern.
- **What the images show (observation at book resolution; no scale in any frame):**
  - *Pl. 143, basin.* A rocky hollow in the gorge floor, with a lighter, flatter floor in the middle distance, a tuft of vegetation and a dark recess low on the right-hand wall. No masonry, plaster, dam wall or cut channel can be seen at this resolution. The frame is small (336 × 366 px).
  - *Pl. 144, conduit and tunnel mouth.* A cliff spur with a dark, roughly rectangular opening at one bedding level. A ledge at the same level runs to the left of the opening. The plain shows far below on the right. This is the conduit entering the rock, as the caption says.
  - *Pl. 145, tunnel interior.* A narrow, tall, rock-cut passage. Its walls are rough and its floor is level. A bright, roughly rectangular opening is cut through the left-hand wall, partway along. The caption does not mention it.
  - *Pl. 146, tunnel exit.* A large, squarish rock-cut opening in fractured limestone, with a level bed leading out of it.
  - *Pl. 147, conduit traces.* A woman points at a lighter band on the cliff face, at shoulder height, beside a broken ledge.
  - *Pl. 148, open aqueduct.* A line of set stones runs across the stony plateau towards the cliff base.
  - *Pl. 149, sluice.* A line of rough stones across a shallow built channel at the edge of the excavated area, with a man standing beside it.
- **Inference (R01, entries 21–22).** These are 1950s photographs of the gorge works, taken before later clearance and damage. Allegro does not tie them to the scroll here. Plate 145's side opening may be one of the openings in the long tunnel that the project already records (Schulz's opening A and fissure B). Which one it is cannot be decided from this frame; it needs Schulz's plates or a located photograph. The book-resolution plates show no dam, no stone north of the intake and no feature at the basin that the scroll's wording requires. Better copies (the 1959 print, or Allegro's negatives in the Manchester archive) would be needed for detail.

## The L120 coins (plates 101, 173–174)

- **Evidence.** The legend of the model (pl. 101) names "the room in which the three juglets of silver coins were discovered". Plate 173 says a juglet packed with silver coins lay under the plaster floor inside a doorway in the north-west, "Nearby were two other deposits"; plate 174, "Well over five hundred coins".
- **Image (pl. 173).** A worker crouches over a small deposit on a floor, close to a wall corner and an opening in the wall. This fits the caption's "inside the doorway".
- **Image (pl. 101).** The numbered model has a north arrow. Location 1 (aqueduct outlet into the settling basin) and 2 ("probable baptistry") are at the north-west corner; 14 (bathhouse) is beside a cistern (17) in the east. Inference: these positions agree with L132/L138 in the north-west and the cracked cistern in the east.
- **Inference.** Allegro's own text is the source of the "three juglets" description. It does not give 558. Callegher 2023 p. 59 describes two small pots and a different narrow-necked vessel, with 561 coins.
