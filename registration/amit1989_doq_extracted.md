## 1. Introduction (מבוא), p. 223

1. **Evidence (p. 223).** The Hasmonean fortress Doq, called that in 1 Maccabees and "Dagon" in Josephus, has been identified by scholars with the remains on the summit of Ras Qaruntul (ראס קרנטל) above Jericho, at the top of the cliffs.
2. **Evidence (p. 223, Fig. 1, n. 1).** At the site there are foundations of buildings, walls (חומות), retaining walls (קירות תמך), a defensive moat (חפיר הגנה) "and more". No date or phasing is given here for these summit remains beyond the identification with the Hasmonean fortress. For sources, research history and description of the remains, n. 1 refers to Meshel 1978.
3. **Evidence (p. 223, n. 2).** Earlier visitors did not notice any cisterns. These were the PEF surveyors (1873), then Dalman (1914), Alt (1926), Bramki (early 1930s; publ. 1944), and after the Six-Day War Ze'ev Meshel, Yoram Tsafrir and others. None of them found remains of the fortress's water supply.
4. **Evidence (Fig. 2, p. 224).** The map marks a moat (חפיר) running across the west side of the summit hill, and "church foundations" (יסודות כנסיה) as a rectangular enclosure on the summit. "Deir Qaruntul" (the monastery) is marked on the cliff below to the south-east.
5. **Inference.** The rectangular walled structure on the summit in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 is the modern church that the monks started (see §4.3 below: "near the church whose building began"). It is not presented as a Hasmonean building. The chapter offers no archaeological date for the summit fortifications themselves.

## 2. Discovery of the system (גילוי המפעל), pp. 223-224

1. **Evidence (p. 223).** The author argues that a fortress of this kind, far above and far from any perennial water source, could not have been without a water system.
2. **Evidence (p. 223, n. 3).** In 1972 (5732) Meshel (Tel Aviv University) was running a comprehensive survey of the Jericho fortresses with the author's participation, and they decided to re-examine the fortress's surroundings. They paid special attention to the water problem on a survey trip in summer 1972 (n. 3 gives the date as 30.8.1972).
3. **Evidence (p. 223).** A topographic analysis pointed them to the northern spur and to the high, steep cliffs at the foot of the fortress on its east. On the descent of the northern spur they found a rock-cut stretch of aqueduct coming from the west. It led them to **nine cisterns** cut into the cliff slope, facing east toward the Jericho plain.
4. **Evidence (p. 224, n. 4).** A further survey trip located the aqueduct's starting point and traced its whole length. A preliminary report appeared in 1973 (n. 4).

## 3. The aqueduct (אמת המים), pp. 224-225

1. **Evidence (p. 224, Fig. 2 point א).** The aqueduct starts north-west of the fortress, at grid ref. 1906 1426, in a smallish wadi that runs from west to east toward the Jericho plain, north of the fortress ridge.
2. **Evidence (p. 224).** The intake: the aqueduct begins at a rock step in the wadi bed, a small waterfall about 2 m high. On top of the step survive two lines of small stones set in plaster, about 1.2 m apart. The author reads these as the remains of a built chute or channel (מיגלש) that joined the natural rock channel to the head of the aqueduct.
3. **Evidence (pp. 224-225).** There is **no diversion dam**. The wadi above the intake is short (under 1 km), so the builders did not fear the force of floods. They collected **all** the flood water in the bed on rainy days. This contrasts with aqueducts that "capture" larger wadis, Hyrcania for example. There, a low dam diverts only a small part of the flood, because the full volume would destroy the dam and silt up the aqueduct. The wadi bed above the intake is natural bedrock, almost clean of soil and stones for hundreds of metres.
4. **Evidence (p. 225, Figs. 2-3).** Course: south-east at first (Fig. 3). It picks up two more channels (point ב), then turns north and east, wraps around the northern flank of the fortress hill, and finally turns south onto the eastern flank (point ג) toward the cisterns.
5. **Evidence (p. 225).** **Two pools** sit just before the turn south, next to the aqueduct but below its level, built partly by rock-cutting and partly by masonry. In the western pool, three plastered walls partly survive, possibly with remains of a few steps; it measured about 1.5 × 1.5 m. Little of the second pool survives: only its rock-cut eastern wall, with some of its plaster.
6. **Evidence (p. 225, n. 5).** On their function the author writes that the pools were evidently for the day-to-day use of the people living in the buildings nearby and of the **guard soldiers (חיילי מישמר) stationed in this sector**. n. 5 identifies those buildings: the Netzer excavation of December 1982 uncovered a complex of rooms and a corridor on the descent of the north-eastern spur, near these pools, and these remains are **attributed to the Herodian period**. n. 5 also reports that Meshel proposes reading the pools as a **miqveh**, because of their shape and because they could collect flood water, which counts as "undrawn" water.
7. **Evidence (p. 225).** From the intake to the opening of cistern 2, where it arrives, the aqueduct is exactly 0.5 km long. Internal width varies from 60 to 80 cm. It is built on rock terraces; some stretches are cut entirely in the rock (Fig. 4, section I), others are partly cut and partly built (Fig. 5, section II).
8. **Evidence (p. 225, n. 6). Dating by plaster.** The aqueduct is plastered along its whole length with white plaster containing small stone grits and pebbles. The author states that this plaster is **characteristic of the Hasmonean period**, citing Porat (this volume), whose typology classes it as white single-layer plaster (type printed as "1I").
9. **Evidence (p. 225).** In places there are repairs to the plaster, but in the author's view they are **repairs from the same period**, and they should not be taken as evidence that the aqueduct was used in a later period.
10. **Evidence (p. 226, Fig. 6).** The aqueduct floor was set at the level of cistern 2's ceiling and the top of its opening. It ran past cistern 1, which lies lower, and reached cistern 2. At that level it continued south past the next cistern. Cistern 2 was filled by a **chute** (מיגלש) that branches off where the stretch between cisterns 2 and 3 begins and winds down to the edge of the cistern's opening. This is the only well-preserved chute.
11. **Evidence (p. 226).** From it the author reconstructs the filling method for the whole row. The main aqueduct ran straight along the row above the openings, and a filling chute branched off to each opening. In his view this arrangement regulated the distribution between cisterns and avoided overflow. It also helped deal with the silt and refuse carried by flood water: the water settled in a **small settling pool** built at the lip of each cistern. One such pool survives at the opening of cistern 9.
12. **Evidence (p. 226).** Cistern 1, which lies below the aqueduct, was filled by a hanging chute that came down to its opening; scant remains of it survive south of the opening. The aqueduct can be traced as far as the point above cistern 3. Beyond that only very scant remains survive, destroyed because the cliffs it was built on are so steep.
13. **Inference.** The chapter describes no siphon, bridge or dam. The only intake structure is the small plaster-set channel on the rock step.

## 4. The cisterns (בורות המים), pp. 226-227

1. **Evidence (p. 226, Fig. 7, n. 7).** The cisterns are rectangular or trapezoidal in plan. They recall the cisterns of Masada and other fortresses in form, though not in size: a Masada cistern holds 2,000-4,000 m³ (n. 7). They are cut into the hard Turonian limestone. Natural joints made the cutting difficult, so the walls are not smooth and have bulges and recesses.
2. **Evidence (p. 227).** Unlike Masada, there are no steps from the openings down to the floors. People got in for cleaning and repairs with a portable ladder or ropes.
3. **Evidence (p. 227).** Sizes in the text: length 7-11 m, width 3-5 m, depth 5-7 m. Total capacity of the nine cisterns is about 2,090 m³. Most lie at one level; cistern 2 is cut a little higher. The row runs the whole length of the hill's eastern flank. Spacing is mostly about 20 m, but cisterns 2-3 are about 70 m apart and cisterns 3-4 about 35 m. Most openings are in the upper middle of the eastern face, so the lintel is roughly at ceiling height; cisterns 6 and 9 are exceptions. Cistern 4 is the odd one out: its whole opening is cut higher, with its sill at ceiling level. The openings are well hidden among the rock masses and can be seen only from very close. Contrast Alexandrion and Cypros, where the openings cut in soft chalk are visible from far away.

5. **Evidence (p. 227).** Construction: the openings were first cut about twice their final height, for easy access and for removing spoil. After cutting, the lower half of each opening was closed with a built wall and plastered together with the rock walls. This let the cisterns be filled almost to the ceiling. Dark water-line bands on the plaster show a maximum water level 1-1.5 m below the ceiling.
6. **Evidence (p. 227).** The walls carry a thick, largely preserved plaster coat; the ceilings are unplastered. The plaster has three layers, sometimes four. The innermost fills joints and evens the surface. The second is a bonding layer, with its face scored into a net of slightly sunk squares so the next coat would grip. The outer layer is fine white plaster with small stone particles.
7. **Evidence (Fig. 2).** Fig. 2 numbers ten cisterns. Nos. 1-9 form the row along the eastern cliff from north (1) to south (9), ending near Deir Qaruntul. No. 10 is nearer the summit, between the summit and cistern 1.
8. **Inference.** The cisterns are not dated independently (no finds are reported from them). They are dated only as part of the system, which the author ties to the founding of the fortress (§5.1).

## 5. Date, later use and the Herodian question (p. 227, nn. 5, 8)

1. **Evidence (p. 227).** Doq appears in the sources as a fortress from the start of the Hasmonean period. For the rest of the Second Temple period there are **no historical sources** on the site. However, **test excavations (חפירות בדיקה) uncovered remains of the Herodian period** (n. 8, which points back to n. 5: the Netzer excavation of Dec. 1982, a complex of rooms and a corridor on the north-eastern spur near the pools).
2. **Evidence (p. 227).** The author's dating: the water system was "certainly" set up when the fortress was founded, at the beginning of the Hasmonean period. Unlike the water systems of other fortresses (Hyrcania, Cypros and others), **no additional Herodian phase** can be seen at Doq. He concludes it is reasonable to assume the Hasmonean system simply **remained in use**.
3. **Evidence (p. 227).** Byzantine reuse: monks who founded a laura here called "Douka" (after the old fortress) found the system destroyed. They restored only the stretch they needed and added a short drain winding down the slope. They also apparently cut **cistern 10**, near the summit. It is bell-shaped, entirely unlike the others; a similar cistern is in the Byzantine chapel complex on the lower terrace at Cypros.
4. **Evidence (p. 227).** Modern reuse: the monks who settled in the rebuilt monastery from 1874, and Bedouin herders, used the restored stretch. The monks also built a cistern on the summit itself, near the church whose construction had begun. Other traces of the monks are a Greek inscription of 1898 in red paint on the wall of cistern 1, and inscriptions, crosses and other graffiti at the opening of cistern 6.
5. **Evidence (p. 228, n. 2).** Meinardus (1969) records nine large cisterns halfway along the path from the monastery to the summit, about 70 m above the monastery, opening toward the Jericho plain. He suggested they were "built in the Herodian period to irrigate the agricultural crops at the foot of the mountain". Amit notes that Meinardus does not link the cisterns to the fortress and makes gross errors, but grants him priority. Amit learned of the note only after his own discovery.
6. **Inference.** The chapter contains **no statement of destruction or abandonment** in the 1st century BCE or 1st century CE. The only "destruction" mentioned is that the Byzantine monks found the system destroyed: the date is not specified, and the author attributes the loss of the aqueduct's southern stretch to the steep cliffs.

## 6. Guard post, watch-tower or "mishmar"

1. **Evidence (p. 225).** The pools near the aqueduct's turn south, on the north-east spur, served the residents of nearby buildings and the "guard soldiers stationed in this sector" (חיילי מישמר). The nearby buildings are the rooms and corridor excavated in 1982 and attributed to the Herodian period (n. 5).
2. **Evidence (Fig. 2, p. 224).** The legend includes a "tower" (מגדל) symbol, an open square. Two are marked. One is on the high ground at the far west/south-west of the map (the 100+ m contour), at the end of a long double-line feature. The other is north of the fortress hill, near the 60 m contour. The legend calls the double line "דייק" (dike/dyke). It runs about 500-600 m from the western tower toward the north-west. The text does not discuss either tower or the dike, and does not date them.
3. **Inference.** The "guard soldiers" phrase is the author's functional interpretation, not a find (no inscription or other find is cited). Given n. 5, it most naturally describes a Herodian-period outpost on the north-east spur that guarded the aqueduct sector. The two "towers" on the map are possible outlying watch-towers, but the chapter gives no description, date or interpretation of them. Their source should be sought in Meshel 1978 (n. 1) or the 1982 excavation.

4. **D. Amit**, "The water system of the Doq (Dagon) fortress" (Hebrew), in **Z. Ilan** (ed.), *Judean Desert and Dead Sea: Collected papers for the 19th conference of the Society for the Protection of Nature*, Tel Aviv 1973 (5733), pp. 359-362. This is the preliminary report.
