## (a) Greek letters, abbreviations, numerals, outside writing, and marks

### Alphabetic numerals in P.Se’elim 4

**Evidence:** Cotton reproduces Greek letter numerals as ages in **P.Se’elim 4, fragment a, column ii**, paired with names in column i. The heading/word accompanying the numbers is ἐτῶν, with restorations where damaged. Her bilingual table explicitly gives the following equivalents. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 181; repeated in *Roman Census*, p. 376.)

| Line | Brief verbatim numeral | Age in Cotton's translation | Qualification |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | κε | 25 | Age word restored |
| 2 | ιγ | 13 | Age word partly restored |
| 3 | ιθ | 19 | Age word partly restored |
| 4 | μα | 41 | Age word partly restored |
| 8 | ξα | 61 | Age word preserved |
| 9 | ξζ | 67 | Age word preserved |
| 12 | κβ | 22 | Age word restored |
| 13 | λς | 36 | Age word restored |
| 14 | β | [?]2 | Cotton's translation is uncertain; the census paper prints the beta with an underdot |

**Inference:** These are compact Greek letter groups functioning as numbers. Their identification is supported directly by Cotton's translations and the age column. They are not examples of the seven Copper Scroll groups serving as unexplained marks after inventory entries. Their placement is a parallel column alongside persons' names, and their function is expressly identified. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, pp. 181–182; *Roman Census*, pp. 375–377.)

### A possible subscriber-title abbreviation in P.Yadin 18

**Evidence:** In **P.Yadin 18, line 76**, Cotton quotes **“[…]τιτος χει̣ρ”**. She proposes, tentatively, that the last group abbreviates **χειροχρήστης**, the person who writes a subscription for someone unable to write it. She interprets a raised horizontal line as an abbreviation indicator. She disagrees with Lewis and notes his acknowledgment that the line's ending does not clearly read **μάρ(τυς)**, the word for witness. (*Subscriptions*, p. 125 n. 48.)

**Evidence:** Cotton says the relevant feature is absent from Plate 19 of the edition she discusses; her proposal relied on a photograph of the papyrus. The requested Cotton reader section contains no reproduction of it. (*Subscriptions*, p. 125 n. 48.)

**Inference:** This is the closest Greek abbreviation example in the requested passages to a short appended letter group. It belongs to subscription/signature material and has a proposed occupational/legal expansion. Cotton presents neither its expansion nor the reading as established. It is not a standalone group after an inventory entry.

### Signature marks and abbreviated forms in comparative documents

**Evidence:** Cotton discusses crosses made by people unable to write, followed by subscriptions written on their behalf. Her concrete Greek example is **P.Mich. XI 607 (569 CE)**: the party makes three crosses by his own hand, and another person writes for him. The quotation includes **Ἀντι(νόου)**, **ἀξιωθ(εὶς)**, **ὑπ(ὲρ)**, and **γράμμ(ατα)**. These are word abbreviations printed with editorial expansions. This is an Egyptian Christian parallel, not a Judaean Desert document. (*Subscriptions*, p. 120 n. 25.)

**Evidence:** In **Tjäder no. 20 (590–602? CE), Ravenna**, Sisivera makes a cross; the scribe writes her subscription, and a witness acting as chirocrista authenticates her mark. The Latin examples include **Rav(ennae)**, **s(an)c(t)ae**, **h(onestae)**, **f(eminae)**, **s(upra)**, **s(crip)tae**, **v(ir)**, **d(evotus)**, **scolar(is)**, **fact(a)e**, and **Rav(ennatis)**. Cotton also reports that one witness's subscription, lines 87–89, uses Greek letters. (*Subscriptions*, p. 121.)

**Evidence:** Comparable authentication subscriptions occur in **Tjäder no. 16 (c. 600 CE), lines 33–34**, and **no. 27 (middle of sixth century CE?), line 1**. Cotton distinguishes authentication of an illiterate person's cross from merely writing a document or witnessing it. Irene's novella of **797–802 CE** also requires an illiterate principal or witness to make a cross, with another person writing the remainder. (*Subscriptions*, pp. 120–122.)

**Inference:** These parallels establish the use of nonalphabetic signature marks and abbreviated words in subscription formulae. They do not establish such a practice for the Copper Scroll or identify its Greek groups. The geographic and chronological distance of the comparative examples must remain explicit.

### Writing on the verso, signatures, and authentication

- **Evidence:** A double deed contains an inner/upper and an outer/lower version of its text. The inner version is legally binding and is rolled and tied; signatures occur against the stitching on the verso. Cotton cautions against treating the survival of this format as proof of a Roman requirement. (*Diplomatics*, pp. 150–152.)
- **Evidence:** Verso signatures stand near the tie, at right angles to the outer recto text. The first signature starts behind the right recto margin in a Semitic deed and behind the left recto margin in a Greek deed. In Greek deeds, Greek signatures run away from the stitching and Semitic signatures toward it; in Semitic deeds, the direction is reversed. This identifies a physical arrangement of signatures, not an archive-code system. (*Diplomatics*, p. 152.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton's examples include **P.Yadin 11**, seven Greek signatures on a Greek deed; **P.Yadin 12**, four Nabataean signatures and one Greek; and **P.Yadin 20**, six Semitic signatures and one Greek. (*Diplomatics*, p. 152 n. 18.)
- **Evidence:** In **P.Mur 18**, the principal's and substitute writer's subscription occupies two successive lines on the verso. Yardeni's correction identifies Zecharia son of Yochanan in the first line and Yehosef, who writes for him, in the second. Cotton says the handwriting and verso signatures suggest an outer version of a double deed, but warns that reasoning solely from verso signatures would be circular. (*Diplomatics*, p. 153 n. 20; p. 155 and n. 26.)
- **Evidence:** The two Aramaic words **רישה כתבה** at the end of **P.Ḥever 60**, a tax receipt of **125 CE**, authenticate the receipt through Reisha, the person who issued it. Cotton interprets the verb factitively: he had it written. She rejects interpreting him as the Greek document's scribe. (*Diplomatics*, pp. 155–156.)
- **Evidence:** Explicit Greek scribal acknowledgments occur in **P.Yadin 15**, **ὁ δὲ γράψας τοῦτο Θεένας Σίμωνος**; **P.Yadin 17**, **Θεένας Σίμωνος λιβλάριος ἔγραψα**; and **P.Yadin 20**, **ἐγράφη διὰ [Γερ]μανοῦ λι̣βλα̣ρ̣ίο[υ]**. They identify Theenas or Germanus as the writer, using full verbal acknowledgments rather than isolated initials or monograms. (*Diplomatics*, p. 155.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton reports no sealings attached to the Cave of Letters deeds, including intact/rolled examples. She records one exception from Murabbaʿat, a sealing associated with **P.Mur 29**, a First Revolt sale deed. The supporting reference is **DJD II, pp. 140 ff., plate XLIc**, not a reproduced plate in this Cotton section. Two other seal impressions cited in n. 31 were found with letters. (*Diplomatics*, p. 157 nn. 31–32.)
- **Evidence:** The sealing language in **P.Yadin 25**, paralleled in **P.Dura 26 (227 CE)**, is treated as a inherited formula: the witness names/signatures rather than actual attached seals establish their presence. (*Diplomatics*, pp. 157–158.)

**Inference:** Outside writing in these passages must be distinguished by function. An outer text is a second version of a deed; a verso signature validates or witnesses it; the end of a receipt can carry an authentication. None is identified here as a Greek-letter docket, inventory-entry label, registry number, archive siglum, or monogram.

## (b) Layout of lists, registers, and amounts

### P.Se’elim 4

- **Evidence:** Six fragments remain from a large document. There were at least four columns, arranged in two pairs: names on the left, corresponding ages on the right. Fragment a preserves three columns; the age column belonging to the third name column is lost. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 181; *Roman Census*, p. 375.)
- **Evidence:** In column iii, a full name and patronymic precedes additional names indented by slightly more than **1 cm**. Cotton interprets this as a head of household followed by other male members, probably sons. Rows align people with their ages in the paired column. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 182; *Roman Census*, pp. 376–377.)
- **Evidence:** The census paper counts at least **49 persons** across the surviving fragments: at least fourteen in columns i–ii, thirteen in column iii, and twenty-one in fragments b–f. This is Cotton's reconstructed minimum, not a surviving ancient total line. (*Roman Census*, p. 376.)
- **Inference — arithmetic check:** The three component minima printed in that paragraph sum to **48**, while Cotton's stated overall minimum is 49. Both are retained here; the reader passage gives no explanation for the difference.
- **Evidence:** Cotton interprets the register as abstracted from census declarations and probably intended for male-only liturgical obligations, such as manual service. She rejects the identification as Bar Kokhba's military roster, partly because the ages include thirteen and sixty-seven. Its precise date and place are unknown. Greek language, fluent writing, and format support its official character. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, pp. 181–182; *Roman Census*, pp. 375–377.)
- **Evidence:** If refugees deposited it during the second revolt, Cotton places its compilation shortly before that revolt because such registers needed updating; the census paper also offers the first half of the second century CE as the probable broader date. These are conditional arguments, not a preserved date formula. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 182; *Roman Census*, pp. 375–377.)
- **Inference:** The demonstrated hierarchy is spatial: an unindented household heading and indented subordinate names. No ruled separators, punctuation system, subtotals, or grand-total convention is described for this register in the passages examined.

### Land declarations and tax accounts

**Evidence:** In **P.Yadin 16** and **P.Ḥever 62**, submitted at the Arabian census of **127 CE**, declarants identify themselves and their domicile, then enumerate land by plot name, size, tax, and two abutters. Cotton's printed comparison table at p. 370 juxtaposes these documents with the Roman census form; it is a modern scholarly table, not evidence that the papyri themselves had that table layout. (*Roman Census*, pp. 369–370.)

**Evidence:** The quoted plot descriptions write amounts in Greek words. For **P.Yadin 16, lines 19–21**, the components are fifteen sata of Syrian/mixed dates, ten sata of pressed dates, and crown tax of one melaina plus thirty lepta. The brief amount example **σάτα δεκαπέντε** means fifteen sata. For **P.Ḥever 62, fragment a, lines 16–17**, the declared half share measures the sowing area of one saton and three kaboi and pays one melaina plus forty-five lepta: **μέλαν ἓν λεπτὰ τεσ̣σ̣α̣ράκοντα̣ πέντε**. (*Roman Census*, pp. 370, 373.)

**Evidence:** Cotton treats the local units and the Nabataean monetary/tax vocabulary as continuity within a Roman declaration form. She distinguishes tax stated for each plot from a valuation of the plot. The crown-tax description does not mean aurum coronarium, which was not an annual tax; P.Ḥever 62 generally lacks that designation except in fragments a–m, lines 17–18. (*Roman Census*, pp. 373–374 and p. 373 n. 50.)

**Evidence:** **P.Mur 89–107**, parchment fragments, are most plausibly administrative lists of taxes received in money and in kind; they are in Greek. Cotton does not describe their entry separators, number notation, subtotal system, or total lines here. (*Roman Census*, p. 375 and n. 60.)

**Inference:** The land-declaration examples support recurrent per-plot descriptions and mixed cash/in-kind liabilities. They do not demonstrate a treasure inventory or a letter-group coding scheme. No denarius abbreviation is reproduced in these particular amount examples.

- **Evidence:** Hebrew was used for legal/administrative writing and official letters during the revolts alongside Aramaic; Aramaic remained the usual legal language. Cotton explains the use of Hebrew through the ideology of an independent Jewish polity. (*Languages*, pp. 130, 136–137; *Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 176.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton finds Yadin's proposed official decree reviving Hebrew attractive, but explicitly rejects his earlier-Aramaic/later-Hebrew chronological division. The suggested decree is an explanation, not a decree reproduced in the source. (*Languages*, p. 130 n. 16.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton's strongest physical example is **DJD XXVII no. 8**: the hidden legally binding text uses ordinary legal Aramaic; the visible outer Hebrew text signals the independent state's ideology. She similarly connects archaizing paleo-Hebrew coin legends in both revolts with national symbolism. (*Languages*, pp. 136–137.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton's generalization about scarce Hebrew documentation before the First Revolt and between the revolts expressly carries the **4Q348 exception**. Her treatment also distinguishes secular documentary writing from the continuing use of Hebrew for worship and religious literature. (*Languages*, pp. 129–130, 136 n. 39; *Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 176 n. 15.)
- **Evidence:** Greek documents at Masada are mostly attributed to its Jewish defenders, **66–73/74 CE**. Cotton treats these as evidence that Greek writing has no necessary ideological implication; their nonlegal character excludes them from her subsequent legal-document discussion. (*Languages*, pp. 141–142.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton associates Greek legal writing with communication under Roman rule, but refuses a simple inference from document language to the substantive law applied. Arabia's documentary shift accompanies its annexation in **106 CE**; Judaea's first safely dated Greek legal documents appear considerably later. (*Diplomatics*, pp. 149–150.)

**Inference for R12:** Documentary Hebrew can support comparison with revolt-era administrative/legal practice, but cannot date the Copper Scroll by itself. The exception, coexistence of languages, continued religious Hebrew, and conditional ideological explanation rule out treating Hebrew as an exclusive chronological marker. The selected passages supply no linguistic comparison between the Copper Scroll's Hebrew and Mishnaic Hebrew.

## (d) Mur 29 and 30: dating formulae and the First Revolt

| Label | Document | Short verbatim date elements from Cotton | Formula and location | Dating implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence | P.Mur 29, outer text lines 9–10 | **“fourteenth Ellul”**, **“Year Two”** | Redemption of Israel, **ל[גא]לת ישׂ[רא]ל**; concluded **בירשׁלים**, in Jerusalem. | Evidence | P.Mur 30, outer text lines 8–9 | **“twenty first Tishrei”**, **“Year Four”** | Redemption of Israel, **לגאולת ישׂראל**; in Jerusalem | Originally read as September/October 135 if assigned to Bar Kokhba; Cotton moves it to the First Revolt |

**Evidence:** The formulae and original second-revolt conversions above are at *Languages*, p. 131. The reassignment is proposed at pp. 132–134 and stated more firmly at *Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 176. The latter cites two independent studies: Eshel, Broshi, and Jull (1998) and Cotton's 1999 languages paper (p. 176 n. 14).

**Inference — calendar conversion:** On the ordinary inclusive reckoning beginning with 66 CE, revolt year 2 corresponds to **67 CE**, and year 4 to **69 CE**. These conversions express the implication of Cotton's First Revolt assignment; the passages read do not print exact Julian equivalents for either contract. No exact Julian day has been calculated here.

- **Evidence:** Both are double sale deeds with a public declaration before four named signatories; some surviving verso signatures differ from those four. Cotton does not identify them as a court merely because they witnessed a public transaction. (*Languages*, pp. 130–131.)
- **Evidence:** Cotton regards Jerusalem as the actual place of contracting. Treating it merely as propaganda would leave the contracts without their normal place statement. Their original attribution to Bar Kokhba would imply his authority in Jerusalem as late as autumn 135, an implication Cotton finds historically troubling. (*Languages*, pp. 131–132.)
- **Evidence:** Mur 29 and 30 omit the leader's name, whereas many second-revolt formulae name Shimʿon son of Kosibah. Cotton expressly says omission is not decisive: the securely second-revolt no. 8a also omits it. (*Languages*, p. 133.)
- **Evidence:** Other support includes an early-compatible cursive hand, first-revolt numismatic evidence allowing a redemption-of-Israel formula, and Murabbaʿat's occupation and documentary history across multiple periods. Carbon-14 evidence is discussed as corroboration, with Cotton warning that its uncertainty cannot independently resolve such a short chronological interval. (*Languages*, pp. 133–134, p. 132 n. 22, p. 134 n. 31.)
- **Evidence:** The 2003 paper uses the reassignment to argue that Hebrew legal contracts were written in the First Revolt as well as the Second, with a comparable ideological explanation. (*Bar Kokhba Revolt*, p. 176.)
