## Changes and retained disagreements

**3Q entrance: additional direct figure inspection, shared excavation lineage.** Humbert p. 45, figs. 2.9a–b / viewer 62, reproduces a photograph captioned as modern spoil after clearance of 3Q's entrance and a photograph of workers removing large blocking rocks. These images document the modern work and changed approach; they supply no surveyed ancient entrance datum or original Copper Scroll findspot plan. Individual archive IDs, photographers and capture dates are not given in those captions. The photographs should remain linked to the 1952 excavation lineage, rather than counted as an independent later observation of an undisturbed deposit.

At p. 46 / viewer 63 Humbert cites DJD III pp. 7–8 for the low ceiling, pottery debris and northern chamber findspot. Those descriptions are **already captured** in the project's DJD III extraction. His proposed movement of jars by modern Bedouin, inferred from the lack of soil between fragments, is an interpretation; it does not establish the time of every disturbance. The unpublished 1952 de Contenson plan identified by the later 11Q introduction remains a separate archive dependency. No copy of it appeared in these inspected figures.

**Ancient 4Qa–4Qb connection: explicit disagreement.** Humbert pp. 52–55 proposes an intermediate chamber joining 4Qa and 4Qb, possibly connected farther to 10Q and perhaps toward 5Q. Figure 2.22a, p. 56 / viewer 73, reproduces the DJD 6 plan/section. Figure 2.22b is his reconstruction, with restored cavities explicitly shaded. Its connecting chambers must not become observed architectural links when the plan is reused. He acknowledges the missing exact position survey of 10Q and allows independent systems around 5Q.

Taylor p. 31 / viewer 48 discusses Humbert's reconstruction in the same volume and questions whether the inferred intermediate chamber could have existed, given the hill and runoff. The earlier Bélis 2003 concordance pp. 412–413, following the excavation description, treats 4Qa/b as originally separate. Retain the disagreement among excavation-based interpretation, later morphological reconstruction and Taylor's objection. A north arrow and scale bar in a reconstruction cannot establish the restored passages. No dated ancient threshold connecting the proposed system was obtained.

Puech p. 105 n. 44 / viewer 122 expressly rejects Humbert's proposed linked escape system, citing the separate entrances and de Vaux's original description. His interpretation of the deposited manuscripts and the feasibility of escape remains an author argument. This supplies a third explicit position in the same volume; it does not add a new excavation of the missing link.

**Erosion model: distinguish mechanism from recovered layout.** Massonnat pp. 61–63 / viewers 78–80 describes marl dissolution, runoff concentration and collapse, and compares similar morphology elsewhere in the wadi. That appendix supports erosion as a process affecting preserved caves; it does not identify the exact vanished chambers or date their collapse. Humbert's earthquake sequence and refuge reconstruction remain his interpretation. Taylor's proposed AD 115 collapse, pp. 27–31, is a historical attribution, rather than an independently dated cave-collapse layer in these pages.

**Twin Cave: native photograph closes a narrow image-access gap.** Taylor p. 17, fig. 1.4 / viewer 34, shows the paired front openings, with 11Q labelled to their north. The caption uses “Cave of the Column,” while the paragraph gives Bar-Adon's name “Twin Cave” and the old-grid reference 19371293. Her notes 54–55 identify Bar-Adon's 1989 pp. 15–17 and later excavation history. This is the figure whose pixels were previously unchecked in [the Twin Cave follow-up](https://github.com/quadrin/CopperScroll/blob/research/resource-sweep-2026-10-14/research/sites/entry25_twin_cave_followup_2026-10-01.md). The native photograph was inspected and privately archived here. It supplies no surveyed entrance azimuth, ancient threshold or dated separating wall. Taylor's account of subsequent Jones work and Amar's soap-production interpretation, pp. 21–22, reports competing functional proposals and cited investigations; it is not another original field campaign.

**Cave crosswalk: repeated evidence, wider figure coverage.** Taylor pp. 12–14 and 19–21, figs. 1.2/1.8, distinguish Q manuscript sigla, the 1952 GQ numbering and the later PQ survey numbering. The amended map credits its placements largely to Pfann's modification of Ziegler. GQ37 is relocated from the Ain Feshkha vicinity to about 1 km south of Qumran; Taylor distinguishes it from “Milik's Cave.” The location correction repeats Patrich and the archive-derived concordances; it is not a new survey. Keep the underlying map and amended overlay in the same lineage, with the altered placement visible. No coordinate transformation was performed.

**A withdrawn preservation proposal: repeated within this batch.** Taylor p. 26 n. 111 / viewer 43 explicitly withdraws her earlier bitumen treatment proposal for the linen wrappers because she found no surviving objects to confirm the reports. Her broader planned wrapping/storage interpretation remains her interpretation. The parallel [Taylor 2023 extraction](copper_changes.md) records a later withdrawal on p. 322 n. 113. Keep these as successive statements by the same author, rather than independent negative material analyses. Any project reference to treated wrappers should preserve the withdrawal and trace the actual object claim, rather than accumulating citations to earlier versions of the hypothesis.

**11Q inventory: preserve the later correction.** Taylor pp. 14, 25 and 32 repeats or relies upon the reported 11Q iron arrowhead. The complete 11Q-volume reading already found that the putative arrowhead arose by splitting one mattock description in a later account. Taylor's 2016 citation shares that textual lineage and must not revive it as an independent observation. Likewise Humbert p. 46's claim of no post-68 visits cannot override the later source record's medieval and modern evidence. The new pass adds direct reading of these older claims; the correction belongs to the existing 11Q record.

**Chronology and function: competing models, not a new deposit date.** Taylor distinguishes relatively sparse jar/wrapper assemblages from richer refuge repertoires and models repeated use of caves; Humbert distinguishes planned cliff deposits from hurried refuge deposits in the marl. Both draw on the same excavation collections. Their different reconstructions of occupation and deposition do not multiply the number of observations. Taylor pp. 28–29 also discusses later coins, jewellery and dated linen, including references into Qumran II. Those source-specific datings require their own context and object identity; they do not date every grave, conduit or manuscript deposit.

Two secondary totals are internally inconsistent: Taylor p. 24 gives 255 textiles but categories of 184 Roman, 71 Chalcolithic and five medieval; pp. 28–29 describes ten L29 coins but lists categories totalling eleven. Preserve the printed totals and components separately pending reconciliation with their inventories. No silently corrected aggregate or numeric inference was made here.

## Manuscript chapters: dependencies and limits

**García Martínez, pp. 67–79 / viewers 84–96.** The complete chapter reviews competing collection/deposition models and compares preserved contents of the individual caves. At pp. 73–74 he distinguishes palaeographic date, language and find position when assessing 3Q15. He cites de Vaux's statement that the position away from other manuscripts neither proves later deposition nor rules it out. His preference for a common collection is an interpretation of the surviving corpus; at p. 79 he explicitly says the preserved material cannot conclusively prove a single collection and deposit. Similar contents and shared caves therefore must not become an independent date for the Copper Scroll. The discussion repeats the original DJD find context and the already disputed editions, while making the inferential limits explicit. Greek architectural loanwords are compared with other technical vocabularies at p. 74; this is a genre comparison, not a new manuscript reading or location.

**Hempel, pp. 80–86 / viewers 97–103.** The chapter derives a proposed restricted, scholarly character for Cave 4Q from its manuscript profile, with the Community Rule as a test case. That proposed social access arrangement does not establish a physical library installation. The introduction lists alternative shelving, storage and disruption explanations. Her p. 83 correction identifies 6Q17 as parchment, despite its mistaken description elsewhere as papyrus after being photographed among papyri. Preserve material identification at the object level; a photograph's page grouping cannot establish material or provenance. The unnumbered Maskil table on p. 83 was inspected, with its source categories retained. No new architectural constraint or candidate relation follows from it.

**Tov, pp. 87–95 / viewers 104–112.** Tov warns that apparent corpus exclusivity depends on comparator survival and the character of those comparators. He distinguishes local preparation/copying, imported manuscripts, script, material and genre. His p. 92 discussion separates original text, later scribal signs and later user marks, noting that these layers often cannot be distinguished. These are applicable limits on interpreting an unexplained mark as an original functional instruction. He supplies no new engraving-order observation for 3Q15. His p. 91 table 5.1, inspected natively, contains catalogue counts and alternatives with differing periods and cultural groups; it should not be used as a homogeneous archaeological sample without reconciling those definitions. His conclusion prefers particular scribal/community models, while acknowledging uncertainty about production place.

**Puech, pp. 96–105 / viewers 113–122.** The opening and conclusion explicitly adopt June AD 68 as the deposition terminus from de Vaux's occupation reconstruction. His assignment of 3Q15 to AD 68 on p. 101 is therefore not an independent excavated deposition date. He also uses dated external comparators and discusses relative letter-form development; those comparisons remain separate from the assumed cave-deposition limit. At p. 105 he distinguishes the date of the writing support from the date of writing and notes that the manuscripts lack supporting stratigraphic context. His claim that shared handwriting across caves proves one deposit is a further interpretation. Shared copying and a single subsequent deposition event are separate claims. No reserved manuscript photograph was opened to assess his letter identifications.

These chapters cite overlapping earlier palaeographers, editors and original collections. Their agreement or disagreement represents analysis of those inherited observations; it does not supply four independent new field datasets. Existing text/date and engraving-order assessments are not reopened by these discussions alone.
