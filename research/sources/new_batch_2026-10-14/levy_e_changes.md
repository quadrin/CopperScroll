## Corrections and methodological additions

Tauxe et al., printed pp. 1760–1770, Figures 2–7 and Tables 1–2, distinguish specimens, field fragments and technical “sites.” A technical site may combine fragments assumed to have the same age and is not necessarily one archaeological site. Their comparison of earlier selection criteria, CCRIT and BiCEP reuses earlier measurements. It must not become another independent dating witness merely because the interpretation is republished. Tables 1–2 retain criterion thresholds, while Figure 2 shows why the original specimen fit and a criterion-compliant fit differ. The chapter distinguishes 95% BiCEP credibility intervals from earlier extended-error procedures; the uncertainty measures cannot be substituted without explanation.

Printed pp. 1764–1769 add two separate chronological limits: old wood in associated radiocarbon samples, and an “old house” problem in which objects grouped in one context can have different production ages. Averaging many fragments does not resolve that age mixture. Cooling-rate and anisotropy corrections can materially alter intensity estimates. The concluding Hazor example in Figure 7 illustrates a prior and posterior age distribution, not a free-standing date that can be transplanted into an unrelated construction sequence. These are useful checks for future heated-material dates; they supply no date for a current scroll landmark.

Greenberg's chapter, especially printed pp. 1780–1785, distinguishes the histories of individual objects from the moment an assemblage comes together and distinguishes an archive's stored contents from their interpretation. Figures 1–2 show a historical map and aerial photograph with modern campus boundaries or annotations. Those additions are interpretation layers, not original map observations. Dever's chapter, printed pp. 1794–1797, advocates testing artifact function through use and experiment while acknowledging provisional interpretations. LaBianca's models in Figures 3–7 are theoretical schemes. The author explicitly leaves their operation at particular places and times to empirical investigation (p. 1810); they cannot date a cistern or establish a regional rarity denominator.

Braswell, printed pp. 1823–1854, separates a unit of account, a medium of payment, a measure of value and stored wealth. The Egyptian example of a house valued in shati but paid for with a bed and cloth (p. 1837) is especially useful against treating every quantified inventory expression as the actual material transferred. The proposed commodity-money role of Faynan ingots is qualified by the author (p. 1833). His chartalist argument is an interpretation, not direct evidence for the Copper Scroll's bookkeeping system. The modern monetary examples and Mesoamerican or Melanesian comparisons are background; their values, units and social meanings are not Judean conversions.

## Dhiban water, caves, graves and phase controls

Ji supplies potentially useful east-of-Jordan inventory leads while preserving important dating limits. Printed p. 1857 describes Musaytiba's reservoir about 70 m northeast of the large square building: 13 × 24 m, cut 6.3 m into bedrock, with plastered steps descending from the northwest corner. A second reservoir lies about 150 m east of the first and is still used with modern cement. The modern coating is not evidence for its ancient lining or construction phase. Qrayya has at least six dwelling caves (p. 1858); Aliyan, Kaum and Dheibeh also have caves or cisterns (p. 1859). These descriptions warrant original survey follow-up, not immediate feature identification.

Printed pp. 1867–1869 describe WT-14's dams and bedrock channel carrying runoff to a cistern, related systems at Toga and WT-48, and settling tanks at Mudayna ath-Thamad. DS-152 has 16 dams and 15 cisterns; DS-131 has terrace dams and enclosure walls. Figures 3–4 preserve the mapped relationships, scales and north arrows. The author explicitly says a few surface sherds cannot chronologically verify the constructions (p. 1868). A Nabatean or early Roman attribution based on pottery and architectural similarity must remain conditional until construction contexts are obtained.

Figure 1 and printed pp. 1862–1865 reconstruct routes by connecting settlements; they are not a survey of every surviving road segment. In the qasr discussion, upper platforms may reuse earlier buildings and both dating and function remain partly speculative (pp. 1870–1876). Proposed military structures, religious platforms and inns are alternative functions. The average spacing calculation uses straight-line coordinate distances, explicitly different from ancient travel distance (p. 1876).

The ceramic totals also need reconciliation before quantitative reuse. Printed p. 1861 gives 89 painted sherds, then describes 11 Phase I sherds as 8% of the sample; p. 1881 describes 21 Phase II sherds. The denominator behind the stated phase percentages is not established by those lines. The same paragraph says 65 of 78 Hellenistic sites continued into the Roman period and reports 81%; retain the supplied counts and percentage as distinct statements rather than silently normalizing them. Figure 2 plots site distribution, not a sherd-count histogram.

Possible seasonal shelters and burial rings around the plateau are differentiated as hypotheses (pp. 1865–1866). The comparison to Qazone shaft graves does not date a grave–pit or grave–pool junction. Likewise, sparse Nabatean pottery or an unverified photographed coin need not prove Nabatean occupation of Ataruz (pp. 1877–1878). Ataruz in central Jordan is geographically distinct from the project's western-pool Atara target. No hydraulic result for Atara follows from this chapter.

The most direct follow-up sources are Daviau and Foley's 2007 Wadi ath-Thamad water-management report (SHAJ 9, pp. 357–365), Ladurner's 2013 agricultural-settlement report (SHAJ 11, pp. 613–623), and Ji and colleagues' Dhiban survey reports for 1996–1999. This chapter updates and synthesizes the same survey campaigns; overlapping descriptions must be joined by original site and field observation.

## Identity, names and archive provenance

Arbel, printed pp. 1889–1910, treats the Ottoman Jaffa hexagram coffins as an identity problem rather than identifying religion from one symbol. An 1828 marker was reused in a later burial frame (p. 1890), and the late coffin manufacture relies on timber, saw marks and nails (p. 1893), not the marker's first date. The final interpretation favors the cemetery context but leaves categorical identification unresolved (p. 1907). The period is much later than the scroll. Its value here is the controlled warning against assigning identity, date or function from a shared symbol alone.

Killebrew and Skinner, printed pp. 1911–1931, contrast a modern sign denying Napoleon's presence with the historical-source case for Tel Akko. The 1799 Sidney Smith map in Figure 9 differs in provenance from Alison's 1848 atlas in Figure 11 and Smith and Howitt's 1857 isometric plan in Figure 15. The latter two are later publications even where the prose groups the illustrations as siege-era evidence. Figures 10 and 14 are modern overlays or digitizations, not new field observations. The same May 9 eyewitness letter reproduced in Barrow, Howard and La Jonquière is one witness, not three (p. 1930). This case provides a source-tracing control, not a scroll association.

Cline, printed pp. 1933–1951, supplies a concrete archival correction. The date pencilled on the Albright–Badè letter is 1927, whereas its contents and event sequence support 26 November 1926 (p. 1938, note 9; Figure 1 at p. 1939). New APS correspondence changes the evidence available after Cline's 2020 book, but his ultimate explanation remains a hypothesis. The contemporaneous published statement that Albright stood on Megiddo's summit conflicts with the later biographical account of denied access (pp. 1949–1950). The decisive 18 October 1925 letter remains missing, as does the exact instruction behind the incident. The chapter demonstrates why an absent archive item must remain absent rather than being supplied from a plausible narrative.
