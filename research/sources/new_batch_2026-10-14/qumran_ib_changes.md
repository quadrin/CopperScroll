## What the additional chapters contribute

### Cemetery identity: a necessary location distinction

Jonathan Norton, “Reassessment of Controversial Studies on the Cemetery,” pp. 107–127 / viewer 137–157, distinguishes the **1955 northern secondary cemetery**, which he calls Qumran North, from the **cimetière nord on the eastern plateau**. The former contained about a dozen graves a few minutes north of the ruin; Tombes A/B were opened there in 1955. The latter includes T9/T10, opened in 1951. Norton pp. 107–109 says the 1994 volume-I p. 214 diagram omits Qumran North, while p. 347 conflates the labels. His table 8, p. 114 / viewer 144, keeps A/B separate from T9/T10 in table 7.

**Evidence lineage:** Norton credits Puech 1998 for recognizing the distinction and cites de Vaux's reports, archival descriptions and the same human remains examined by Sheridan. The parallel source-8 reader reports that Puech p. 31 n. 3 already distinguishes the two groups, their dates, the mislabelling and the two photographs. Deduplicate Norton under that documentary correction. His collection-history discussion adds detail, rather than another field discovery. The original 1994 pages and their photographs were not opened in this pass. Coordinate registration, an opening beside these graves and an absolute construction date are absent from these inspected pages. A plan of the eastern headland cannot be substituted for a plan of Qumran North when assessing a northern-secondary-cemetery relation.

Norton pp. 109–115 also identifies discrepancies between descriptive depths, figure-derived depths and later compilations. His table keys distinguish missing data from recorded absence. At p. 110 he recommends Schultz's tables for purposes beyond his own discussion. Preserve those lineages when using a tomb dimension; Norton's compilation is not a replacement field measurement.

### Burial form and material preservation do not supply a date by themselves

Norton pp. 118–124 separates grave orientation, shaft form, burial position, associated finds and proposed cultural/date assignments. In his assessment, neither east–west orientation nor a shallow pit uniquely establishes a later burial. His p. 122 appraisal explicitly treats much of the cemetery's association with the site's main occupation as an assumption. Pottery in fill need not date an inhumation. He reports that paraffin consolidation restricts radiocarbon work on de Vaux's human remains; this describes the collections as known in 2003, without establishing the current feasibility of decontamination methods.

**Contribution:** an explicit dating and preservation limitation for the evidence ledger. These pages do not date a candidate opening, pool or grave-mouth relationship. His discussion of comparable shaft graves at Qazone, ʿAin el-Ghuweir, Beit Safafa and Abila is a secondary comparison; those sites' original reports require their own provenance checks. No regional frequency or exclusivity estimate follows.

### A bundled metal assemblage, with a conditional concealment history

Noël Lacoudre et al., “L’amas métallique KhQ 960,” pp. 397–404 / viewer 427–434, report radiographic, analytical and conservation work on a compact assemblage recovered at Qumran in 1953. The seven components include a small copper-alloy vessel and iron tools. The remaining organic impressions, shown in figs. 10–12, document an exterior wrapping or mat; the assemblage's compactness reflects grouping as well as later corrosion. Figs. 1–3 distinguish radiographic appearance, restored objects and Chambon's interpretive drawings. These are different representations of the same assemblage.

Humbert's contextual addendum, p. 405 / viewer 435, says the approximately twenty-piece metal lot was catalogued from **L52 north, lower level**. He acknowledges imprecise field notes, repeated alterations and incomplete later-phase plans. He proposes that it lay in a small plastered basin in the room's northeast corner. The proposed basin placement is an inference from the inventory and architecture, not a precisely recorded find coordinate. His reconstruction has L43 sealing L52 and the bundles sealed by Period III rebuilding. Dating deposition to AD 68 depends on accepting the proposed Period-III succession; his alternatives include abandonment and concealment.

**Contribution:** a source-specific comparison for bundled valuable equipment and reuse/sealing of an installation. It does not independently establish intentional hiding in AD 68. Lacoudre's functional and Essene attribution and Humbert's contextual reconstruction must remain attributed to those authors. The observed mat impressions support packaging; they do not establish the purpose of packaging. A scoped search of existing research Markdown found no prior KhQ960/L52 extraction. That search establishes a local ledger gap, not absence from every historical project output.

### Cave numbering: prevent records of different caves from being joined

Mireille Bélis, “Révision commentée des différents systèmes de numérotation,” pp.409–415 / viewer439–445, supplies an archive/publication concordance. Her p.409 expressly says the **excavation date** sometimes supplied the only reliable criterion for distinguishing3Q and8Q provenance. Preserve the full dated record and its original numbering scheme: there is no valid global `G8/GQ8 → 8Q` replacement rule. In the1952 scheme, Cave8 / Gr8 / A3 refers to3Q; the specific object **GQ8–12 = 3Q036.2** is catalogued as the3Q lamp in Młynarczyk2016p.115 (DJDIII,p.15,Fig.5:6). The distinct **Gr8Q–12 = 8Q045.1** is the8Q lamp there (DJDIII,Fig.6:3); the2016 appendix,p.276, records its separate1955 lineage. These2016 object-level cross-checks were supplied by the companion reader in this sweep. A prefix alone must not override object number, date and catalogue context. The existing [DJDIII extraction](https://github.com/quadrin/CopperScroll/blob/research/resource-sweep-2026-10-14/research/history/djd3_text_cave_list_extracted.md) already distinguishes8-x from8Qx and records the1955/1956 dating inconsistency. Those observations remain **already captured**, while Bélis adds archival variants and explicit discussion of uncertain equivalences.

At pp. 414–415 Bélis leaves **D14 = 7Q** uncertain, arguing that the 1952 northern position favors the small cliff Cave 7. Do not propagate D14 as an unqualified identity for the southern marl cave 7Q. Her p. 411 n. 12 distinguishes the 1952 small Cave 24 from Patrich's different Cave 24 north of 11Q, explored in 1988. These are catalogue safeguards, rather than newly surveyed structures. Missing entrance bearings or morphology remain missing when absent from her archival sources.

Bélis p. 412 describes 4Qa and 4Qb as separate cavities; p. 413 explains that pooled manuscript provenance drove their shared 4Q designation. This is secondary reporting of the excavation record. It does not supply independently measured thresholds or a new entry-25 match. Her p. 414 reports an unsuccessful attempt to access W. Reed's working notes at ASOR; this historical access statement does not prove that the records remain unavailable today.

The p. 414–415 maps retain grid labels, north arrows and scale bars. Their caption and context identify them as cave-distribution maps, not surveyed plans of individual openings or their construction phases. No new registration or measured distance was derived from them.

### Pottery methods: provenance signal and age require separate records

Kaare Lund Rasmussen, “On the Provenance and Firing Temperature of Pottery,” pp. 101–104 / viewer 131–134, describes a small blind-labelled sample set, magnetic susceptibility, thermoluminescence sensitivity and firing-temperature estimation. The p. 101 methods explicitly state that the regenerated provenance signal contains no age information. Pages 102–103 separately discuss a rough TL age assessment of **KLR2621 / K12 / KhQ2622** and interpret it as modern, with unknown burial irradiation conditions and a caveat about heating after recovery. This is a sample-specific result, not a date for a water installation.

The author treats provenance discrimination as limited by sample size and reports both agreements and disagreements with neutron activation results. His table 1 identifies the archaeological/control labels, while table 2 and figs. 1–3 report analytical results. A chemical or firing-temperature group cannot be silently converted into a construction phase. This methodological evidence can qualify future source comparisons; it provides no new candidate location.
