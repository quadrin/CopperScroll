## T. Canaan, printed172–203 / PDF181–212

The article reports early twentieth-century customs concerning women's status, marriage, seclusion, etiquette, property, menstruation/childbirth, raids, sexual accusations, homicide and refuge. It also compares those customs with biblical, Qur'anic and classical sources. Such comparisons are the author's interpretive framework. They do not establish continuous practice from the Copper Scroll period.

Printed177 / PDF186 n5 explains that the local term for a childbirth “pit” can designate the space below a seated mother, rather than an excavated pit. This is a modern Arabic usage and cannot determine the Scroll's Hebrew terminology; its value is a warning against turning a translated functional label into a specific archaeological structure.

Printed181–183 / PDF190–192 gives observations about female saints, female spirits associated with springs, and matronymic or female-derived personal/family/tribal names. The spring information points back to Canaan, JPOS I p162, and the shrine statistics to his Mohammedan Saints and Sanctuaries, p235ff. These are bibliographic leads about modern beliefs, not ancient inscriptions. Printed183 lists, among others, Sarah and Raḥil families in Jerusalem, Wardeh at ʿEn ʿArik and Bir Zet, and female-derived tribal appellations. Nothing in this article directly associates one of these modern families or names with a Scroll site.

Printed184 / PDF193 reports roof/balcony parapets made of clay tubes, 1–1.5m high, and the “threshold” as a boundary in domestic custom. Printed189 / PDF198 describes visiting tombs and communal cemetery activity in Hebron. These are modern ethnography; they do not supply ancient thresholds, bearings, cave geometry, or Koḥlit grave/pool relationships. Printed191 / PDF200 describes coin-bearing headdresses and jewelry; it is not evidence of an ancient deposit.

Delta: article completed and categorized as contextual ethnography; no candidate-specific archaeological measurement or dated relationship found. Arabic footnote extracts were visually inspected where OCR garbled them, but no new critical Arabic transcription is offered.

## Albrecht Alt, printed204–221 / PDF213–230

Main section, printed204–215 / PDF213–224, argues for Orda as a late-antique episcopal and administrative center in the saltus Gerariticus. Alt begins from the Madaba mosaic's relative position of Orda between Seana and Photis, and locates its broad search area on the Gaza–Beersheba route near the Wadi esh-Sheriʿa crossing at Abu Hureira. He explicitly reserves selection of a particular ruin for archaeological investigation (printed205–206 / PDF214–215).

Printed206 n1 / PDF215 n1 rejects treating Abel's resemblance between Orda and Khirbet Umm ʿAdre as proof; the resemblance leaves a possible location, not an established one. Printed206–207 / PDF215–216 traces how the forms ΟΡΑΩΝ and ΑΡΔΩΝ in synodal acts, and their Latin translations, became misread as Arad. Alt's proposed correction to Orda depends on separate mosaic evidence and letter/vowel explanations. This is a source-transmission case, not a new reading of a Copper Scroll name.

Printed209 / PDF218 cites Eusebius's rounded 25-Roman-mile (37km) distance south from Eleutheropolis to the saltus and treats it as compatible with his southwest location. Footnote states that Eusebius does not distinguish intermediate compass directions here. That author-specific assertion must not become a general license to relax directions in the Scroll.

Printed210 / PDF219 notes a revised proposal for Barsama: Khirbet el-Far, 7km ESE of southern Tell el-Farʿa. Printed211–214 / PDF220–223 develops a hypothetical transfer of administrative functions and a change from the archaizing episcopal name Gerar to Orda. The names can refer to a territory/office while its actual center changes. Printed214–215 / PDF223–224 explicitly declines to derive the Bronze/Iron Age city Gerar's exact location from the late-antique administrative center. Good methodological comparator: territorial continuity does not prove settlement identity.

Appendix, printed215–221 / PDF224–230, interprets a medieval episcopal description joining Pharan/Kadis to distances copied from Eusebius's Arad entry. Alt proposes a relocated episcopate and favors the neighborhood of el-Eksefe/Malaatha, about7km from Tell Arad, over Tell Arad itself, because of ecclesiastical remains. This is his reconstruction; it is not a securely dated migration sequence in this review. Printed220 n2 / PDF229 n2 tentatively relates Ason/Ausan, inscribed Asoa and biblical Jesua (Neh11:26), then Tell es-Saʿwe or a neighboring site. Preserve the tentativeness.

Delta: full original argument read; provides no Atara pool–channel drawing and no independent Copper Scroll candidate measurement. It adds documented examples of corrupt name transmission, archaizing territorial titles, reused distances and relocated administrative centers.

## Ditlef Nielsen, printed222–237 / PDF231–246, with G. Dalman's reply printed237–240 / PDF246–249

Nielsen explains his1929 Petra work and disputes many functional identifications in Dalman's1908/1912 books. Printed224–228 / PDF233–237 distinguishes observed rock cuttings from terms such as sanctuary, sacrificial dining place, altar, libation basin, shrine niche, and hooks for votive offerings. His counts of wrongly classified monuments are his critical judgments, not results independently checked here.

Printed229 / PDF238, fig1, presents the first el-Ḥabis “sanctuary” as quarrying plus workers' accommodations. Nielsen reports a stone-descending route and broken-stone heaps on the western slope. Dalman replies at printed238 / PDF247 that fig1 reproduces his Abb170 and omits the more informative view in Abb169; he emphasizes the summit remnant and ascent. Therefore the supplied photo cannot adjudicate the quarry-versus-sanctuary claim by itself.

Printed230–231 / PDF239–240, figs2–3 continuing at printed232 / PDF241: Qaṭṭar ed-Der has dripping water, collecting basins, symbols/graffiti, and a large cave with seating on three sides, niches, and paired holes. Nielsen proposes a travelers' rest/inn and possibly garden irrigation. Dalman printed238 / PDF247 accepts drinking use but says there is no irrigation outlet and that a garden physically connected with the basins has not been demonstrated; he also describes a cross cut into an older deity-symbol. Practical use and perceived sanctity can coexist. The modern label or presence of a symbol does not establish a single exclusive function.

Printed232–233 / PDF241–242, fig4: Nielsen reclassifies the “second sanctuary of the hermitages” as a water reservoir, citing a conduit through a rear wall approximately0.50m high toward habitation below. He quotes Dalman's contrary functional judgment about the channel. Dalman's reply, printed239 / PDF248, describes a rock slab with an edge on only one side, questions storage in a shallow open basin under seasonal rainfall/evaporation, identifies a separate nearby cistern, and says no physical connection with that cistern is visible. These are opposed interpretations and observations; neither may be silently promoted into consensus. The article gives no measured hydraulic levels, dated joint, section drawing, or stratigraphic demonstration.

Printed233–235 / PDF242–244 argues that varied buildings and rock cuttings on ed-Der belong to different periods and that excavation is required. Nielsen reports masonry watch-house remains at several summits; this is attribution based on his inspection and topographic interpretation. At printed235 / PDF244 he challenges an “altar's” orientation and recorded raised rim, instead attributing the block to quarrying with unsuitable soft veins. Fig5 at printed236 / PDF245 shows this block but supplies no north arrow or scale. It cannot establish entrance bearings or a phase chronology for any Copper Scroll cave.

Printed236–237 / PDF245–246 notes repeated later rock cutting, rain, earthquakes and recent collapse between1927 and1929. That observation is useful when distinguishing a historical survey configuration from present geometry. It concerns Petra sandstone, with no automatic transfer to the limestone caves under investigation.

Dalman's reply explicitly defines “sanctuary” more broadly than Nielsen's apparent public-sacrifice definition (printed237–238 / PDF246–247). Some disagreement is classificatory. At printed239 / PDF248 he allows the ed-Der block could precede the tomb façade, reminding us that apparent neighboring features need not be contemporary. Again, he offers a possible history rather than an excavated sequence.

Figure assessment: all five original figures inspected. The halftones show major rock shapes and settings. Dark/grainy regions, lack of scale, and selected viewpoints prevent independent channel-gradient, exact wall-height, construction-joint or bearing measurements. No new precision extracted from them.

Delta for Atara: a useful published counterexample to inferring basin–channel function from labels. The outstanding Atara requirements remain the actual connection, relative elevations/flow direction and compatible phase. Petra supplies no Atara observation. Delta for entry25: generic comparanda for overprinting/collapse and functional uncertainty, with no IV/17, Twin Cave or XII/53 measurement. No candidate score changes follow.

## W. F. Albright, printed241–251 / PDF250–260

Printed241–244 / PDF250–253 reconstructs Tirzah's historical requirements from biblical texts and textual variants. Its chronological and political reconstructions, including the dating assigned to a Song of Songs poem, remain Albright's1931 interpretations; this review does not endorse them as current consensus.

Printed243 / PDF252 discusses variants in2Kings15:14–16, including Tharsila and competing Tirzah/Tipsah/Tappuah readings. He warns that a district may preserve a destroyed principal town's name (printed243–244 / PDF252–253). Printed244 n3 / PDF253 n3 cites Klein's Tulluzā–Talmudic Turlusa association. This is a secondhand citation to pursue if needed, not direct inspection of the Talmudic witness.

Printed245–246 / PDF254–255 reports northern Tell el-Farʿah about11km straight NE of Nablus and15km by road; length slightly over300m and width over200m, correcting a prior300m width. Spring lies to the north; an incised stream surrounds NW/W/S, and another valley NE/E. Those measurements are Albright's report, not new geospatial measurements. Surface pottery mixes periods because of denudation. He lists Bronze and Iron Age material plus scattered Roman, Byzantine and Arabic sherds, and explicitly says the suggested ninth-century abandonment is unproved. No individual Roman structural phase or grave is established.

Printed247 / PDF256 rejects identifying northern Tell el-Farʿah with Ophrah through name resemblance. He says the ordinary form Farʿah names elongated ridges at multiple places and gives Hajleh/Hoglah and Ṣarʿah/Ṣorʿah as phonetic analogies. He proposes Ophrah–et-Taiyibeh through euphemistic renaming. This creates a specific risk in this volume: Alt's southern Tell el-Farʿa at printed210 / PDF219 and Albright's northern Tell el-Farʿah at printed245 / PDF254 are different places.

Printed248–251 / PDF257–260 uses Samaria ostraca to rearrange Manassite districts. Printed249 / PDF258 records Hefer, Hoglah, Noah and Tirzah among biblical subdivisions; he reports Hoglah and Noah in ostraca, while Tirzah/Mahlah/Milkah do not occur in the ostraca being discussed. Printed249–250 / PDF258–259 explicitly notes uncertain reading of a district name and proposes Srʾl. Printed250 / PDF259 discusses Ḥaṣerot, Qoṣo, ʿzh, Yṣt and Gebaʿ, plus Almaton/Amatin and Elat-parʿon/Farʿon proposals. The Shishak-list Almaton comparison is explicitly assigned probably to a different, southern town in his footnote.

Potential association leads: Hajleh/Hoglah at printed247 and the Hoglah district in Samaria ostraca at printed249–250; Tulluzā/Turlusa at printed244n3; Ṣarʿah/Ṣorʿah at printed247; Ason/Asoa/Jesua from Alt printed220n2. None is here asserted to match a Copper Scroll spelling or to add a new attestation to an existing project name row. Exact name matching and original witness inspection remain necessary; source novelty against the full repository was outside this subtask.

Delta: an original1931 topographic argument and reported surface evidence completed. No Roman Koḥlit pool/grave configuration or entry25 geometry appears in this article. Its general method is a useful comparator, but an elimination argument remains sensitive to completeness of the candidate inventory and independence of initial constraints.
