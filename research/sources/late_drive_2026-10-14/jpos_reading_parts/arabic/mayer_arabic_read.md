# Mayer Arabic continuation: direct visual reading receipt

15 October 2026 UTC / 14 October Los Angeles. Source: L. A. Mayer, "A Sequel to Mujir ad-Din's Chronicle," *Journal of the Palestine Oriental Society* XI (1931), printed pp. 89–97 = supplied PDF pp. 94–102, in `work/jericho/raw/jpos.pdf`. This is an edited Arabic chronicle text and poem, with manuscript folio markers and editorial apparatus. It is not an excavation report or the original manuscript. The Arabic OCR was not used to establish the readings below.

All nine pages were rendered at 2× and read directly, including the footnotes; seven received additional 3× whole-page renders and six received 4× lower-page crops for checking apparatus and small print. The printing is sufficiently legible to establish each page's content and the material claims below. This is a complete page-by-page visual traversal, with bounded lexical/apparatus uncertainties retained below. It is **not a certified complete transcription**. Where the modern editor printed lacunae in a damaged manuscript witness, a larger scan cannot recover the missing manuscript words.

## Page receipts

### Printed 89 / PDF 94

**Read:** the two concluding English editorial paragraphs, Arabic invocation and earthquake passage through its page-end continuation, manuscript folio markers J/L 277r and 277v, and both footnotes. The English paragraph distinguishes the witnesses' relationship to the printed chronicle and its 898–900 AH narrative. That is Mayer's textual assessment, not new project manuscript collation.

**Arabic content:** a major earthquake after noon on Thursday, 10 Dhu al-Qaʿda 902 AH. The listed affected places are Jerusalem, Hebron, Gaza, Ramla, al-Karak, al-Salt and Nablus, extending to Damascus. Upper-storey buildings in Jerusalem and Hebron are reported destroyed or cracked. The account names Sultan Qaitbay's school at Gaza as destroyed; his Jerusalem school suffered on its qibla/south, north and east sides; the head of the Chain Gate minaret fell. Nablus is said to have suffered most, with about 500 people killed under rubble. A frightening aftershock is placed on Sunday night, 10 Muharram; another notice begins Wednesday, 12 Rabiʿ I and continues on the next page.

**Footnotes:** note 1 preserves a J/L spelling difference in the minaret word. Note 2 gives a damaged alternative witness reading, including a different date, 13 Dhu al-Qaʿda, and fragments concerning people and tents in the mosque; the editor explicitly explains that dots represent completely obliterated words in the manuscript's final page. Do not combine that witness's date/fragments with the main text into a single reconstructed event sequence. Several square-bracketed letter restorations and the editor's (!) remain editorial, not directly observed manuscript letters.

**Limit:** dates are retained in the calendar printed by the edition. No Gregorian conversion or independent earthquake-catalogue identification was performed. The damaged apparatus witness cannot be read continuously because the edition explicitly lacks words.

### Printed 90 / PDF 95

**Read:** the earthquake continuation, a copying notice, the complete religious prose commendation and its couplets, the subscription continuing to p. 91, and both footnotes.

**Content:** the Wednesday, 12 Rabiʿ I notice specifies 903 AH and a before-noon shock, felt by some people; it also recalls earlier shocks and people leaving their dwellings, by day and night. A copying formula names Musa ibn Fayyan; exact personal-name vocalisation remains unverified. The following religious preface praises a learned author's compilation and its usefulness to readers of Jerusalem's history. It contains the usual profession of faith and prayers for Muhammad and commends the book's author as chief qadi. The subscription names Muhammad ibn Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr ibn Ali ibn Abi Sharif al-Maqdisi, ending across the page boundary.

**Footnotes:** note 1 says only manuscript L continues from the marked point; the earlier J/L labels must not be carried through the remaining text as two continuing independent witnesses. Note 2 records that the subscription word bearing the marker is crossed out in the manuscript. These are Mayer's apparatus reports; the underlying manuscript was not opened here.

### Printed 91 / PDF 96

**Read:** completion of the subscription; the obituary; the poem attribution and opening five couplets; manuscript folio 278r; note 1.

**Content:** obituary for Kamal al-Din Abu al-Maʿali Muhammad ibn Abi Sharif al-Shafiʿi, dated Thursday night, 15 Jumada I 906 AH. His funeral prayer is placed after Thursday's noon prayer at al-Aqsa. A burial-location sentence places him in an upper courtyard/burial area containing the grave of Shaykh Khalifa al-Maliki. The author refers readers to a fuller biographical account and says the scholar had read this chronicle during 902 AH. The following poem is attributed to ʿAlaʾ al-Din Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Ayyub ibn Mansur al-Maqdisi al-Shafiʿi, associated with the Salahiya school. Its opening praises Jerusalem's visitation, the Night Journey, prophetic presence, the qibla and the Last Judgment.

**Footnote:** the edition corrects the manuscript's spelling for the word meaning souls; that is a textual emendation, not a second reading made from the original manuscript by this project.

**Limit:** the exact spelling of the burial-area noun in the obituary is not secured here beyond the courtyard-like reading. No modern cemetery/plot identification is made from it. Personal-name normalisations are transliteration aids, without independent identification searches.

### Printed 92 / PDF 97

**Read:** every couplet of the continuing poem. No separate editorial footnote text is present on this page; the parenthesised marker within the poem is retained as printed.

**Content:** longing for Jerusalem; Abraham as the friend of God and father of prophets; divine blessing on the sacred land; visitors' hospitality and consolation; Muhammad's Night Journey and assembly of prophets; Solomon and the building; sins remitted through visitation and appropriate worship. The poem connects scriptural personages with sacred-land praise rather than recording an excavation or a dated construction observation. Its descriptions of chambers/places of divine manifestation belong to devotional narrative.

**Limit:** poetic/descriptive vocabulary is not geocoded. The internal marker has no matching footnote text on this supplied leaf.

### Printed 93 / PDF 98

**Read:** every couplet, including the transition at manuscript folio 278v. No footnotes occur on the page.

**Content:** scriptural allusions to Jesus, David, Israel/Jacob, Moses and resurrection imagery; poetic descriptions of seasons, water, vegetation, worship and illumination. The closing section tells visitors to adhere to prescribed practice and avoid innovations, including touching/kissing local stones and visiting a tree, cave or rock as if their ritual treatment had independent authority. Mecca is distinguished in the stone-touching/kissing rule.

**Archaeological limit:** the cave/rock/tree words demonstrate their place in this author's late-medieval ritual argument. The passage gives no survey, dimensions, exact location, ancient name equivalence or proof of the relevant practices' antiquity. Its poetic environmental comparisons must not be coded as measured springs, pools or vegetation evidence.

### Printed 94 / PDF 99

**Read:** every couplet and the two printed spelling-variant notes.

**Content:** continuation of the ritual prohibitions: imitating pilgrimage rites at Jerusalem, circling the Rock, acts associated with departing Hebron, running and pebble-casting at Bab Hitta, treating graves as worship places, treating Jerusalem visitation as a substitute for the pilgrimage, and special claims made for the Cradle, Bab Hitta and Dome of the Balance. It rejects unsupported stories from custodians/visitors, including a place associated with Ali, a grave attributed to Adam's mother and a "throne of Bilqis" in al-Aqsa. It then warns against other writings/stories and ritual practices.

**Footnotes:** two notes preserve manuscript spellings for the edited forms at the standing/ritual-pause word and the proclamation/display word. The printed variant glyphs were inspected; their full diplomatic normalisation is unnecessary for the source's archaeological use and is not claimed.

**Interpretation limit:** these are the poet's prescriptions and objections to named traditions, not independent verification that every object, attributed grave or traditional identification was physically present as described. The particular writings/person referenced in the later warning are not identified independently here.

### Printed 95 / PDF 100

**Read:** final poem couplets, manuscript folio 279r, note 1, and the entire prose opening of the 914 AH notice.

**Content:** the poem returns to prescribed al-Aqsa/Jerusalem worship, salutations and remembrance, lamps and devotional practice, David and the Psalms, Solomon, Muhammad's Night Journey, Abraham and prophetic descendants, John son of Zechariah and Mary, and prayer for the poet's own burial in Jerusalem. The repeated interrogative word in the relevant verses asks "where" the earlier holy persons have gone; it must not be mistranscribed as the patronymic "son of." The verse mentioning monks is clear in subject but its exact noun morphology remains unsettled here.

The prose then dates an event to **914 AH**, during Sultan al-Malik al-Ashraf Qansuh al-Ghawri and Amir Janbirdi al-Ghazali's authority over Damascus, al-Karak, Jerusalem and Hebron, with responsibility for the two sanctuaries. Jerusalem residents had intended to join the Meccan pilgrimage after a long interruption; the narrative continues on p. 96.

**Footnote:** a manuscript spelling variant is reported for the opening of the poet's prayer to God. No archaeological datum depends on that spelling.

### Printed 96 / PDF 101

**Read:** all prose and both notes, including folio marker 279v.

**Content:** pilgrimage had reportedly been interrupted for more than ten years because of insecurity. A group of local Arab escorts agreed to protect the pilgrims and bring them to Aqabat Ayla to join the Egyptian caravan; people from Hama and elsewhere joined. Snow began in Jerusalem on Monday night, 5 Shawwal, equated in the source with 5 Shubat in the solar/Rumi calendar. After an interruption, rain on Wednesday–Friday melted much of it. Departure was planned for Saturday, 20 Shawwal = 10 Shubat. Heavy snow fell overnight and for seven successive days/nights, intensifying on Friday; Friday attendance became impossible for many. Further intermittent snowfall and rains prevented pilgrimage departure, with snow lying in al-Aqsa and the city's streets and on roofs. The text reports many deaths, continuing onto p. 97.

**Footnotes:** note 1 explicitly supplies a missing verb with two alternative proposals, equivalent to "occurred" or "was". That is an editorial lacuna with alternatives; neither option is silently turned into a transmitted word. Note 2 distinguishes the manuscript's singular form from the editor's plural at the many-deaths phrase.

**Limit:** paired calendar expressions are reported, without independently reconciling them to a modern calendar. Snow depth is described rhetorically, without a numerical depth. Road insecurity, routes and causal explanation are source claims.

### Printed 97 / PDF 102

**Read:** all prose and the copyist's concluding formula. The only apparatus marker on the page has no corresponding footnote text printed on this leaf.

**Content:** increased mortality; some people did not receive mosque funeral prayer and some children were buried where they died. Market buildings and other structures collapsed with deaths under rubble. For more than twenty days Jerusalem suffered blocked routes, interrupted livelihoods, expensive/scarce wheat and other food. Worship at al-Aqsa ceased for days, including calls/prayers at night; snow remained in the mosque and streets for more than a month. The author calls this worse than an episode in **877 AH**. The copying colophon names the work as the history of Jerusalem and Hebron by qadi Mujir al-Din al-Hanbali al-Maqdisi and dates completion of this copy to early **Rabiʿ II 956 AH**, naming the copyist Jibril al-Husayni, followed by nisbas whose exact normalisation remains unverified.

**Limits:** 956 AH dates this copy/subscription, not the snow event and not any ancient structure. The market-collapse and burial claims are a transmitted chronicle report; no excavation context, plan or demolition layer is established. Note marker (1) after the verb introducing the shortage has no printed note on this page; no missing note is invented.

## Relevance and retained limits

The continuation supplies a late-medieval report of damage to named buildings and a sixteenth-century copying colophon, plus a poem preserving and disputing sacred-place traditions. Its personal/scriptural names and cave/rock/grave associations can enter a historical-association record only with that genre, date and attribution. A shared name with an ancient text does not establish continuity, exact location or feature identity.

The earthquake and snow accounts might inform later disturbance history of named Jerusalem structures; they do not date an ancient pool, channel, cave threshold or tomb-mouth relationship. This read yielded no Atara pool–channel evidence and no Jericho pit/grave relation. The Arabic word for Hebron/al-Khalil in the title and narrative is a city/sanctuary reference; do not turn it into a Copper Scroll Koḥlit equivalence.

All nine pages received direct reading; residual limits remain exact vocalisation/normalisation of Musa's patronymic (p. 90), the obituary's burial-area noun (p. 91), the monks' noun morphology (p. 95), the copyist's final nisbas (p. 97), the explicitly damaged witness apparatus (p. 89), and absent footnote text for internal markers on pp. 92/97. These do not conceal uninspected paragraphs. Preserve them in the page ledger instead of labelling the Arabic a fully resolved transcription.
