### Coordinates, measurements and entrances

Langford, Davidovich and Frumkin, *Survey, mapping and research methods*, p.8/viewer2:

- Historical coordinates and elevations derived from maps could err by tens of metres. The atlas marks approximate rounded coordinates **נצ״מ**. Coordinates marked **נ״צ** usually derive from handheld GPS, with horizontal accuracy of a few metres; cliff obstruction reduces it.
- Cave mapping uses a connected traverse of stations measured for distance, direction and slope, supplemented by wall/roof/floor measurements and drawing. The chapter describes the Malham system as BCRA **5C**, with station-position errors under **0.1 m**; random errors are distributed along traverse loops. This statement describes the survey method, not a verified error budget for every reused historical drawing.
- **Project inference:** relative survey accuracy cannot provide the same accuracy for global entrance coordinates. Neither number establishes the location or age of a buried ancient threshold. The entrance-to-traverse tie, original survey/date, map scale, projection and independent phase evidence must accompany a target measurement.

P.9/viewer3 distinguishes three displays:

- A plan preserves horizontal relationships; main level black, lower level red dotted, upper level blue dash-dot. Complex levels may be displaced with connecting lines.
- A developed vertical profile preserves passage dimensions, height and slope locally but **does not preserve true spatial relationships between different passages**. A normal vertical projection preserves relations within one projection plane but foreshortens features outside it.
- Cross sections show representative shapes at labelled locations. A section symbol and view direction must be followed rather than inferring correspondence from page placement.

The same page explicitly distinguishes **פתח**, a connection of the cave with the surface, from **כניסה**, a convenient opening for human entry; where multiple openings exist, the principal one is generally labelled כניסה. These labels therefore cannot by themselves establish that only one opening existed, define each opening's outward aspect, or date its use.

P.10/viewer4 describes archaeological survey of sediments, exposed excavation/looting sections, surface and spoil finds, and the surrounding access/building evidence. Atlas periods usually derive from diagnostic objects and only sometimes radiometric dates; the authors warn that renewed analysis can revise earlier dates or find the data insufficient. **Project inference:** a cave-level period in the atlas must remain separate from construction dates for a specific blocking wall or floor.

P.11/viewer5 is a blank Malham field-survey form, raster-only and omitted by native text extraction. It was rendered at higher resolution and read visually. It requests surveyor/date, coordinate units, name source, map/traverse accuracy, approach, opening dimensions and inward azimuth, cave dimensions, geology, sediments, archaeology and biological observations, with locations relative to survey stations. It contains **no filled target record**. Notably its opening-azimuth field is inward from the opening toward the first traverse leg. A filled field therefore needs direction-convention handling before an outward-mouth-aspect comparison; do not transpose an inward bearing into an outward bearing unchanged. The form's date codes include Roman and Byzantine, but its empty fields date no cave or wall.

### Geological geometry and archaeological geometry

Frumkin, *Geology, morphology and landscape development*, pp.12–22/viewers1–11, was read completely. The discussion distinguishes confined-aquifer caves from smaller weathering, vadose, piping, wave, fracture and primary-tufa cavities. The geological map on p.22 is generalized, with a 10 km scale; its north arrow and stratigraphic legend are retained in the private render.

Pp.15–16 explain that much cave formation preceded present surface hydrology, with confined aquifers and bedding/structure influencing morphology. P.18 discusses erosion and roof collapse that expose or modify mouths and sediment accumulation that can ultimately fill caves. The Umm Qatfa example concerns roughly half a million years and cannot establish a retreat rate for a Qumran mouth over two millennia. Pp.18–20 distinguish formation of the cavity from dating a sediment deposited in it; a deposit's age can be much younger. The illustrations document specific geological examples, not contemporary entrance floors at the three entry-25 candidates.

**Project inference:** collapse, mouth retreat, infilling, excavation and later use are mechanisms that must be checked at each candidate. These regional mechanisms cannot justify an arbitrary reconstructed ancient mouth, missing entrance, depth origin or wall date. Shared karst morphology has no demonstrated discriminatory value for the entry-25 comparison in this batch.

### Archaeological history

Davidovich, Porat and Ullman, *Archaeology and history*, pp.23–37/viewers6–20 of the supplied introduction file, was read completely, including the chronological table at p.37. Frontmatter/viewers1–5 is title, rights and contents, rather than the separate atlas general introduction pp.1–2.

P.28/viewer11 explicitly lists **XII/53**, citing **Cohen and Israel 2002**, among the caves with Early Chalcolithic/Wadi Rabah material, alongside XI/10 and Qumran24. This is a secondary reference to an excavation campaign already in the project. It supplies no ancient entrance datum, no date for XII/53's wall, and no independent Roman-phase evidence. The reference should link to the existing Cohen/Israel record rather than create duplicate corroboration.

Pp.23–24 describe function assessment through physical setting, access, spatial form, material evidence and written sources. The chronological overview distinguishes accessible pastoral/storage caves from refuge and other uses, with many case interpretations explicitly tentative. Pp.28–30 discuss the limited diagnostic evidence behind some prehistoric period assignments. P.32 describes difficult-access refuge caves that could receive rock cutting or water installations; that does not assign such a phase to any target's present architecture. P.34 synthesizes First Revolt and Bar Kokhba evidence, with different typical access/assemblage patterns; its regional counts are 2015 synthesis estimates, not a new complete modern inventory or an equal-opportunity control set.

P.36/viewer19 specifically reports stone walls installed for Bedouin storage in Upper Tekoa caves in the early twentieth century. **Project inference:** a published modern example reinforces the need to date individual walls independently; its date cannot be exported to Twin, IV/17 or XII/53. The reproduced Copper Scroll discovery photograph on p.32 is an object-context illustration, not a high-resolution view of XII10, and was not used to reopen the reserved manuscript-reading test.

### Research campaigns and precise retrieval routes

Shivti'el, Davidovich and Porat, *History of research*, pp.42–47/viewers1–6, was read completely and all appended pages inspected. This chapter is obtained despite the stale index.

- **P.43:** Qumran expedition work in **1952–1956**; an **8 km** escarpment strip contained **230 examined caves**, of which roughly **40** yielded archaeological material. References are **de Vaux1953b,1956,1962** in this chapter. The forty are find-bearing caves, rather than the full number examined. This is historical campaign extent, not a present comprehensive cave inventory or evidence against every unrecorded opening.
- **P.45:** Bar-Adon's **1967–1977** emergency and independent cave work along the escarpment between Nahal Arugot and Qumran; much remained unpublished, with an explicit reference to **Bar-Adon diaries**. This supports the field-diary record class behind the existing Twin archive route. It supplies no diary shelfmark, Twin-specific plan or original measured section.
- **P.45:** Malham established in **1979**; early surveys in Daliya, upper Makukh, upper Tekoa and Yahel combined systematic walking with aerial/opposite-cliff photographs; research appeared in **Niqrot Zurim** in the 1980s–1990s. The page cites Frumkin1988:15–19 for early Ketef Jericho caves. Bibliography entries themselves at atlas pp.431–446 remain outside these files.
- **P.46:** **Patrich1983–1994**, including detailed Qumran/Ein Feshkha escarpment work and five excavated caves; hundreds of caves across northern desert drainages were documented but most material remained unpublished. **Patrich1994** and **Patrich/Arubas1989** identify the publication trail. This does not identify a native target station sheet or its current custodian.
- **P.46:** Operation Scroll **1993**, fifteen survey teams from Netiv HaGdud to Hazezon; many revisited previously investigated caves. **Wexler2002** is the overview route. Repeated examination of the same cave/campaign must be deduplicated.
- **Pp.46–47:** Malham's **1998** oblique aerial photography along the escarpment; **2001–2005** Malham/Bar-Ilan environmental/archaeological survey led by Frumkin/Eshel, field leads Porat/Lisker, principally Darga–Arugot; **2006 onward** surveys of Masada and Har Badar map areas under Davidovich. The chapter cites **Eshel/Porat2009**, **Lisker et al.2007,2009,2010**, **Davidovich2008,2014**. These are retrieval routes and provenance ancestors, not new target geometry.

### Large-cave comparison

Langford and Frumkin, pp.48–52/viewers1–5, was read completely, including the raster comparison panels on pp.51–52. They compare the largest caves, not the entry-25 candidates.

P.48's PEF Chariton map and p.50's PEF HaGai map are attributed to Conder/Kitchener1883. P.49's regional locations map has a 10 km bar and north arrow. P.51 contains a ten-cave length table and a composite of plans shown at one 50 m scale; the caption says all levels are on one plan and the arrow indicates magnetic north. P.52 contains selected developed profiles at a 20 m scale, explicitly projected onto one plane even when they bend and contain multilevel passages. These are suitable morphology/coverage comparanda with projection limits, not true elevation/entrance-bearing maps for the three targets.

The historical accounts document expanding survey coverage: Nahal Qina from a roughly90 m known passage to1,411 m after a second opening was found in2013 (p.49); Sela from600 m to1,200 m after accessing an elevated internal window (p.49); Arak en-Naasane from310 m mapped to1,150 m in2012 (pp.49–50); Makukh expanded from roughly520 m after later campaigns and another opening (p.50). These examples cannot establish missing passages at a different cave. They do show why incompleteness must remain explicit rather than treating an old mapped extent as an exhaustive absence result. Cave-length totals are cumulative passages, not a straight entrance-to-target distance.
