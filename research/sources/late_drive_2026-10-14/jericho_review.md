## Abel: Atara and Nasbeh, printed141–143 / PDF146–148

On143, Abel discusses the Montjoye establishment near Scopus/Shaʿfat, citing Guérin *Judée* **I395** for a church, monastery, well and birkeh. **That hydraulic mention belongs to Shaʿfat, not Atara.** The next paragraph identifies Cannasaba with Khan en-Nasbeh. Neither passage establishes an Atara reservoir, outlet, bottom channel, flow direction or shared construction phase. The medieval settlement claim cannot date a particular reservoir or masonry contact.

The existing Atara record already has Guérin III6–7’s two reservoirs/partition, Alt’s spring interpretation and gallery, and Grant’s conduit fragments. ## Mallon: Ghor survey, printed55–62 / PDF60–67 and159–163 / PDF168–172

Both articles and their footnotes read completely in the supplied original; p55 map visually inspected at full page. They mix observed remains, historical geography and author interpretation.

On56, Mallon records three springs in the southern eastern Ghor. At ʿAin ʿArus he describes remains of a built basin, **apparently** Arab-period, using stones from ed-Deir. The construction period and spolia identification are his interpretations; no sealed context, section, wall contact or invert is published. On57, ʿAin Sueimeh’s water is described as captured by local Ghawarneh and conducted across the softened banks of Wadi ʿAdeimeh to fields. This documents a then-current channel in the eastern Ghor, not an Atara hydraulic connection. The p55 diagram has a kilometre scale, routes and wadis; it has no pool/channel section or candidate datum.

On58–59, surface materials at Tell Iktanu are reported as mixed, ranging from early Bronze through the author’s Jewish-period attribution; Mallon reports finding no Roman or Byzantine material. The negative applies to this surface visit, not to a systematic excavation or whole-site exclusion. His argument connecting Iktanu/Shaghur to Zoar is interpretation.

**Internal correction to retain:** on60 Mallon suggests Shaghur as an Arabic equivalent/continuation of Greek Seghor. On160n1 he reports that local residents use *saghur* for the mill aqueduct, individually or in series, and that the same name occurs at Nablus. He explicitly allows accidental phonetic resemblance rather than descent from Seghor. A name-association database must carry this qualification; the earlier passage alone would overstate continuity. This is not a Copper Scroll toponym identification.

On160 Mallon records about a dozen ruined mills in series on Wadi Hesban’s southern bank, broadly labelled Roman, Byzantine and Arab. The broad labels do not individually date any conduit or mill and supply no first-century joint-use observation.

On161–163, the separate subsection “Tell Ras el ʿAin” records Mallon and Neuville’s15February1931 visit to a promontory immediately west of ʿAin Dug and ʿAin Nuʿeimeh. Surface walls, pottery and flints support their Bronze I–II attribution. Flint drawings appear on162. Scattered comparable material occurs on the eastern plateau near the source and southern rising slopes; megalithic monuments and Mousterian elements before caves are noted on163 along Wadi Siday. **No surveyed cave plan, ancient entrance bearing/threshold, first-century phase, pit-mouth/grave conjunction or north-of-Tell location is established.** Do not turn the Ras el ʿAin place-name into a location at ʿAin es-Sultan.

## Kenyon 1954: entire article, bibliography and plates

All11 supplied PDF pages read; fig.1 and plates I–II visually inspected. The opening103 states the article’s Neolithic scope. Its spring description establishes the author’s geographic context, not the construction date of the spring-house or a pool east of the tell.

On107, a rectangular plaster basin lies within the building interpreted as a possible sanctuary below the Neolithic wall, with scorched basin/floor. This is an authorial functional interpretation in a Neolithic context, not a Roman water installation. On108, the approximately30 individuals beneath house floors and the plastered skulls also belong to the pre-pottery Neolithic sequence. On109, large irregular rubble-filled pits in pottery-Neolithic levels are interpreted as extraction pits for brick material. Their forms or proximity to earlier burials cannot be transferred into entry60’s first-century pit/grave requirement.

**Figure-only later feature:** printed104 fig.1’s upper-right is labelled “Byzantine rubble-filled pit.” This qualifies any blanket assertion that all pits depicted are Neolithic. The figure is a W–E section of **Trench I**, accompanied by a schematic section from the western edge toward the centre of the tell and a line1m north of TrenchI. Its lower skeleton annotations belong to pre-pottery Neolithic layers. It supplies neither a located pit opening north of the tell nor contemporaneous mouth/grave access. No numerical geometry or phase link was inferred from it.

On109, Kenyon describes bedrock sloping toward the spring and the tell’s accumulated occupation deposits. This is topographic/formation context, not an ancient pool date. On110, the radiocarbon values and chronological estimates are those available in1954; no modern recalibration was attempted. The bibliography points to Garstang1935/1936 and Zeuner1954. Those target works were not opened through these references.

Relative to the already read Kenyon II–III record, this article does not resolve the uncertain absolute date of pits truncating first-century graves, the mouth relationship, or immediate north-strip coverage. Its excavation lineage does not become independent corroboration merely because this shorter publication is newly available.
