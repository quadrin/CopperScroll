## What changes

This source adds a measured architectural comparison for a rock-cut bath predating a later public building, and an adjacent concealed passage system. It does not resolve Atara's pool/channel relationship, entry 25's specific cave geometry, Hyrcania's measuring origin or Siloam's main-basin conduit junction. No Copper Scroll location identification follows from the source.

The chronological control matters: the excavators place the synagogue's first phase in the second half of the fourth century CE, based on coins, ceramics and glass; its substantial rebuild falls in the late fifth or early sixth century (p. 119). An earlier mikveh physically constrained the synagogue's construction (pp. 118–119). Its origin in the Second Temple settlement is explicitly a conjecture, rather than a dated excavation result (p. 122). The cave/passage section supplies no independent construction date. This source therefore supports a reuse/geometry comparison and cannot establish a first-century synagogue or treasure concealment.

## Page-anchored findings

- **115–116, location and campaign.** Site on the northern slope of Tel Maon, south of Carmel. Preliminary identification in 1987 led to excavations in 1987–1988. Modern construction reused ancient building stones, dwelling caves and cisterns. These are campaign context, not independent corroborating datasets.
- **117, observed synagogue envelope and entrances.** Long axis south–north, toward Jerusalem; external dimensions 15.5 × 10.5 m. Exterior wall construction uses large dressed stones, inner wall faces smaller stones in mortar; wall thickness 0.90–1.00 m. Eastern main entrance measured 1.5 m wide; a second entrance south of it measured 1 m and lay about 1 m higher owing to the rock slope, reached via rock-cut steps and a bench-stone. The two-stage plan is illustrated with a 5 m scale. Differing entrance levels are explicitly explained by terrain.
- **118–119, physical sequence.** A salient rock remnant in the southwest of the hall could not simply be removed because an earlier underground ritual bath lay below. The bedrock was incorporated into seating/structure. Excavators infer first synagogue phase second half fourth century, then a late fifth/early sixth-century basilical rebuild. The original single hall with benches acquired internal supports; the envelope largely persisted.
- **121, mikveh observed form.** Below the hall's southwestern portion, entered from outside along its western wall. Two rock-cut rooms: western entrance/changing room, 4 × 2 m and about 2 m high, with grey plaster traces up the walls; bathing chamber 4 × 3 m and 3 m deep. Passage between rooms 1.5 m wide. Low limestone-slab partition, 0.30 m high, with an opening. A rock-cut depression outside the partition is interpreted as a sediment trap for bathers to rinse their feet before entering. Across the bath are four broad steps, varying in size and narrowing toward the bottom.
- **121–122, function and water.** Side-room round pit, about 1 m deep, is interpreted as possibly for bathers' clothes; wall niches possibly held lamps. The bath's water level was about 1.25 m above the bottom; capacity at that level about 9 m³. Authors compare this to roughly ten times the halakhic minimum, and infer communal use. Rainwater was collected from the roof and led through rock-cut channels via the entrance room; a collection-channel segment was found outside the building's southwest corner. Two plaster phases were seen in the bathing room, with the later plaster preserved to approximately 2 m above the bottom. Roof collapse and plaster traces establish repairs, without a securely dated first construction horizon.
- **122, conjectured earlier date.** The authors suggest that the older mikveh belonged to the Second Temple settlement and was repaired when a synagogue was built during renewed Jewish settlement in the fourth century. Keep this hypothesis separate from the observed priority of the bath to the synagogue.
- **122, concealment/escape system observed geometry.** Near the mikveh entrance, at the base of the north end of the western wall, a narrow, low opening leads into a rock-cut system. Initial elongated room about 8 × 3 m. Its northern side showed earth/stone collapse including large dressed blocks possibly from the synagogue. In the southwest corner a passage entrance 0.60–1.00 m wide. About 2.5 m from the entrance the passage changes course through a diagonal stepped opening, continuing at a level 2 m lower, generally southward. About 20 m were traced, with acute bends and straight stretches 1.5–3.0 m long. Continuation blocked by collapse. Author infers a possible exit; that exit was not observed or measured.
- **122, finds and limits.** Much pottery came from collapse in the mikveh; authors think vessels fell or were thrown from the synagogue, chiefly reflecting its second phase. Fourth/fifth-century jars and Byzantine lamps predominate; Late Roman Ware noted. Metal assemblage includes nails, bronze rings possibly belonging to glass-lamp suspension, a bronze needle and unidentified small copper/metal objects. Coins are sparse: a Valentinian coin dated 364–376 and small coins attributed to the first building stage in the second half of the fourth century. No hoard or treasury is reported.
- **123–125, marble menorah.** Numerous fragments came from collapse, without a recovered original position. Reconstruction, dimensions and installation proposals are separated in the article from fragments actually recovered. Authors consider lion-head supports and connection to bronze fittings; parts of the base remain conjectural. This is late public-building cult furniture, not evidence of hidden treasure.
