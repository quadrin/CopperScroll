# Netzer 1981: full reading of the supplied scan

Obtained: all 154 supplied viewer pages, the 139 numbered illustrations, all 13 plates and their catalogues, notes and three-page abbreviation list. No supplied page or useful graphic remains unread. Printed 132 is absent, probably a blank verso; that physical leaf was not inspected. The printed 117 footer is partly cropped. Source bytes and SHA-256 are in `source_manifest.json`; every viewer page is recorded in `page_coverage.csv`.

This is *Greater Herodium*, Qedem 13, by Ehud Netzer, with Rachel Bar-Nathan, Rivka Birger, Ya'akov Meshorer, Joseph Naveh and Silvia Rozenberg. Its preface is dated December 1981. It combines 1972/1973/1978 excavation reporting, subsequent observations and material adapted from Netzer's 1977 dissertation. Earlier plans, the thesis and related 1980 publications are overlapping evidence, not separate discoveries.

The six chapters cover the project and recording system (printed 1–4), early exploration (5–9), Lower Herodium's installations and buildings (10–53), specialist finds reports (54–78), mountain palace-fortress structural analysis (79–101), and functions/planning/comparisons (102–110). Plates follow at 111–131, notes at 133–144 and abbreviations at 145–147. Sequential full text was consumed with native checks of every useful figure, plate and table. Native pages also resolved the inscription vocabulary, printed coin-date error and several captions absent from OCR.

The numbered hiding-place labels are a useful storage-language comparison. At printed 71/viewer 79, Naveh tentatively reads two Hebrew ink inscriptions on bell-shaped jar fragments from service building B20. The proposed translations designate hiding places 40 and 35; the first also refers to earth or mud. His printed discussion uses מטמר and the root טמר. Grain contents are inferred from the storeroom context, not demonstrated by residue analysis. This reading does not establish any Copper Scroll location, quantity, author or association. The unrelated B41 ink marks are reported as unreadable (Ill.98); the labels are illustrated separately in Ill.99. These are jar photographs, not protected Copper Scroll manuscript images.

Useful installation evidence retains substantial uncertainty:

- The large pool B101 measures 69.2×45.5 m; an aqueduct-to-pool inlet contact was not located (printed 11/viewer 19, Ill.9). Its island piers and the later niched room B42 have different construction relationships. The proposed tholos is a restoration (printed 13–14,28; Ills.20,47), and the room's pigeon-raising function remains tentative.
- Channels and pools B40/B58 were built above fill covering the destroyed B52 hall. The local sequence is observed; an Urtas supply connection is inferred. B46's 1.1 m-square settling-pool interpretation is tentative, and its bottom was not reached at 2 m excavation depth (printed 26–27/viewer 34–35).
- C11/2 is a stepped plastered pool 3.6×2 m,2 m deep, with six steps; adjacent C35 is 3.5×1.4 m and 2.7 m deep. Only C35's western edge reached the floor, above about 40 cm of pottery. The frigidarium/ritual-bath alternatives and missing divider are explicitly uncertain (printed 47–49/viewer 55–57, Ills.77–79).
- The lower mountain cisterns are approximately 30×5 m,25×4 m and 15×3 m, the first two divided into halls. Their original heights are unknown. Combined capacity of 3000 m³ is calculated, not an independently measured original volume. The intermediate cistern is 9×6 m, with a 13×2 m corridor and a round raising-hole below the court; proposed water transport and pack-animal supplementation are interpretations (printed 141/viewer 148, notesV29–30; Ill.116).
- Cistern entrance corridors and blocked windows inform the artificial-fill sequence, but the author says a section is needed to certify that sequence (printed 90,93,96/viewer 98,101,104). Reconstructed upper floors, roofs, tower names and dimensions inferred from Josephus remain distinct from surviving fabric.
- A roughly 3×3 m burial cave with about 10 loculi lies near the northern area's northeast corner. It was rubbish-filled and still unstudied in this report (printed 143/viewer 150, noteV86). Two Byzantine course houses each had a pear-shaped cistern (printed 134/viewer 141, noteIII50). Neither report supplies a measured tomb–mouth relation satisfying the project's frozen image test.

Dating requires context-specific handling. Pottery and coins predominantly date occupation, floors or dumps. Limited founding-period pottery and architectural unity underpin the author's 23–15 BCE construction argument. Northern wing and pool chronology is partly transferred by typological similarity to the better-contextualized service building, not by independent absolute dating (printed 69–70/viewer 77–78). The 48 CE earthquake explanation has explicit alternatives (printed 134/viewer 141, noteIII22). Lower-floor vessels along W30 can be later intrusion; starred plate items are labelled disturbed (printed 135/viewer 142, noteIV3). Chemical analysis of jar salts was inconclusive (printed 134/viewer 141, noteIII13).

Two source discrepancies remain visible. Printed 75/viewer 83 gives Agrippa I's regnal year 6 as AD 2 despite the same heading dating his reign 37–44; native inspection confirms that the error is in the print. Ill.138's caption and diagram show four Jericho axes, while noteVI26 at printed 144/viewer 151 enumerates three. Neither discrepancy was silently repaired.
