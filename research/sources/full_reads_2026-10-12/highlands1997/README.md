## Reading scope

The early chapters describe the 1980–87 survey, ceramic classification and the region’s geology, hydrology, vegetation, geographic units and historical economy. Their comparators include modern agriculture, census and tax records, not a direct ancient population census. The two-volume site catalogue follows at printed 131–849, interrupted by Volume II’s front matter. It ends with complete references at 850–856. Chapter 9 publishes the prehistoric survey at 857–881, followed by site-name and period indexes and final leaves. ## Findings that change how the catalogue should be used

**The survey has uneven detection and dating limits.** Selected terrain was walked, while modern construction, cultivation and vegetation restrict many sites. Several villages were never surveyed by this team. Catalogue fields may come from Porat, Kallai, earlier collectors, Mandate files or written history rather than a fresh assemblage. Large ruin extent, ceramic scatter, building heap and a period-specific settlement area are different measurements.

**Water installations often lack construction dates.** Menuha chalk can permit unplastered cisterns (printed 82/PDF 100), so plaster absence is not a universal age/function criterion. Jaryut’s covered conduit/reservoir (325/344), Ras Alam’s cut reservoir and subterranean vaults (372/391), Ein es-Sawiya’s vaulted water passage (637/672), and Tana et-Tahta’s stepped cistern (845/880) retain source-specific dimensions or forms, with no automatic date transferred from nearby pottery. Byzantine monastery, crypt, bath, fort and press labels remain interpretations where the author does not supply direct evidence.

**Counts and forms do not identify a unique feature.** Beit ʿIweis has 15–20 pit apertures with multiple openings per pit and a columbarium cut into one (211/230). Aperture count is not pit count. Hablata has nine shallow circular cuttings linked to small basins, interpreted as probable winepresses (384/403). The round-shaft cemeteries frequently depend on morphology or robbery spoil rather than a sealed burial date. One entry explicitly asks whether openings are cisterns or shaft tombs (778/813).

**The publication preserves unresolved inconsistencies.** At printed 668/PDF 703 the EB table and following inclusive-count prose conflict. Sheikh Mazar’s EB II–III total includes EB I (794/829), so those rows cannot be added as independent totals. Muntar’s blank MB count derives from a separately cited Spanier collection. Other entries explicitly question whether architecture and pottery are contemporary or whether an early sherd was transported from an adjacent site.

**Chapter 9 is a selected prehistoric survey.** Barkai, Gopher and Friedmann publish Prausnitz/Friedmann’s 1967–68 field records, with finds studied in 1994–95 (857/892). Western perennial-water wadis were selected; interfluves and land east of grid 170 were not walked. Wadi Qanah cupmarks/mortars have no direct cut date (858/893), and proposed in-situ cave/terrace deposits remain unexcavated inferences. Its Ein Fawwar and Ein Sheikh Sultan sites are located by the printed coordinates and are distinct from the Jericho candidates.

## Provenance and limits

The bibliography, English source summaries, online survey records and period indexes repeat earlier field evidence. Their appearance here is no additional independent archaeological observation. The table headings Hell/Rom and Hell-Rom were retained separately, with blank and letter size codes kept as printed. The final indexes were read row by row; they do not supply new construction phases.

No protected manuscript photograph or Tell es-Sultan north-strip target was encountered. Public reproduction rights are unestablished, so copyrighted figures are represented by retrieval/access records. Construction, cutting, occupation, fill, disturbance and modern reuse retain separate dates.
