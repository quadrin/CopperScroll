## Synthesis by part

**Arrangement of the necropolis, printed 1–13 (PDF 15–27).** The authors compile over 900 known caves within a mapped 42-square-kilometre area, with substantial destruction and uneven archaeological coverage. Their geology table, earlier distance sample and geographical quadrant table have different denominators (printed 2,9,10); they cannot be combined into one census. Roads, lower aqueduct, farms, quarries, terraces, gardens and burial installations shared the urban periphery. Proposed aqueduct dates and burial-purity rules are interpreted alongside archaeological exceptions. The distribution maps are useful locators and exploratory context.

**Caves and their components, printed 15–47 (PDF 29–61).** Courtyards, vestibules, entrances, closure slabs, stone doors, loculi, arcosolia, benches, collecting niches and standing pits are distinguished. Decorative facade types have overlapping dates. A useful measurement warning appears at 27–29/PDF 41–43: competing cubit/foot values are discussed, but even one complex does not establish a uniform unit. Drainage interpretations require caution; a sloping floor is not proof of intended drainage. Central standing pits typically facilitate handling bodies, while secondary collecting pits and lower ossuary repositories may belong to later alterations (printed 30–44). Literary terms and excavators' function labels remain separate evidence.

**Burial practice and objects, printed 49–65 (PDF 63–79).** Primary burial, decomposed-bone collection and ossuary use have distinct histories. Individual inscriptions may accompany multiple persons or family remains; a named ossuary does not establish every skeleton's identity. Textual practices concerning shrouds, oil, wine and mourning are compared with finds without treating them as uniformly observed. Lamp soot and positions support practical lighting, while vessel functions and beliefs remain debated. The authors' counts of recorded and preserved ossuaries have collection and loss limits (printed 52). Pottery, coins and glass often date a use or reuse event rather than the original cutting.

**Earlier and later burial, printed 67–69 (PDF 81–83).** Older bench tombs, new Second Temple graves and later Roman/Byzantine reuse overlap. Post 70 objects can occupy one part of a cave containing intact earlier remains. Long-lived forms provide broad dates, and communal or sectarian attribution requires evidence beyond a burial type.

**Catalogue, printed 71–309 (PDF 85–323, with 165 absent).** The corpus moves from Mount Scopus and the Mount of Olives through Kidron, Hinnom, the southern/eastern suburbs, western districts, Sanhedria, Eshkol, Givat Mivtar and French Hill, then intramural and north-wall contexts. Entries preserve survey, rescue excavation, archive publication, unexcavated, blocked, unfinished, destroyed and lost conditions. These are uneven detection opportunities. Plans differentiate recorded rock, later alterations and reconstruction; the source's scale, north and datum conventions must be checked for each proposed measurement.

Several exact-page examples matter for later work:

- Tombs of Kings, printed 133–136/PDF 147–150, combines recorded and reconstructed parts. A sarcophagus's queen-name variants do not directly identify Helene. Little Sanhedrin, printed 137–138/PDF 151–152, has an actual plan/count differing from a 23-place tradition, with comparative rather than find-based dating.
- Mid-Kidron, printed 140–149/PDF 154–163, separates Absalom's original southern access from a later western breach, Bene Hezir's inscription from inferred occupants, and Zechariah's solid monument from the associated incomplete cutting hypothesis. Silwan's earlier tombs and later conversions require separate phase arguments.
- Southern shaft cemetery, printed 217–219/PDF 231–233, retains competing late Second Temple/Bar Kokhba dating and later reuse. Qumran/desert comparisons are the authors' comparisons and supply no independent community identification.
- Jason Tomb, printed 241–243/PDF 255–257, preserves courts, portico, loculi, collecting features, ship and menorah engravings. Its chronological synthesis includes coins and earthquake/looting hypotheses; these are distinct from recorded remains. Frieze Tomb, printed 252–256/PDF 266–270, has conflicting historical routes/locations and stylistic dates.
- Sanhedria, printed 257–272/PDF 271–286, includes unfinished cuttings, multi-level collecting/storage features, later connections and unexcavated accesses. The Sanhedrin name does not identify the occupants. Wealthy-family and dating claims remain author interpretations.
- Abba Cave, printed 290–291/PDF 304–305, distinguishes its Aramaic inscription from the collecting-pit bones. The authors report anthropological grounds against identifying the remains as Matatyah Antigonus. Umm al-Amad, printed 291–292/PDF 305–306, combines recorded rock and Avigad's reconstruction; its architectural comparison is a dating argument.
- French Hill, printed 293–305/PDF 307–319, records destruction, doubtful duplicate registration, lost caves and unpublished rescues. The interchange exposure on 304 distinguishes excavated caves from those preserved by a route change. Named families and generation estimates are inferences from inscriptions and assemblages.
- Intramural/north-wall contexts, printed 305–309/PDF 319–323, separate cutting, burial use, later urban enclosure and clearance. Christian lamps/coins date particular cists; the authors warn against extending that date to every nearby cist. Ossuary fragments can reflect redeposition or stoneworking.
