## McCown: observations and qualifications

The opening literature review separates rival Mizpah identifications, their textual emendations, topographical assumptions and inherited observations (printed pp. 13–49 / PDF 37–73). It favours Tell en-Nasbeh cumulatively, while acknowledging unresolved archaeology at alternatives. The archaeological synthesis distinguishes evidence on the tell from neighbouring tombs and other sites (pp. 50–63 / PDF 74–87). Its historical and ceramic dates are the publication's 1947 assessments, not independently updated chronology.

The tomb chapters explicitly record robbery, rebuilding, mixed deposits and unequal recording between excavation seasons (pp. 67–124 / PDF 91–148). Early objects in a reused tomb do not date its cutting. T56 has separate entrances and a court drain; the native plan is Fig. 16, not the erroneous OCR reading “36” (p. 113 / PDF 137; plate 23 / PDF 381). T33's suggested early-fourth-century use rests on external lamp comparisons rather than a local dated coin; its mixed fill and shaft cement do not date first cutting (pp. 115–118 / PDF 139–142).

Wampler distinguishes waterproofed cisterns from silos and dates pottery groups with explicit uncertainty (pp. 128–147 / PDF 152–171). Cistern 176 lacks a published plan and contains a suspicious late coin; that coin cannot securely date use. Cistern 183 has separate proposed fill/use groups. Silo 295's three mouths connect below, possibly through accidental intersection. Cistern 368's joined bottle-shaped cavities and masonry divider constitute one installation, with accidental joining among the explanations. These are feature identities and proposed episodes, not interchangeable counts of chambers or cisterns.

Cistern 363 preserves covers, a floor drain and a probable contemporaneous relation to rooms 617/605 (pp. 136–137 / PDF 160–161; plate 45:1 / PDF 403). Plate 45:2 shows multiple plaster layers in Cistern 159; the caption interprets them as building/repair and use phases. Plate 45:3 shows the two openings of Cistern 368. A survey elevation or pottery-group date must not become a deposit depth or original cutting date.

The building chapter supplies a particularly useful chronological qualification: Cistern 320 and Silos 317/321 were closed before the low colonnade in rooms 389/390 (p. 213 / PDF 237, Fig. 53A; plate 77 / PDF 435). They cannot be treated as active water/storage features contemporary with those pillars on that evidence. In Building 3, Cistern 325 probably predates the added room 267, and the stair is also an addition (pp. 207–212 / PDF 231–236, Fig. 51; plate 75 / PDF 433). Room 331's stair may postdate its pillars (p. 214 / PDF 238, Fig. 53B; plate 78:2 / PDF 436). Spatial proximity is insufficient to join these into one entrance–pillar–cistern phase.

The hypothetical early gate was proposed after excavation without the targeted search needed to establish it. McCown and Wampler differ over whether the observed architecture represents separated gate periods or changes within one construction episode (pp. 198–201 / PDF 222–225, Figs. 47–48). The apparent standing-stone association is explicitly modern: the expedition placed the surface stone beside the proposed gate. Plate 73:2 shows its absence and 73:3 shows it reset on a later dividing wall (p. 244 / PDF 268; plate 73 / PDF 431). The photograph therefore cannot independently establish an ancient stone–gate relation.

Stratigraphic and industrial chapters repeatedly distinguish transported sherds, rebuilding and reused equipment (pp. 179–186, 221–232, 249–264 / PDF 203–210, 245–256, 273–288). Room 409's Greek fragment below a floor qualifies the room's earlier pottery assignment. Vats reused on edge in later walls are separate from active industrial equipment. Winepress A lacks a date; the small group from B supports only the author's qualified pottery-period proposal. Missing field descriptions limit the published press plans (pp. 255–258 / PDF 279–282, Figs. 67–68; plates 97/99 / PDF 455/457).

The inscription chapter retains disputed readings and uncertain object contexts (pp. 156–174 / PDF 180–198). The MSH/MSP stamp's final letter remains unresolved in the source; contextual plausibility cannot settle it. Initial cuneiform readers later exchanged opinions, so their final agreement is not three independent observations. Catalogue copies, figures and later summaries inherit the original excavation ancestry.

The remaining artefact chapters, appendices A–G, object crosswalks and plate descriptions were read in full (pp. 265–303 / PDF 289–327). Harrison notes that only about 70 percent of the beads were available for study and that the bracelet/anklet classification was not documented (pp. 267–269 / PDF 291–293). Fink's treatment of the gilded iron object did not recover its original shape (p. 274 / PDF 298). The dagger's chemical results and changing hardness interpretation do not establish its manufacturing history or archaeological date (pp. 277–279 / PDF 301–303). The coin appendix is a partial list identified from photographs; its mint/ruler qualifications and duplicated object number require preservation rather than silent reconciliation (p. 275 / PDF 299, plate 102 / PDF 460).

Two internal catalogue discrepancies remain visible: plate 57:19's M2400 versus body text M2420, and plate 57:26's Cistern 3 versus body text Cistern 33 (plate descriptions p. 297 / PDF 321; plate 57 / PDF 415; body pp. 165, 170–171 / PDF 189, 194–195). These warrant object-record verification before reuse. The addenda revise a Greek-pottery attribution/location and add comparisons without supplying new installation dates (p. 304 / PDF 328). Index references include Vol. II, which was not supplied. The final survey map (PDF 471) was inspected in full and enlarged quadrants: it preserves grid, north arrow, scale and loci, but is an aggregate survey rather than a single-phase construction plan. Faint labels are not reconstructed.

## Pritchard: three installations and uncertain bearings

The rectangular reservoir northeast of the mound, the small western stepped pool and the large circular cut are separate features (pp. 67–70 / PDF 4–7). The northeast reservoir measures 11.30 × 18.30 m, lies 36 m from the village spring and has four plaster coats. Embedded jars are tentatively first-century BCE; their function is unresolved. The western pool is approximately 2 × 2 m and more than 2 m deep, beside houses with Alexander Jannaeus coins; its supply was unknown. The circular cut has an 11.50 m diameter and 42 steps exposed to 10.51 m, without reaching bottom. Its unplastered walls and absent rim conduits leave competing supply/function explanations.

The 1956 tunnel clearance established 51 m and 93 steps, an external northern mouth, a southwestern spring branch and a southern stepped branch (pp. 71–75 / PDF 8–12, Figs. 4–5). Earlier survey bearings were questioned. Washed-in fill cannot date the cutting; the later Iron II construction proposal depends on structural order and wall contacts. The report supplies neither a modern verified entrance bearing nor proof of continued first-century use of every feature. Its site-name evidence does not establish a Copper Scroll identification.

The supplied page order is unusual: PDF 1 is JSTOR front matter, PDF 2 is printed 66, PDF 3 the cover/printed 65, and PDF 4–12 printed 67–75. The adjoining article begins on PDF 12; only that supplied portion was read.

## Rahmani: distinct access and burial episodes

Jason's Tomb contains Chamber A with ten kokhim and a central pit, plus Chamber B used as a charnel chamber; the reported approximately 25 burials are not 25 intact tombs (pp. 61–67 / PDF 2–12, Figs. 1–3). Robbery, transferred fragments and secondary burial in kokh 9 complicate depositional associations. A column base preceded the raised porch floor and its drainage treatment. The southern forecourt approach is distinct from the internal northern courtyard doorway with west-jamb bolt/socket evidence (pp. 65–69 / PDF 6–14; plates 16–19 / PDF 49–52). Ten niches are not ten chambers, and the report does not demonstrate a single joined cistern beneath the column.

The charcoal ships, later scratched menorahs and reconstructed damaged ship are distinguished in the discussion and plates (pp. 69–75 / PDF 14–20; plates 20–22 / PDF 53–55). The sequence supplies relative order, not an independently dated engraving event. Sailor identity, religious identity and occupation are interpretations.

The complete finds table, pottery discussions and object catalogues preserve room origins, joins, transfer and imperfect minimum-vessel estimates (pp. 76–91 / PDF 21–36). Forty-six procurator coins comprise 42 from kokh 9 and four from courtyard fill; the latest are 30/31 CE (pp. 92–96 / PDF 37–41). Rahmani associates a secondary burial and final blocking with that date. This does not date original cutting. His earlier chronology, proposed robbery in 37 BCE and earthquake association in 31 BCE depend on typology and historical correlation (pp. 97–100 / PDF 42–45).

All supplied plates 13–28 were inspected, including the reconstructed monument and inscription photographs/facsimile. Repeated halves of Fig. 3 are parts of one plan. The separate epigraphic commentaries are cited but absent; reading the illustrated inscriptions does not amount to reading those missing articles or independently recovering their damaged letters.

## Arnona 2008: locus identity and incomplete coverage

The report concerns permit A-4785, May 2006, with Areas A and B about 30 m apart (PDF 1–3). Area A has a quarry, an unknown-function installation L108 and eight individually labelled pits: L101, L103A/B, L104A/B, L106 and L107A/B. L105/L112 denotes one separate, partially cut large feature, approximately 6 × 6.8 m and 2.5 m deep. Its probably deliberate fill includes minority Iron II and majority Early Roman pottery. The nearby lmlk handle was found during mechanical preparation, 7 m east of the quarry, and does not date the cutting (PDF 2; Figs. 3–11 / PDF 5–10).

Area B has three smaller pits, L109/L110/L111A, tentatively associated with an earlier exposed Second Temple building (PDF 2, Fig. 12 / PDF 11). Adding pits across areas or counting both L105 and L112 as separate features would invent a group. The interrupted excavation leaves the large cut's full extent and target-volume coverage unresolved. North arrows, scales and plan levels were inspected; no common original-use phase or left-of-entrance relation is established.

## Arnona 2024: morphology without a secure date or function

Permit A-9163, December 2021, exposed one bell-shaped rock cut, approximately 5.3 m deep, 4 m across at its base and 1 m across its mouth (PDF 1–3). Approximately 3 m of deliberately deposited soil contained stones, modern refuse including a ladder, and nondiagnostic pottery. No plaster survived. The source retains unfinished-cistern, lost-plaster and agricultural explanations; nearby Iron II, Persian and Hellenistic remains do not independently date this cut.

Fig. 2's plan and east–west section retain a north arrow, 2 m scale and L1 levels (PDF 5). They are source-survey information, not a deposit datum. The opening, rough-hewn wall and level bottom were inspected in Figs. 3–5 (PDF 5–7). The linked 3D model remains outside this reading's scope.

## Research consequence

These are exploratory readings of previously selected or exposed sources. They improve source coverage and preserve concrete construction/use/fill distinctions, correction notices and retrieval routes. They supply no registered unseen prediction or regional discrimination. Source-specific observations must be applied with their actual phase, datum and ancestry; any identification test requires its own existing registration procedure.
