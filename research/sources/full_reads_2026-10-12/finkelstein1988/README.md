The most useful methodological finding is the catalogue key on pp. 140–141. A site's listed area describes the whole ruin, water distance is a straight-line distance to the nearest permanent source, and later installations are mostly reserved for the final survey publication. A scatter containing Iron I pottery therefore does not date every cistern, cave, press or wall in the ruin. Entries such as DF7's medieval cave campsite (p. 143), Jibit's burial caves and cisterns (p. 145), NCR41's two winepresses and two cisterns (p. 159), Shuna's pits compared with the Gibeon winery (p. 173), and Deir Daqla's crypt/loculi (p. 175) retain their separate feature-date uncertainty. The lower Beth-Horon Iron I material was reported only from a stored antiquities collection (p. 177).

Plans and reconstructions need particular care. Tell en-Nasbeh's plan combines several periods (pp. 62–63, Fig. 12). Beersheba VII's full perimeter is reconstructed from a limited exposed sector (pp. 242–243, Fig. 79). Mt. Ebal's central remains are separated from Zertal's altar/ramp reconstruction (pp. 82–85, Figs. 24–25). Architectural phases in the typological discussion need not represent a single chronological progression (pp. 237–238).

At Shiloh, earlier pottery in construction fill is distinguished from the later wall/glacis (pp. 212–215), and the separate Iron I fields lack stratigraphic links establishing their contemporaneity (p. 220). Building 335 contains a rock-hewn plastered courtyard cistern (p. 222); the source does not independently date its cutting or plaster. Entrance access and basement steps are inferred where a late wall and Byzantine kiln prevent verification (pp. 222–223). Figure 70 is explicitly an isometric reconstruction. A summit sanctuary is favored through circumstantial and negative evidence, with later damage limiting recovery (pp. 232–234).

Silo and vessel dates also require separate construction, use and deposition records. Silo plans aggregate cuts made throughout a phase, including cuts through other silos (pp. 264–269). The Izbet Sartah ostracon's recovery in silo 605 does not securely attach it to Stratum II (p. 77). Long-lived jars can appear in later destruction deposits after their manufacture ceased (pp. 277, 281). At Shiloh, cultic fragments above collapsed bricks and material in a slope dump have contexts distinct from the destroyed floors (pp. 226–227).

Population and environmental comparisons remain models. Ephraim estimates use 25 persons per dunam and arbitrarily halve early site areas, while omitting archaeologically invisible pastoralists (pp. 193–194). Regional estimates of 40,650 inhabitants at known sites and a corrected maximum near 55,000 include uncertain attribution, coverage corrections and sites occupied during only part of Iron I (pp. 332–335). The water-distance comparison shows broadly similar distributions from Middle Bronze through early/late Iron I, and supplies no date for a specific cistern (pp. 195–196). Modern agriculture, village populations and tent camps are separately dated analogies.
