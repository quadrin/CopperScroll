# Survey of the supplied book archives

1 October 2026 (UTC). Targeted review for CopperScroll, combining earlier reading with new inspection of the remaining supplied volumes.

## Coverage and result

Nine supplied ZIPs contain eight distinct books. All eight have been surveyed through their front matter/contents and selected relevant pages. This is a relevance survey, not a complete reading of every volume. Contact sheets were used for navigation; findings below rely on full-sized page inspection or explicitly identified earlier reading. Extracted but unread pages supply no evidence.

Archive 09 contains 174 scans from archive 02. All 174 corresponding JPEG files were compared byte-for-byte and are identical. It supplies additional packaging, not another source or independent corroboration. Historical download-status statements in its README do not describe the present set of available archives.

The useful additions concern chronological controls for tombs and roads, Jericho's priestly economy, the evidential limits of imperial bath traditions, and a gazetteer retrieval target. No new exact Copper Scroll feature was established. Entry assignments, candidate confidence and atlas geometry remain unchanged.

## Findings by book

### 01: historical geography

Page 119 distinguishes Roman-period Jericho at Tulul Abu el-Alayiq from Tell es-Sultan and modern Jericho. Its irrigation account pairs Wadi Qelt/Ein Fara with a northern Achor identification at Wadi en-Nu'eimeh. This records the author's geographical interpretation; it does not settle the competing Achor/Buqeia traditions or identify a Scroll outlet.

Earlier reading connects Hyrcania with Khirbet Mird and its aqueduct, and upper/lower Beth Horon with Beit Ur. Those connections establish regional search contexts. They provide no measured entry-29 pool, entry-35 gorge/cairn or entry-40 tomb/channel. The road discussion distinguishes route antiquity from later Roman paving.

Appendix pp. 184–185 is an inventory with bibliographic references for tombs, towers, bridges, quarries and water installations in several regional territories. Its individual entries are retrieval leads. Their compressed descriptions do not establish a feature's phase, modern position or Scroll correspondence. The cited surveys remain unread in this pass.

### 02: political, social and cultural history

Earlier Safrai passages describe Roman balsam estates, confiscation after CE 70 and continuing cultivation through tenancy. A change of owner cannot be used as evidence that an irrigation installation was abandoned.

The new regional excerpts describe later settlement and economic conditions. Contents attribute the Judaea/southern-coast third–fourth-century chapter to Joshua Schwartz. Pages 183–185 discuss Lod and display a regional map after Bar Kokhba. Literary accounts, municipal territory and settlement distribution are different forms of evidence. A later city's territory must not become a first-century feature boundary.

These pages help check historical setting and name continuity; they add no measured cache feature. The partial ZIP must not be counted as a second witness to this volume.

### 03: archaeology and art

The strongest earlier contribution is the complete burial chapter. Samaria's courtyard/cistern tomb is E220, dated in the chapter to the third century CE. Beth She'arim plans, later tombs at Mazor and the Mount of Olives, and repeated-use burials provide architectural comparisons with explicit dating limits.

The waterworks chapter distinguishes initial construction, later enlargement and maintenance. Contour-following aqueduct length and straight-line separation measure different things. Neither route length nor a surviving repair can substitute for the Scroll's distance or a candidate's construction date.

See the detailed earlier note for the E220 correction, Beth She'arim figure provenance and excavation-report leads.

### 04: late-antiquity part A

Avni's agriculture chapter differentiates terrace masonry, dated soil deposits and settlement-based chronology. Selected OSL results in the Judaean hills cannot date every terrace or exclude earlier farming. Negev, Petra and Jordan-valley irrigation histories require separate regional sequences. Processing basins can serve grapes or olives; their shape alone does not establish water storage.

Patrich's urban chapter supplies source plans distinguishing excavated paving from reconstructed street routes. Caesarea's successive pipe-fed and water-wheel bath supply, and Jerusalem's later houses, cisterns, walls and processional streets, require installation-level phase separation.

The primary retrieval target is Patrich's 2016 study of water supply to the Caesarea governor's private bathhouse. It was identified bibliographically, not independently read. The urban review was saved before this archive-wide survey.

### 05: late-antiquity part B

Avni, pp. 983–996, updates the burial evidence beyond the older Tsafrir survey. Preservation differs between rock-cut tombs, built graves and trench burials; absence of surviving tomb architecture is not a reliable absence of burial.

Page 992 reports ossuary use continuing into the second–fourth centuries in some districts, including the southern Shephelah/Hebron region and western Galilee. **Project implication:** an ossuary alone does not establish a Second Temple date. The particular tomb, burial deposit and region need their own chronology.

The excerpt also distinguishes church crypts and later urban cemeteries from earlier burial contexts. Beth She'an's later cemetery sequence and Tiberias's multiple cemeteries cannot be assigned wholesale to the Scroll's period. E220 reappears through the earlier Samaria literature; this is a shared source tradition, not a fresh independent excavation.

New primary-report leads, unread here:

- G. Avni, “The Urban Limits of Roman and Byzantine Jerusalem: A View from the Necropolis,” JRA 18 (2005), pp. 373–395.
- G. Avni, U. Dahari and A. Kloner, The Necropolis of Beth Guvrin–Eleutheropolis, IAA Reports 36 (2008).
- Crowfoot, Kenyon and Sukenik, The Buildings of Samaria (1942), figs. 40–41, cited for E220.

Limor, pp. 1113–1118, distinguishes sparse early Christian visits from the broad fourth-century pilgrimage movement and the formation of a Christian sacred landscape. The excerpt discusses Egeria and the Bordeaux traveller (333 CE). These witnesses describe their own later routes and settings. Page 1118 separates itinerary stages and travel infrastructure; stage mileage supplies no local cache distance.

### 06: Jerusalem

Earlier Siloam pages distinguish the source, tunnel-mouth pool, possible overflow pool and later church/pool arrangements. The approximately 16 × 15 m reconstructed basin belongs to a particular historical arrangement. Its dimensions must not be transferred to every feature called Siloam.

The Kidron discussion records Byzantine reuse of Second Temple tombs and later stairway traditions near Jerusalem. Neither supplies the desert gorge-mouth feature sought for entry 35.

Rubin's new pp. 199–200 establish the Byzantine survey's chronological frame and early literary pilgrimage context. They add no new hydraulic plan or first-century feature identification. The original pilgrim texts remain distinct from their modern summaries.

### 07: roads and pilgrimage

New pp. 148–150 describe stepped approaches in the Judaean foothills and refer to survey sites and measurements. The authors explicitly acknowledge that they could not recheck some surveyed segments and cannot confidently identify their functions. Their pilgrimage attribution remains an interpretation.

Pages 186–188 provide useful counterexamples: steps can lead to a local cistern, a cave, a spring, fields or a press. The book distinguishes these local access works from steps on proposed longer pilgrimage routes. It also states the difficulty of determining the exact time at which route steps were cut.

Pages 189–190 advance the authors' religious interpretation and discuss the terms for an ascent and a step. That argument is not independent stratigraphic dating.

**Project implication:** separately record observed steps, their endpoints, the proposed route/function and the evidence for their construction phase. A staircase alone identifies neither a pilgrimage route nor the Scroll's landmark. Earlier Beth Horon and Adummim passages still supply no entry-40 tomb/channel or entry-32 outlet-wall match.

### 08: Ariel

Joshua Schwartz's complete pp. 52–57 article connects Jericho's priestly population, Temple supply and agricultural economy. The Hasmonean palace-complex ritual baths support a discussion of purity practices. The article supplies historical context for Jericho/Qumran questions; it does not locate a treasure, name a Scroll pool or demonstrate a hydraulic correspondence.

Page 57 identifies the article as an abbreviated version of Schwartz, “On Priests and Jericho in the Second Temple Period,” JQR 79 (1988), pp. 23–48. The full paper remains unread. It must not be counted as a second independent witness to the same argument.

Esti Dvorjetski's complete pp. 58–66 article examines Roman emperors and healing baths, including Tiberias/Hammat, Emmaus and Hammat Gader. It treats literary traditions, proposed imperial itineraries, coin evidence and archaeological observations with different evidential status. A reported visit cannot date every bath structure. Later pilgrim descriptions concern later installations or use; they do not supply a Second Temple phase by themselves.

The p. 203 review identifies Tsafrir, Di Segni and Green's Tabula Imperii Romani: Iudaea–Palaestina: 264 pages and five maps, covering roughly 1,250 sites and the period 332 BCE–638 CE. **Only the review was read.** The gazetteer is a strong next retrieval target for historical names and site bibliography; its entries and maps have not yet corroborated any candidate here.

## What this changes in the research workflow

1. Tomb screening needs structure and deposit dates; ossuary presence is insufficient.
2. Route screening needs endpoints, construction phase and the basis of the proposed function.
3. Hydraulic comparisons need a dated installation and a measured plan; regional supply history is insufficient.
4. Repeated summaries and abbreviated articles must be traced to their shared source before counting corroboration.
5. Prioritize original TIR site entries, burial reports and bathhouse supply/phase reports over further broad surveys.
