## 3. Findings per article

### 3.1 Kh. el-Kîliya (Y. Magen, pp. 321–332). This is the main new item.

EVIDENCE (image):
- **Location.** The site is above Wadi el-Wahitah, at map ref. 1826 1487, 5 km east of Kfar Ramon (p. 321). The repo's Koḥlit unit S1831 has 182650/148750, so the two agree. A smaller fortress, "el-Qaṣr" (map ref. 1839 1490, 18.0 × 14.30 m), stands across the wadi. Magen places both on a Roman route from Jericho to Ṭaiyiba (p. 332).
- **Setting.** The complex covers over 2 dunams. Building I is on the north and Building II on the south. To the east are a few caves, a large cistern, hewn steps and agricultural terraces. To the SW and NW are round pens of the Arab period. East of the site, on the slope above the wadi, are a few caves that "might have served as seclusion cells" (p. 321).
- **Plan.** Fig. 2 is the "General plan of the site" (p. 322), with a north arrow (up) and a 0–5 m scale bar. It has spot heights. Building I is an exact square, 20.7 m a side, with a fortified wall on the east and north (p. 322). Building II is 33.60 × 26.70 m (p. 328). Sections 1-1 and 2-2 and a plan and section of the crypt tomb are on p. 324–325, with a scale bar.
- **Cistern in court L-5 (p. 323).** The courtyard is 9.40 × 8.40 m. Its cistern opening is "at its center, slightly to the north". The cistern is "a large cistern, over 10 m. deep" and bell-shaped. Its thick pinkish-white plaster differs from the reddish Byzantine–Arab plaster. Two covered channels, one from the SW and one from the W, carried roof water into it by way of a small settling pool. Two more channels took overflow eastward.
- **Other channels.**
  - L-1: a settling pool in the west wall, and a covered channel under the threshold into the court. This channel is second construction phase (p. 324).
  - L-2: a hewn and plastered channel covered with slabs leads "out to the cistern on the west" (p. 324).
  - L-9: a hewn channel in the north wall carried excess water, not water into the cistern (p. 327).
  - South wall: a hewn and plastered overflow channel that went out of use in the second phase (p. 328).
- **Burial room L-4 (crypt).** The room is 8.40 × 4.40 m, with five burials under the mosaic. Fifteen skeletons lay in the central built and rock-cut tomb, which is 3.86 × 2.60 m and 1.60 m high (pp. 324–327). A monolithic trough-tomb, 2.0 × 0.70 m, "may have been used as a water basin" in the first phase (p. 325). Lamps of the 6th–7th c. came from the crypt (p. 329).
- **Gate.** The gate is 1.10 m wide and had a round rolling stone 32 cm thick, added in the second phase (pp. 323–324).
- **Dating evidence (p. 329).** Most of the pottery is Byzantine. A few pieces are Late Roman (rouletted). Also present are Early Arab ("Kh. el-Mefjar" type) and Mamluk–Ottoman sherds. Coins:
  - one of Pontius Pilatus dated 31 AD;
  - one imperial coin of Ascalon, late 2nd or early 3rd c.;
  - a late Roman coin;
  - Arcadius, Honorius and Theodosius II;
  - the rest probably 5th c.
- **Magen's conclusions (pp. 330–331).**
  - The pre-Byzantine finds show a Late Roman settlement of the 3rd–4th c.
  - It is unclear how the Pilate coin reached the site. The "possible use of the site during the Second Temple period" should not be ignored.
  - He takes the plan and building technique to be a Roman frontier fort with a tower (Building I) and a service wing (Building II).
  - Among his arguments he lists "the white plaster coating the cisterns unlike the plaster used during the Byzantine period" (p. 331).
  - In the Byzantine phase the courtyard was roofed with arches, a church was built on the upper storey, and the gutters and court channels into the cistern were added (p. 331).

INFERENCE:
- The excavator gives the L-5 cistern itself no phase directly. By his argument it belongs to the original Roman (Late Roman, 3rd–4th c.) fort. The feeding channels in the courtyard are Byzantine.
- One coin of 31 CE is the only object of the 1st c. CE. The book reports no structure, layer or feature dated before 135 CE.
- The cistern lies at the centre of Building I, only slightly north of the court's centre. It is not north of the site.

### 3.2 Mt Gerizim, Church of Mary Theotokos (Y. Magen, pp. 333–342; L. Di Segni, pp. 342–350)

EVIDENCE (image):
- **Site.** The site is 83 × 100 m. On the south is a square enclosure (71 × 56 m) with an octagonal church at its centre. On the north is an irregular fortified enclosure with "a rectangular building at the center, probably a large water pool", called Bir el-Rasâs (p. 334).
- **Towers.** The southern enclosure has four corner towers of 8 × 8.6 m and one more on the south wall. The gate in the north wall is 8.60 × 9.60 m and has four phases, the later two probably Arab. Walls survive up to 4 m high (originally c. 7 m) (pp. 334–335).
- **Peristyle and cistern.** The peristyle is 39 × 53 m. "To the west of the church there is a large cistern," into which the paved area drained (p. 336). The church is 30 × 37.4 m.
- **Plans.**
  - General plan of the Byzantine enclosure (p. 335): no scale bar or north arrow visible in the photo.
  - Plan of the church and the surrounding wall (p. 337): a scale bar is present but too small to read.
  - Ritmeyer reconstructions of phases 1 and 2 (pp. 338–339): north arrow and a 0–25 m scale.
- **The pool (pp. 338–340).**
  - The northern enclosure has, at its centre, a large structure, "probably a water-pool". It is plastered, has a floor of flat stones, and shows no evidence of a vault.
  - "The pool had a large cistern," with walls south of it.
  - The pool "is not an organic part of the Byzantine construction". Its building and stone-dressing differ from the enclosure.
  - "It is possible that the pool is from a building phase earlier" than the enclosure and church. Magen reconstructs it as an open pool for rain from the church and upper enclosure.
- **Phases (p. 340).** Phase 1 is the church, gate, peristyle and probably the wall, all under Zeno (484). Phase 2 is the northern enclosure, which Magen attributes to Justinian. No remains of an earlier wall were found.
- **Hellenistic (p. 340).** Magen reports a Hellenistic town around the church site. (paraphrase) a sounding in the centre of the church found "remains of a Hellenistic construction" He proposes that the Samaritan holy place was in the church area (pp. 340–341).
- **Inscriptions (Di Segni).**
  - Six inscribed slabs were found, all reused. Two were in later floors and four in the debris (p. 340). Most were pierced as door counterweights (p. 343, OCR).
  - Inscription 3 is "likely to be a Samaritan dedication" from before the Christian takeover (p. 346).
  - Inscription 4's letter-shapes are "consistent with a dating in the fourth century" (p. 346).
  - Conclusion (p. 348): at least five of the six have a Samaritan background. They come "either from the phase preceding the building of the church" or from converted Samaritans. The former is "more likely".

INFERENCE:
- The book dates nothing on the summit to the 1st c. BCE–2nd c. CE. The pre-church evidence is Hellenistic (construction in a sounding, period not narrowed) and 4th-century CE (inscriptions). The pool is undated and "possibly" earlier than 484.
- This **confirms** the JSP 8 position that later use is Late Roman/Byzantine. It **adds** two items JSP 8 may not have:
  1. a Hellenistic construction directly under the church;
  2. an undated, possibly pre-Byzantine pool with a large cistern (Bir el-Rasâs) in the northern enclosure, plus a Byzantine-phase cistern west of the church.
- Neither item is a dated in-window feature, so neither reopens Q47 by itself.
- I did not open the entry 57 record or the JSP 8 note (column XII exposure rule), so I cannot say whether these features are already recorded there.

### 3.3 Herodion, Byzantine churches (E. Netzer, pp. 165–176)

EVIDENCE (image):
- **Fig. 1, general plan (p. 165).** North arrow points right; scale 100 m. It marks the churches (1–3 in Lower Herodium, 4 on the summit) and shows the pool, the long course and the aqueduct line.
- **Summit chapel.** Corbo's 1962–67 excavation on the summit found a chapel c. 7.8 × 3.4 m (p. 165).
- **Reuse.** In the 5th c. CE, Lower Herodium's structures were cleared and rebuilt, reused, or burned in limekilns (p. 166).
- **Northern Church.** It is in the mostly unexcavated northern section. The hall is 10.4 × 8.5 m. A plan with a 0–5 m scale is on p. 167.
- **Eastern Church.** It is built over the eastern edge of a large Herodian building, probably the main wing of the palace. The hall is 12.3 × 8.3 m. A plan with a 0–5 m scale is on p. 171. Beneath the baptistery floor "was a (looted) tomb" (p. 171).
- **Central Church.**
  - It adjoins the south of the monumental Herodian building beside the pool complex, in front of a long terrace that Netzer queries as "Herod's funeral ground?" (p. 171).
  - Clear evidence was found of an earlier Byzantine phase under it: either a chapel above a small barrel-vaulted tomb, or a burial room (p. 171).
  - Its walls reuse Herodian ashlars, "perhaps the facade of Herod's burial monument" (p. 171).
- **Summary (p. 175).** Byzantine activity was found inside two huge Herodian galleries, each c. 110 × 10 m, east and west of the pool complex. It was also found near and below the eastern gallery, "named the “earth-dam” by the excavators" (note 7 refers to *Greater Herodium*). The churches date from the mid-5th to the 6th c.

INFERENCE:
- Everything new here is Byzantine reuse. The Herodian features (pool complex, galleries, "earth-dam", monumental building) are already the substance of Netzer 1981 (Qedem 13). Netzer 1981 is the primary source, and this book cites it.
- The only point that touches a ratings basis is the excavators' "earth-dam" label in the Tekoa–Herodium sector (entry 42). This is an excavator function label, given here at second hand.

### 3.4 Martyrius monastery (Magen et al., pp. 92–164). Comparandum only.

EVIDENCE (image):
- **Enclosure.** It is 70.6 (E) × 79 (N) × 77.4 (S) × 65.4 m (W), with a wall c. 70 cm thick (pp. 93–94). Fig. 4 is the ground plan (p. 94), with a north arrow and a 0–20 m scale.
- **Gate.** The north gate in the east wall had a rolling stone 2.5 m in diameter (p. 95). The authors note that rolling stones sealed tombs "during the Second Temple and the Roman periods". They cite Kh. el-Kîliya as a Byzantine parallel.
- **Cave and burials (p. 99).** The North Wing has a cave, not cut for burial, that the authors take to be Martyrius's own cave, later used to bury priests. A room with a cistern at its centre (L. 205) was probably the baptistery.
- **SW wing cistern (p. 104).**
  - The courtyard above it is 22 × 14 m. Below is a cistern 10 m deep with the same length and width.
  - A row of piers carries arches.
  - Channels bring run-off from west of the monastery, through dams that could be opened and closed.
  - A sluice gate takes overflow outside.
  - An Umayyad irrigation system drew on it (p. 105).
- **Water system (pp. 107–108).** The authors estimate storage at 20,000–30,000 m³. Roofs, courtyards and outside areas drained "to six large cisterns", with overflow from one to the next. Rainfall is given as 300–350 mm.
- **Phases (p. 109).** There are three building phases. The first is Martyrius's foundation (small chapel and solitary cave, no enclosure wall). The second is Paul's enlargement around 482. The third is under Genesius in the 3rd quarter of the 6th c. No Second Temple phase is reported in the pages read.

### 3.5 Beit ʿEinun (Beth Anoth), Hebron hills (pp. 275–286). Comparandum only.

EVIDENCE (image):
- **Graveyard and church (pp. 276, 282–283).** The church stands near a graveyard whose rock-cut tombs are "typical of the Second Temple period" and were in use in the 1st–2nd c. AD.
  - The graveyard begins a few metres SW of the church and runs to the hilltop.
  - One unrobbed hilltop tomb held decorated hard-limestone ossuaries with Hebrew and Greek inscriptions naming Jewish and Edomite names.
  - A rock-cut tomb of the same type under the church was refitted as the church crypt. It has two entrances with descending steps "typical of Second Temple tombs" and a central hall of 2.6 × 2.4 m.
- **Plan.** Fig. 2 (p. 276) has a north arrow and a 0–2 m scale.

NOTE: p. 275 (held boundary spread) is where the article begins and probably where the site is located. I did not see it.

### 3.6 Ḥ. Beit Loya oil press; Luzit burial caves (pp. 287–320). Comparanda only.

- **Beit Loya.** Map ref. 1433 1080. The church is c. 500 AD and the oil press is a second-phase annex. Fig. 1 has a 0–15 m scale (p. 287, image).
- **Luzit.** Byzantine cross-shaped burial caves in chalk, c. 8 km north of Beth Govrin (OCR). Cave II (p. 303, image) has a rolling-stone channel at the entrance, arcosolia of 2.80 × 1.80 m, and a 1.20 m-high entrance with three steps.
- Neither site is near any project candidate.
