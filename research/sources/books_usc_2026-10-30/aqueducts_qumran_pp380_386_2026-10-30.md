## 3. The reading (EVIDENCE unless marked)

**Introduction (p. 381)**
- Water was essential to the "Essene" practices listed (n. 1, Josephus).
- There are 15 pools and ritual baths, with a capacity of "more than 11,000 m³" (n. 2, Wood, *BASOR* 256, 1984).
- The pools were fed by an aqueduct collecting run-off rainwater. A spring such as ʿEnot Tsukim could not serve, because bathing in drawn water was forbidden.
- The length is "quite short (c.500 m)". The construction techniques are those of the desert fortresses.
- The aqueduct was surveyed during and after de Vaux's excavations (n. 3: Masterman *PEQ* 1903; de Vaux *RB* 63, 1956; Schultz *ZDPV* 76, 1960). The authors made a new survey and soundings "in 1980-82".
- n. 4 gives the survey dates as 7 Dec 1980 (Avshalom Institute students) and 24 Jan 1982 (Kfar ʿEtzion field school guides).
  - n. 4 gives the Hebrew edition's publication year as **1988**. The repo cites the Hebrew chapter as 1989.
  - n. 4 also says de Vaux's 1955 survey, published in Humbert & Chambon 1994, shows "no major differences".
- n. 5 cites Netzer, Masada, pp. 353–64 of this volume.

**Intake and dam (pp. 381–382)**
- The start is at the next-to-last large waterfall of Naḥal Qumran, where the ravine cuts the cliffs on a geological fault. Here a basin (no. 2) is enclosed by cliffs.
- The channel is first visible where a side gorge comes out on the N bank (no. 3). Its base is higher than the bed of the main ravine.
- The authors say there is "no doubt" the aqueduct was originally connected to Wadi Qumran and drew most of its water from it. Wadi Qumran drains the Hyrcania valley (el-Buqeia).
- The link to the side tributary is explained by local cloudbursts on the scarps. Masada is cited as a parallel combination of sources.
- "No remains of an installation survive" (p. 381). Floods are said to have destroyed every built feature in the stream bed.
- Dam: "It seems that a dam was built (fig. 1 no. 4)" (p. 382). It closed the basin; silt settled, and clean water rose and was let out from the dam's N end into the aqueduct.
- n. 6: diversion dams elsewhere are low. Here a "more substantial dam" was needed to overcome the difference in levels, and the water pressure was moderate because the dam stood near the waterfall's foot.
  - n. 6 ends by pointing to "a probable literary reference to this dam" further on (this is the Copper Scroll, p. 386).

**Branches and tunnels (p. 382)**
- From no. 3 the channel runs ESE "for some 40 m" along the N bank, higher than the bed. It is rock-hewn against flood pressure; the built sides later collapsed.
- It then divides:
  - **no. 9** winds south;
  - **no. 8** runs straight to a small shaft and a short tunnel, **5.5 m long (no. 10)**, the "west tunnel" (Fig. 2 "A").
- The no. 8 branch rises toward the shaft. Its function is "not clear". One tentative suggestion is that it carried run-off from the rocks N of the channel back west to the junction.
- "after another 40 m" comes a steep crag (no. 11). A tunnel **13.5 m long** was cut through it: the east tunnel, Fig. 2 "B", Figs. 3–4.
- After the tunnel the channel turns NE, away from the ravine, and runs **c. 130 m** over rocks at the foot of the scarp (pp. 382–383).

**Cliff stretch (p. 383)**
- Much of this stretch is completely destroyed (no. 15).
- The surviving channel has excellent white plaster, which the authors call typical of Second Temple aqueducts. It alternates between rock-hewn and built sections. Built sections stand on a supporting wall of large blocks (Fig. 5a–b).
- Cover slabs survive at two places (nos. 12–13). The authors infer roofing where landslides or rolling stones were a risk.
- At the junction of the cliffs and the plain, opposite Kh. Qumran, the channel turned "at approximately a right-angle (fig. 1 no. 16)".

**Plain and settlement (p. 385; the sentence starts on p. 383)**
- From no. 16 it descends "in a fairly straight line for more than 200 m" to near the settlement. There it turns slightly north and then SE (no. 19) before descending to the settlement's edge.
- The **low dam** at the last turn is quoted in §4.
- The construction depends on terrain:
  - in rocky terrain the channel is rock-hewn, or built and thickly plastered;
  - on the plain it is a simple channel, with a floor of flat river pebbles and larger side stones set in the ground and protruding **c. 15–20 cm** (Fig. 6).
- At the settlement:
  - a shallow depression marks the settling point;
  - a plastered chute leads to a distribution installation with partitions;
  - the aqueduct then descends a pebble-paved incline (against scouring) to the pools (Fig. 7).

**Dating (p. 385, "The date of the system, and literary references")**
- "The first system may belong to the Iron Age fortress (8th-7th c. B.C.)".
  - A round pool W of the Iron Age tower may have been fed by a channel. This channel was "later re-used as the basis for the aqueduct".
  - Iron Age water may have come from the Hyrcania valley.
- The Hasmonean settlers (Alexander Jannaeus, 103–77 B.C.) "presumably built an aqueduct shortly afterwards".
- The visible aqueduct: "dates to the final period (about the time of Herod Archelaus, 4 B.C.-A.D. 6)". The authors allow that it may be "simply a reconstruction" of a Hasmonean aqueduct.
  - n. 7 calls the chronology controversial and agrees with Magness, *DSD* 2.1 (1995) 58–65.
- Evidence of two phases in the pools: the large rectangular pool in the S part was half-filled with débris, including architectural elements, and later the main channel passed over that fill.

**Literary references (pp. 385–386)**
- The aqueduct and "the dam at its inception" may be mentioned in a literary source.
- Most scholars identify the Iron Age fortress with Sekaka (Josh. 15:61, after Beth-Arabah and Middin; n. 8, Eshel, *IEJ* 45, 1995).
- The Copper Scroll (n. 9, Milik, DJD III, 288–89), dated by the authors to the end of the Second Temple period, has "in coming from Jericho to Secacah".
  - The authors infer that the ravine should be called Naḥal Secacah / Gai Secacah (גי סככא).
  - The scroll "also includes the terms" aqueduct (*amat ha-mayyim*) and dam (*yegar*, יגר) (Fig. 8).
- The chapter ends: "The archaeological findings match this evidence well." It is signed Israel Antiquities Authority, Jerusalem (D.A.).

**Figures**
- **Fig. 1 (p. 380):** the plan, with an inset map (lower right), sections A-A to E-E (top left, 0–2 m) and an enlarged plan of tunnel B at point 11 (0–5 m). It has a north arrow and a main scale of 0–50 m. Points 1–19 are described in §5.
- **Fig. 2:** panorama (A = the west tunnel, point 10; B = the east tunnel, point 11).
- **Fig. 3:** "The east tunnel." **Fig. 4:** "The E opening of the east tunnel." **Fig. 5a-b:** "Built and rock-cut sections against the cliffs." **Fig. 6:** "The aqueduct on the plain."
- **Fig. 7:** settlement reconstruction, with water installations labelled "number 2", signed L. Ritmeyer. It is a reconstruction, not a survey.
- **Fig. 8:** see the exposure log. Its caption reads "Verse 22 of the 'Copper Scroll'", the passage on the head of the aqueduct and Secacah from the north. It shows a hand facsimile of four lines with a square-script transcription below, in which restorations are bracketed.

**Measurements printed:** total c. 500 m; c. 40 m from no. 3 to the fork; tunnel no. 10 5.5 m; c. 40 m further to no. 11; tunnel no. 11 13.5 m; c. 130 m of cliff-foot stretch; more than 200 m on the plain; side stones protruding 15–20 cm; pool capacity more than 11,000 m³ (15 installations).

**Not printed:**
- no gradient;
- no channel width or depth in the text (only the drawn sections A-A to E-E at 0–2 m);
- no elevations;
- no grid references;
- no discharge.

INFERENCE: the printed stretches add up to more than about 425 m to near the settlement. This is consistent with "c. 500 m".

Differences seen (EVIDENCE; none affects the numbering):
- **Main scale:** 1989 is labelled 0–10–20–30–40–50 m; 2002 is labelled only at 0 and 50 m.
- **Tunnel-B inset:** the 1989 inset labels two arrows פרצות ("breaches/openings"). No English equivalent is seen in the 2002 inset, which has only the A/B/C section marks.
- **Inset map:**
  - 1989 labels two springs, עינות קומראן and עינות צוקים. 2002 labels only "Einot Tsukim"; the upper dot is unlabelled.
  - The 1989 heading מפת הסביבה ("map of the area") is replaced in 2002 by the caption wording.
  - The other inset names correspond: Hyrcania valley, Kh. Abu Tabaq, Karm es-Samra, Wadi Qumran, The aqueduct, Qalia, Qumran, Dead Sea.

Small new observation on sections (EVIDENCE + INFERENCE):
- D-D is drawn at no. 14, on the cliff stretch: an open U-shaped rock-cut channel.
- E-E is drawn at no. 17, just E of 16 on the plain: a low channel with two side stones. This matches the p. 385 plain description.

- INFERENCE: Stacey's appeal to "sections D-D and E-E" (n. 30) rests on this contrast. E-E lies on his stage-1 stretch (16 eastward) and D-D on his stage-2 stretch. The figure therefore shows a technique change at no. 16, which the authors attribute to terrain.
