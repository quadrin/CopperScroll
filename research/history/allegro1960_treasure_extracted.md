## 0. Conventions and bibliographic notes

- **Tags.** EVIDENCE = the book says it. INFERENCE = my conclusion. BK = background knowledge, not from the book. In the item table (§1), every cell is EVIDENCE unless it is tagged [INF] or [BK].
- **Quotes.** Quotes are verbatim, 12 words or fewer, and in quotation marks. Everything else is paraphrase. Map labels read off the figure images (EVIDENCE) are in *italics*, not quotation marks.
- **Locators.** The translation is on pp. 32–55: a facsimile drawing of each column on the even pages, and the transcription and translation on the facing odd page (Col. I p.33, II p.35, III p.37, IV p.39, V p.41, VI p.43, VII p.45, VIII p.47, IX p.49, X p.51, XI p.53, XII p.55). n.N is Allegro's note N (pp.134–170). Line numbers are Allegro's, counted within each column.
- **Imprint.** EVIDENCE: the reproduced 1960 title page (p.3) carries the imprint "Routledge & Kegan Paul Ltd." (as an image). The reproduced p.4 reads "Library of Congress Catalog Card Number 60–9297", "Printed in the United States of America", "Designed by Diana Klemin" and "First Edition". The 2023 copyright page (p.2) says the book was first published in 1960 by Routledge & Kegan Paul. The word "Doubleday" appears in the book only in the Foreword's citation of *PDSS* (p.7). [BK] Diana Klemin was a Doubleday designer. [INF] The scanned copy is the US-printed first edition, issued with the RKP imprint.
- **Not the 1964 edition.** EVIDENCE: the latest dated works the book cites are from 1959: DSS rev. ed. 1959 (p.7), *PDSS* London 1959 (p.7) and Allegro, PEQ Jan.–June 1959 (n.97, p.145). The Foreword (p.6) says the discovery was "made over seven years ago". [INF] With the Cave 3 find dated 14 March 1952 (p.19), the Foreword was written after March 1959.

**Totals.** EVIDENCE (pp.58–60). By the face value of the text: silver "3,282+ talents, 20 minas, and 4 staters"; gold 1,280 talents; 65+ gold bars; 608 pitchers; 619 vessels. On his talent = maneh reading this comes to silver 21½ cwt and gold 8½ cwt, "about £358,000, or a million United States dollars" (p.60). At full Mishnaic weights it would be silver 65 tons and gold 26 tons (n.8). The Qumran hoard of 558 Tyrian tetradrachmae (about 20 lbs) is offered as a check on scale. ## 2. Identifications by region, with his reasoning in brief

All statements in this section are EVIDENCE unless tagged [INF] or [BK].

**Method.** EVIDENCE: the scribe uses alternative names or synonyms "doubtless for purposes of secrecy" (p.70). What matters is where first-century tradition put a place, not where it actually was (pp.63–64). The sites fall into three areas: the Dead Sea including Qumran, the Jericho environs, and Jerusalem (p.64).

**Dead Sea (Ch.4, pp.63–74): Items 1, 2, 18–20, 21–25.**
- Vale of Achor = the Buqei‘a, a five-mile plateau above the Qumran cliffs. The basis is Jos 15:7. n.2 credits the identification to Noth (1955) and compares Cross & Milik. Hosea's "Gate of Hope" would have mattered to the Essenes (p.64).
- "the fortress" (Item 1) = Hyrcania / Kh. Mird, on its conical hill at the plain's western edge. He gives its Hasmonean and Herodian history: Antipater "ignobly buried" there, and it served as Herod's secret prison (p.65). St. Sabas called it "Castellon", and Mird comes from Syriac merdā’, "stronghold" (p.66). n.1 suggests ḥarûbah lies behind medieval "Solitudo Ruba". He tentatively links Item 2 to Antipater's burial (p.68).
- Items 18–20 (presses and pits) fit Hosea's "vineyards". The Arabic karm appears often in Buqei‘a place names (p.68). n.84 cites Karm ’Aṭrad = Kh. Abū Ṭabaq (Cross & Milik).
- Secacah = Kh. Qumran (pp.68–71; n.97). Jos 15:61–62 lists the wilderness towns from north to south. Kippah ("arch", root KPP) is a synonym of QMR, so Qimrôn is the Wady Qumran (p.70). Of the three wady sites, Kh. ez-Zarānîq has no remains earlier than Byzantine (n.97 cites W. R. Farmer, BASOR 147, 1957) and Kh. Abū Ṭabaq lacks the scroll's details. That leaves Qumran, which has Iron II remains (De Vaux).
- He equates Secacah with Josephus's Bemeselis/Bethomē and Zechariah's Mesillah ("Shady Place"), where Jannaeus besieged the rebels: "almost certainly the same place" (p.71). He uses this to tie the Teacher of Righteousness to the revolt, and to derive "Essene" from Aramaic words for "myrtle" and "healer" (pp.71–73).
- The Qumran deposits are pinned to the excavated plan in Fig. 4 (pp.73–74). They are the earthquake-cracked cistern (23), the aqueduct's settling basin and conduit head (22), the 90-ft fix toward the tower (24) and the coastal "eastern road" (25).
- n.97: Conder suggested Middin = Kh. Mird, which Allegro calls "possible".

**Jericho area (Ch.5, pp.75–78, and notes): Items 58, 46, 44, 45.**
- Item 58, "Mount Gerizim". The Samaritan mountain is rejected for two reasons: the directions are too vague for a hilltop full of pits, and Jewish treasure in a Samaritan holy place is "just inconceivable" (p.75). Instead he follows the Jewish and early Christian tradition (Rabbi Eleazar, the Madeba map) that put Gerizim and Ebal near Jericho (pp.75–76).
- He takes these to be the two mounds at the Wady Kelt outlet on either side of the old road: Cypros (Tuweil el-‘Aqabeh; Beit Jubr) and Taurus (p.76; n.298, following Conder and Abel). A blocked well near the tower on Cypros "may well be" the "upper pit" (p.76).
- The Zealots held these forts until Vespasian came in June 68 AD. n.298 calls this a terminus ad quem "if our identification of Cypros with 3QInv’s “Gerizim” is correct".
- Item 46 = ‘Ain Fārah in the upper Wady Kelt (Wady Fārah). "Deeps" (MṢLWT) would be the source of the Psalmist's ṢLMWT, "shadow of death"; "the Great Wady" matches "The Wady" of Jos 15:7. Simon bar Giora stored plunder in caves in this wady (p.78; n.230–232).
- Items 44–45 (notes only, n.222–226): Beth Tamar is probably Archelais at Kh. el-‘Awja, and Phasaelis is an option. Ba‘al Tamar near Gibeah does not fit "the outlet of the Valley of PL‘". The Stronghold of Senaah would be at upper Kh. ‘Awja (Abel).
- Fig. 18 circles Archelais (?), *Mt. Gerizim* (Cypros), ‘Ain Farah, Hyrcania, Secacah–Mesillah and Jerusalem. The Dead Sea and Jericho sites form a "clear line of defence" on Jerusalem's east and north (p.120).

**Jerusalem (Ch.6, pp.79–119, and notes): the bulk of the items.** "some two dozen" are in the Temple area, spread among other sites for secrecy (p.107). His main keys are the Mishnah and Josephus, with the 1864+ Wilson/Warren survey for the underground (pp.79–81).
- **North:** Item 56 = the Antonia Twin Pools, "only a possibility". It solves Josephus's "Strouthion", which he reads as yammôth, "settling-basins" (pp.82–85).
- **Tyropoeon:** "Outer Valley" is the lost Hebrew name of the Tyropoeon. Josephus's "Cheesemakers' Valley" misreads the root (p.85; n.168). Item 35 = the Stone of the Strayers; Item 29 = Wilson's Arch.
- **Hinnom:** Items 37–38 are in the Shaveh (Gen 14:17) = the lower Hinnom to the Sultan's Pool; Bethso may be Beth Shaveh (pp.90–92). Gate of the Essenes = "Gate of the Myrtles" (p.73; n.97).
- **Kidron:** Item 36 is at the Kidron–Hinnom confluence (n.174). Item 55 is in the common graves. Items 52–53 are the Tomb and Garden of Zadok below the Pinnacle (Bene Hezir or the façade to its south); Zadok may be "the Just", tied to James's martyrdom, and the garden perhaps to Gethsemane or Golgotha (pp.93–101). Item 48 is the Gethsemane press (pp.99–100). Item 49 is Absalom's Monument, perhaps Jannaeus's tomb (pp.102–103). Item 47 is possibly Beth Page (pp.101–102).
- **Temple gates:** Item 26 is the eastern Double Gate, i.e. the Golden Gate (p.104). Item 27 is a tower in the east wall (n.132).
- **Temple interior:** the Court of the Peristyle and Great Cistern (3), Tank 8 (16–17), the Inner Court (8), the Second Enclosure (40) and the cisterns before the Nicanor Gate (9–10). Then the Chamber of the Hearth complex (7, 5, 6, 41, 42–43, 4), the Parwah Chamber (31–32), the Treasury of Phineas (34), the shambles court (12–13, notes), the Golah Cistern (59), and the Altar drain and pit (60–61) (pp.107–119).
- **Tombs outside the walls (notes):** the MLHM tombs (14–15), the Queen (28) and the western tomb, possibly Herod's family tomb (57).
- **Unlocated:** Items 11, 30, 33, 39, 50, 51 and 54. [INF] The book gives them no region, though their entries run among Jerusalem items.

**Other.** EVIDENCE: no item is placed outside these three areas. Gerizim is the only nominally northern name, and he moves it to Jericho. [INF] Mar Saba, ‘Ain Feshkha and Siloam are not treasure sites in this book; see §7.

## 5. Opening and first decipherment (Ch.1–2, Foreword)

All EVIDENCE.
- **Find.** On 14 March 1952, an official search team found Cave 3 about a mile north of the first cave. Two rolls lay against an inner wall (pp.19–20). The rolls were once nearly pure copper, now fully oxidized; there were two parts from broken rivets, the strip nearly a foot wide and reckoned about eight feet long (p.20).
- **Kuhn.** K. G. Kuhn read letters from the outside and from photographs of the rolls' undersides, and guessed it was a treasure list (p.22).
- **Manchester.** Director of Antiquities G. Lankester Harding agreed that the smaller roll could be treated in Manchester. Dr B. V. Bowden, Principal of the College of Technology, offered facilities, and Prof. H. Wright Baker built a saw from ex-Service equipment (pp.22–24).
- **Dates.** The smaller roll arrived "in the summer of 1955" (p.23). Baker made the first cut late one evening. The first piece came from Col. IX (Items 39–45), and by the next evening Kuhn's hypothesis was confirmed (pp.24–25). The first roll gave Cols. IX–XII. The larger roll arrived "in January 1956" and was "quickly cut open" (p.27).
- **Foreword.** The decipherment began with the cutting "in Manchester in the winter of 1955–56" (p.6). ‘Awni Dajani attended the cutting "in 1956" as Jordan's representative (p.6). Allegro was invited to publish the text in summer 1957 by Dr Ghuraibi (p.6).
- **Release.** General news of the contents came out of Jerusalem six months after the opening, and the first press announcement treated it as a fairy tale (p.56).

## 6. Who hid it, and when (Ch.7–8)

EVIDENCE.
- **Conclusion.** The scroll is a list of sacred treasure "hidden away by the Zealots most probably during the spring of 68" AD (p.130). The treasure is mostly from the Temple (tithes, sacred vessels, bullion), plus loot from Zealot raids on desert "holy places" such as Qumran (pp.124–125, 128).
- **Not Qumran's Essenes.** The concealers in Jerusalem and in the desert were one group, and "the Qumran Essenes had nothing at all to do with it" (p.120).
- **The Zealots at Qumran.** Under John of Gischala, who controlled the Temple treasuries (pp.122–124), Zealots took Qumran in spring 68. They held it about three months, until Vespasian reached Jericho in June 68, and engraved and hid the scroll there from reports sent in (p.125). Zealot occupation of Qumran is now "a virtual certainty" (p.125).
- **Supporting points.** The Zealots also hid the torn Essene scrolls (pp.125–127). Duplicate copies are possible: Item 61, and "current rumours" of another copper sheet from Cave 11 (p.125).
- **Earlier wording.** Ch.3 calls the scroll a guide for Jewish survivors of "the war then raging" (p.62).
