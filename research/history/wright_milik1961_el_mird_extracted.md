## A1. Who did the fieldwork, when, under whose expedition, and what was done

1. **EVIDENCE (p. 1 / PDF 2, opening sentence; checked on the image).**
   - Quote: "During the first half of April 1960 the Copper Scroll Expedition"
   - Quote: "under the direction of J. Allegro"
   - The expedition "was able to make some investigations at the site of Khirbet Mird". The text layer misreads the name as "Aixegro"; the image reads ALLEGRO in small caps.
2. **EVIDENCE (p. 1 / PDF 2, footnote ¹; checked on a 250-dpi crop).**
   - Quote: "members of the expedition concerned with the work at El Mird"
   - Names as printed: Canon R. DE LANGHE, J. BELSHÉ, Z. VIATR, S. JEEVES, R. IZZARD and G. R. H. WRIGHT. The text layer garbles these as "de Lanche, Bei^shé, Jeevks".
3. **EVIDENCE (p. 1).** The work was limited. Quote: "Owing to inclement weather and shortage of time". Nothing could be done "in an exhaustive fashion", and the results are given "in summary form".
4. **EVIDENCE (p. 2 / PDF 3, fn. 1; p. 7 / PDF 8, fn. 2).** Wright treats the Belgian work at Mird as a separate, earlier event.
   - Quote (p. 2 fn. 1): "In 1952, a Belgian expedition under the direction of Canon de Langhe". That expedition "sought and found manuscripts in the ruins", citing *RB* 1953 p. 526.
   - Quote (p. 7 fn. 2), on the large cistern near the southern end of the summit: "Where de Langhe discovered manuscripts in 1952."
5. **INFERENCE.** Wright's fieldwork belonged to Allegro's Copper Scroll Expedition in the first half of April 1960, not to the 1952(–53) Louvain/Belgian expedition. De Langhe appears in the member list as a member of the 1960 party at Mird. His name in the search snippet therefore does not show that this was the Louvain expedition.
6. **EVIDENCE (p. 6 / PDF 7).** No excavation was done on the summit.
   - Quote: "No excavation was possible, indeed there was little time for anything"
   - Quote (continuing): "other than essential planning"
   - He adds that the surface is largely shaped by the 20th-century monastic settlement and by Bedouin devastation in 1939.
7. **EVIDENCE: what was done**
   - **Survey and planning.** Figs. 1, 2 and 4 are drawn plans. Fig. 1 is signed "GRHW - '60"; Figs. 2 and 4 are signed "GRHW". Fig. 3 (pottery) is signed "MP & GRHW".
   - **Aerial work.** The acknowledgement (p. 1 fn. *) thanks Major Eric Bennet and Capt. Gordon Brown of the Royal Jordan Air Force. They helped the surveying by arranging aerial reconnaissance and photography.
   - **Surface and cistern pottery collection** (p. 8; Fig. 3).
   - **Clearance at the "monument"** (pp. 14–15; see A2.8).
   - **Recording of Herodian graves already opened by Bedouin, with finds recovered** (p. 17; see A2.9).
   - **Measurement of the bedouin-cleared west tunnel and a hurried clearance of the mouth of the east tunnel** (p. 19; see A2.10).
8. **EVIDENCE (p. 2).** The expedition's four objects of study, in Wright's order:
   1. The structures on the hill, with the aqueduct.
   2. A small Herodian monument. Quote: "crowning a neighbouring peak c 300 m to the South West"
   3. Herodian graves "marked by rubble circles on the eastern slopes below the fortress".
   4. Two rock-cut stepped tunnels in the Wadi Abu Sha'le, north of the fortress.
9. **EVIDENCE (p. 1 fn. *).** Other acknowledgements:
   - P. J. Parr helped with the pottery and gave access to the British School of Archaeology.
   - Yussef Sa'ad made the Palestine Museum records available.
   - Fr Bellarmino Bagatti advised on monasteries.
   - J. T. Milik is thanked for his "literary researches" (the appendix).
10. **INFERENCE.** Allegro's statement that "our surveyor charted them and afterward published his plans and description" fits Wright on every point:
    - He was a member of Allegro's party at Mird.
    - He drew and signed the plans in 1960.
    - He published them with description in *Biblica* 42.
    - The acknowledgement speaks of "the work of surveying".

    Allegro's "winter 1959–60" and Wright's "first half of April 1960" are not the same phrasing. Wright gives only the Mird dates, not the dates of the expedition as a whole.
11. **BK.** The Allegro details (dates, the mine detector, the 1962–63 architect, Fig. 5 credited "after G. R. H. Wright, Biblica 42") come from the project brief, not from this source.

## A2. Features described

### A2.1 Setting and orientation

- **EVIDENCE (p. 2).** Khirbet Mird crowns a detached promontory peak on the eastern edge of the Judaean highlands, overlooking the Buqei'a. By consensus it is Herodian Hyrcania. The monastery of Kastellion was set up on its ruins at the end of the 5th century AD.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 2).** From the east it looks like an isolated conical peak about 250 m above the surrounding land (Pl. I). From the west it is seen to be a spur-like promontory.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 2, fnn. 2–3).** Wright gives two map references:
  - Mird: Palestine 1:100,000 Jerusalem Sheet, grid reference 1848, 1252.
  - The monument: marked as a ruin on 1:20,000 sheet 18-12, grid reference 1845, 1250.
- **INFERENCE/BK.** If these are Palestine-grid references in hundreds of metres (184.8 E / 125.2 N and 184.5 E / 125.0 N), the monument lies about 0.3 km W and 0.2 km S of the summit, roughly 360 m to the WSW. That is consistent with Wright's "c 300 m to the South West".
- **EVIDENCE (p. 2 fn. 4).** There is a land-rover track from near the start of the Dead Sea Highway, past Mird to Mar Saba and the Bethlehem road. Fig. 1 marks an "APPROACH ROAD FROM JERICHO" from the SE.

### A2.2 The "ditch": quarried cleft and saddle on the west

- **EVIDENCE (p. 3 / PDF 4).** Mird is almost completely cut off from the higher slopes to the west. A former saddle "has been transformed by quarrying into a sharp cleft". He compares Alexandreion (Sartaba) and Machaerus, both artificially isolated (pp. 4–5).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 3).** On the steepness of this "valley". Quote: "given its contour by revetment and retaining walls". The sentence assigns this to "the east flank of Mird". Much of the work is no longer visible.
  - **INFERENCE.** Since the cleft lies W of Mird, "east flank" may mean the valley's eastern side, i.e. Mird's western face. The wording is ambiguous.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 3).** A narrow neck crosses the valley, partly left in the rock and partly built. It serves both as the aqueduct's course and as an easily defended approach.
  - Today a path winds up from it to the summit.
  - Wright judges the path close to the original line. A well-built rubble salient nearby marks an old defensive post.
  - Fn. 2: Schick showed a path approaching from the north, which is no longer marked.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 3 fn. 1).** He quotes *SWP* (a saddle to the west and a road along a very narrow rock ledge). He also quotes Hamilton, who recognised artificial cuttings dividing Mird from the western slopes.
- **EVIDENCE.** Wright uses no word "ditch" or "moat". His terms are "cleft", "valley", "cutting" and "saddle".

### A2.3 Aqueduct and bridge

- **EVIDENCE (p. 5 / PDF 6).** Much of the masonry bridge carrying the aqueduct across the cutting survives (Pl. II). Its masonry with drafted margins, in the "Herodian" style (Pl. III), fixes the basic date of the site.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 5 fn. 2).** Masonry details:
  - The central part stands 10 courses high, built stepped and battered.
  - The specus is c. 50 cm broad and in places preserved about as deep. Quote: "It is lined with hydraulic cement c 10 cms thick."
  - At the bottom is a passage (via) c. 50 cm wide, perhaps for drainage and for crossing the neck.
  - Ashlar is bedded in earth mortar, with courses c. 40 cm high and blocks c. 25–80 cm long.
  - The lowest stage on the north side is vertically faced Herodian masonry: four courses of alternating headers (c. 30 cm) and stretchers (c. 75 cm). Drafted margins are c. 8–10 cm and bosses project c. 6 cm.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 5).** The aqueduct's course, partly cut and partly built, was followed some distance NW up the slopes towards Jebel Muntar.
  - Fn. 3: the reprinted PEF map (surveyed 1878; War Office 1917 Sheet XVII) shows it running NW towards El Muntar, c. 2 km.
  - Mader names "two mountain tunnels" as the source. Wright could not check this first-hand.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 6 / PDF 7).** Quote: "The aqueduct is broken abruptly after it crosses the cutting". No further channel is visible. Fn. 3 rejects Mader's claim that it carried rain water "into the fortress".
- **EVIDENCE (p. 6).** Immediately north of the bridge the quarrying forms "two square cisterns which are most likely to be reservoirs". Quote: "although they do not appear to be plastered"
  - He quotes *SWP*: the aqueduct once supplied two pools, "30 ft to 40 ft square north of its course".
  - He quotes Husseini: "two rock cut reservoirs".
- **EVIDENCE (p. 6).** Wright doubts that water would be carried across the cutting to fill reservoirs on the far side. He notes that most cisterns on the far slopes are higher than the aqueduct, and says the water supply needs further study (citing Masada; Milik 1959 suggests a source in the wadi en-Nar).
- **INFERENCE.** Allegro's "two great rock-cut cisterns" most plausibly match Wright's two square quarried cisterns/reservoirs north of the bridge. Wright gives no dimensions of his own for them, only the quoted *SWP* figure.

### A2.4 Cisterns and vaults on the summit

- **EVIDENCE (p. 3).** On the very large cisterns. Quote: "These exist on north, west and south flanks of the hill". The summit building platform was made by vaulting over irregularities, which created vaulted reservoirs below the summit complex.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 7 / PDF 8).** The vaults:
  - Two accessible vaults "run parallel north and south".
  - Quote: "beneath the eastern range of rooms is entered by modern stairs" (Pl. IV).
  - The vault under the court is entered "by ropes from a cistern head".
  - Fn. 3 gives the overall dimensions as c. 12 m long × c. 5.20 m broad × c. 8 m deep. The footnote is attached to the court vault, with "this" as the example.
  - Voussoirs are 60–70 × 30 × 25 cm. The vault intrados lies c. 80 cm below the court. No mortar is seen.
  - Walls are plastered with hydraulic cement up to the springing (p. 8).
  - These are Herodian vaulted cisterns, some used by monks "for domestic purposes up to recent times" (p. 8).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 8).** Run-off from the court was channelled into the cisterns; the court's fall can still be seen. So the court design is Herodian too.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 7, fn. 2).** A "large cistern" lies near the southern end of the summit, now easy to see because of a break-in from the surface. This is where de Langhe found manuscripts in 1952. Wright suggests a southern angle tower may have stood over it.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 8 fn. 4; Fig. 3).** Pottery from "a Cistern in the South Face of El Mird" is a Herodian group: a terra sigillata bowl, piriform bottles, globular jugs, pilgrim flasks with twisted handles, and wine jars, one base stained with asphalt.
- **INFERENCE/BK.** Allegro's courtyard vault, "~40 × 25 × 16 ft" (≈12.2 × 7.6 × 4.9 m), matches Wright's court vault in length (c. 12 m) but not in breadth (c. 5.20 m) or depth (c. 8 m). Wright gives no total count of cisterns.

### A2.5 Summit platform, enceinte and towers

- **EVIDENCE (p. 3).**
  - Quote: "a level platform c 40 m x 25 m"
  - A central paved court has ranges of rooms on its east, north and west sides; the north range is now a church (Fig. 2).
  - South of the platform is a lower terrace. At its eastern end is a range of cells built against the rock; at its western end are rock-cut chambers, one with cist graves, mosaic remains and frescoes.
  - A rubble wall at a slightly lower level encloses all this in an irregular oval of about 120 m × 60 m (pp. 3–4).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 4).** No feature can be identified specifically as pre-Herodian. Only the site itself can be so classed, since it is "generally accepted" to be a Hasmonean fort rebuilt by Herod.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 7).** Quote on the enceinte wall: "in the main c 1 m high is manifestly modern"
  - Wright tentatively suggests Herodian towers in three places:
    - At the north angle, where a rubbish pit shows ashlar.
    - Near the southern extremity, over the large cistern.
    - At the SE angle, where foundations of a wider platform lie near a monks' projecting chamber.
  - Nothing could be conjectured for the curving NE wall.
  - Fn. 1: Schick showed a double angular enceinte with bastions, which is no longer visible.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 8).** The court with ranges on three sides is judged original Herodian, because the north and west walls have Herodian masonry in their lower courses. He compares Masada.

### A2.6 Monastery and church

- **EVIDENCE (p. 9 / PDF 10).** Kastellion was founded by St Sabas in 492 AD.
  - Wright sets aside as modern the guard walls over the eastern cistern entrance and the cells at the eastern end of the lower terrace.
  - The church was cleared and reused by monks after 1925. Hamilton photographed its furnished chancel in 1935. The furniture and the rear wall of the apse were destroyed by Bedouin in 1939 (fn. 1).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 10 / PDF 11).**
  - The apse was thrust out beyond the original east wall.
  - The church floor is a plain mosaic with no earlier mosaic below.
  - The plan is a simple "monastic" one. Traces of a marble chancel screen remain, and two screen posts lie on the slope below the apse.
  - The church has two entrances: one on the west (blocked) into the earth-filled west range, and one on the south to the court.
- **EVIDENCE (pp. 10–12).** The figured mosaic in the room south of the church is probably the diakonikon (Pls. V–VI):
  - Tesserae c. 1 cm.
  - The main panel is a 240 cm square, with a border c. 52 cm deep and a field c. 188 cm square, decorated with spirals, plait and a diagonal floret lattice.
  - There is a 75 cm surround with 18 × 18 cm indented squares, and a bird (partridge-like) with a fish(?) above it.
  - The parallels he cites suggest a 5th–6th-century date, part of the "original foundation".
- **EVIDENCE (pp. 12–13 / PDF 13–14).** The tomb cave is at the W end of the south terrace.
  - It is disturbed by the "violent rummaging of treasure seekers".
  - "several (8) cist tombs" are in the centre, with a plain white mosaic on part of the raised surround. Wright assumes these are burials of the most saintly monks.
  - It was later turned into a chapel. Mader studied its frescoes of anchorites (Pl. VII); Wright says only modern repainting is now visible.
  - Husseini (fn. 4) recorded four graves.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 13).** Sabas reused the ruins: the west range as domestic rooms, the north as the church, the east as the diakonikon. The aqueduct was probably out of use, and a few cisterns served the monks while the rest became cells.

### A2.7 "Steps" on the east side

- **EVIDENCE.** Wright describes no ancient steps on the east side. The only stairs he mentions on the summit are the "modern stairs" into the vault under the eastern range (p. 7; Pl. IV).
- **EVIDENCE (Fig. 2, drawing).** The plan draws three flights of steps, none labelled in words:
  - A short flight with an "up" arrow in a small room east of the court.
  - A flight inside a rectangular chamber south of the paved court.
  - A flight at the S/SE outer corner of the enclosure, beside the label "RANGE OF CELLS BELOW".
- **EVIDENCE (Pl. I; p. 2).** From the east Mird appears as a steep conical peak. Wright does not discuss access from the east.
- **INFERENCE.** Nothing in Wright supports an ancient eastern stair or entrance. His evidence for the approach points to the western neck and the zigzag path (Fig. 1).

### A2.8 The "Monument" SW of the fortress (Section II, pp. 14–16 / PDF 15–17; Fig. 4; Pls. VIII–IX)

- **EVIDENCE (p. 14).** Quote: "Possible references in the Copper Scroll" directed the expedition to a small rubble construction crowning a crag 300 m SW of the fortress (Fig. 4, Pl. VIII). It is marked on maps. Husseini called it the "remains of a watch tower", which was also Wright's first impression.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 14).** It had largely been thrown down by Bedouin in recent years, so its original elevation is almost impossible to tell.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 14), the method.**
  - Quote: "sufficient time was found to clear around the base of the monument"
  - Clearing established the plan.
  - Quote: "the disorder within was cleared to leave a section" (Pl. IX).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 14), construction.**
  - The rock pinnacle was clad in rubble masonry to form a regular structure on a square base, which seems to grow out of the slope.
  - The pinnacle may have been quarried to shape, with the spoil reused. Fn. 3 compares the tumulus of Antiochos I at Nemrud Dağ.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 14), dimensions.**
  - Quote: "a square on the side of slightly more than 10 metres"
  - On the NW side, "facing the only approach and hence the front", there is quote: "a platform about two meters broad extending almost the entire length" of the face.
  - Quote (pp. 14–15): "This feature was unsuspected until clearance."
- **EVIDENCE (p. 15).** The base is set out with substantial retaining walls of coursed rubble. They are founded at different levels on the irregular rock, keyed into it, and partly well over 2 m high. The space around the pinnacle is filled with boulders packed in earth-and-pebble mortar.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 15).** At first glance it looked like a truncated pyramid with a rammed-earth top. After the Bedouin debris was cleared this form proved "adventitious", and the evidence was not conclusive. Fn. 1: the front platform does not fit a regular pyramid.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 15).**
  - Quote: "No chamber or deposits were located in the monument"
  - Sherds from the interstices of the fill date it to Herodian times. Fn. 2: the most diagnostic were several "Lekythoi" necks, like the one in the Herodian grave (object 2).
- **EVIDENCE (pp. 15–16).** Wright says little suggests a utilitarian watch tower. He regards it as a funerary monument or high place, or both. Quote: "a funerary monument or high place or both combined". He compares the Iron Age tumuli west of Jerusalem (Amiran, *IEJ* 8, 1958) and Albright's *bamah* theory.
- **INFERENCE/BK.** Wright's account fits Allegro's on several points:
  - A square, about 35 ft a side (Wright: slightly over 10 m ≈ 33+ ft).
  - Rubble masonry around a rock pinnacle.
  - Clearance "around the sides".
  - A platform found on the NW side.
- **INFERENCE/BK.** Wright says nothing about a mine or metal detector, "signals" or magnetic rock.

### A2.9 Herodian graves / cemetery (Section III, pp. 16–18 / PDF 17, 38–39; Fig. 6)

- **EVIDENCE (p. 16).** Quote: "some half a kilometre east from the summit". At the foot of Mird, residual hillocks of soft shale were used as the fortress cemetery. The surface looked like Bedouin activity (Fig. 5).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 16).** The grave markers:
  - Graves are marked by angular boulders brought from a distance, set singly or (once) two or three inside an enclosure.
  - Each grave has a rough stone circle c. 3 m in diameter, with fill c. 10–20 cm above ground.
  - NW of the group are two further circles. The more remote one is a mini-tumulus c. 1.25 m high, nearly clad in stones, with much Herodian surface pottery.
  - The graves have no uniform orientation.
  - The enclosure is a rectangle c. 30 m long, of one course of stones, on an eroded bluff; one angle has fallen into a gully.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 17 / PDF 38).** Bedouin "had cleared out several of the graves (shown opened on plan)". From what survived, the burial method was reconstructed.
  - For the most northerly grave, quote: "almost all the finds were recovered" (Fig. 6).
  - That grave is a pit c. 2.00 × 1.00 m cut c. 1.50 m into the shale. A working ledge was left and a further 0.50 m deepened for the burial.
  - The body was flexed, feet first, under a large flat stone with the grave goods beneath it. In another grave the body was extended. There were no wrappings or coffin.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 17).** Catalogue:
  1. Terra sigillata bowl, D. 15 cm, H. 7.5 cm.
  2. "Lekythos" neck.
  3–9. Ampullae / piriform bottles.

  All are typical Herodian types, with parallels at Samaria and Herodian Jericho (pp. 17–18).
- **INFERENCE.** Wright does not say in so many words that the expedition dug the graves. He says Bedouin had opened several and that the finds from one were "recovered". This is compatible with Allegro's "dug one or two graves", but not proof of it. Allegro's "low hill SE" and Wright's hillocks "half a kilometre east" (ESE on Fig. 1) appear to be the same group.

### A2.10 The stepped tunnels (Section IV, pp. 19–21 / PDF 40–42; Pls. X–XI)

- **EVIDENCE (p. 19).** Two stepped tunnels are cut into opposite cliff faces of the Wadi Abu Sha'le, "due north of the fortress". The wadi bed there is barely 2–3 m wide.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 19), the western tunnel.**
  - It is cut into the S cliff and runs due south.
  - Quote: "cleared to a depth of 30 m by bedouin treasure seekers"
  - Its mouth is more than 2 m above the wadi bed, with no artificial ascent (Pl. X).
  - The bore is rectangular, c. 2 m high, 90 cm wide at the shoulders and 80 cm at the foot.
  - Steps have c. 30 cm treads and 20 cm risers, at a slope a little over 30°.
  - It is straight enough to see from top to bottom. The roof is stepped down at regular intervals (Pl. XI).
- **EVIDENCE (p. 19), the eastern tunnel.**
  - It is cut into the N face, bearing slightly W of N.
  - Its opening is near the wadi bed and was almost buried by a river terrace.
  - Quote: "The mouth has been hurriedly cleared". It is similar to the first but smaller (c. 70 cm at the shoulder).
  - Above it, a rubble revetment retains an ancient, partly rock-cut path.
- **EVIDENCE (pp. 19–20).** An identical tunnel lies "some six miles to the north", on the N side of the Jerusalem–Jericho road. A Palestine Museum note describes it as a staircase "of over 60 steps", partly excavated to c. 35 m.
- **EVIDENCE (p. 20).** Quote: "they are mining adits, but they are not". Wright offers an analogy with the Hierothesion of Mithridates Kallinikos at Arsameia on the Nymphaios: a stepped tunnel 158 m long, excavated by F. K. Dörner in 1954/56. He suggests a Mithraic/"petrogenes" association and accepts the difficulties for Palestine (pp. 20–21).
- **INFERENCE.** Wright gives no step count for the Mird tunnels.
  - At about 30 cm tread and 20 cm rise, each step adds about 0.36 m of slope length. A cleared 30 m would then be about 80+ steps.
  - This is arithmetic only. Allegro's "~100 steps" is BK from the brief.

## A4. Milik's appendix, "The Monastery of Kastellion" (pp. 21–27 / PDF 42–48)

**EVIDENCE (paraphrase).**

- **Foundation.** Following Cyril of Scythopolis, Sabas spent Lent 492 alone on the "hill of Kastellion", about 20 stades NE of the Great Laura, a peak thought haunted by demons. He then returned with monks, including John the Silentiary.
  - They found "a great dwelling place" of marvellously dressed blocks divided into vaulted rooms. Milik reads this as the remains of Hyrcania.
  - Old material was reused for a church and cells. Abbot Marcian supplied provisions; he died in November 492.
- **Phasing.** The first structures were built hastily. The surviving church remains, baptistery, chancel post (hermula) and mosaics probably belong to a slightly later, costly phase by Jerusalem artisans (p. 22).
- **Leadership.** The leaders were *dioikêtai* appointed by Mar Saba:
  - Paul, then Theodore of Melitene, then Sergius and Paul, who later became bishops of Amathus and Aila.
  - Milik asks whether there was a double community, with cenobites in the convent and laurites in nearby caves.
  - The house kept xenodochia at Jerusalem and Jericho.
- **Late 6th century.** John Moschos names abbot Agathonikos. He also mentions a laura on the "Mount of Kastellion", Mard(a), whose garden 6 miles away near the sea Milik matches to Ain Feshkha. A Byzantine house found there may be the gardener's dwelling.
  - Fn.: *Marda* is Christian Aramaic for "fortress" and lies behind Arabic el-Mird. It is not the Marda (= Masada) of the Life of Euthymios.
- **8th century.** The Life of St Stephen the Sabaite records:
  - Zacharias as abbot of Kastellion and St George of the Spelaion (749).
  - Stephen's Lenten retreats in the Kastellion caves, including a basin found there that was empty.
- **The 1952–53 finds.** Milik dates the Arabic, Greek and Christian Palestinian Aramaic texts "discovered in 1952-53 in an underground cavern" to the 8th–10th centuries. He republishes a CPA papyrus letter from "the sinner Gabriel" to the superior of the Laura (Pl. XII), along with Aramaic wall inscriptions and a painted plaster fragment.
- **The crypt.** A small crypt on the S slope (8 tombs) holds the 36 painted saints Mader studied; Milik suggests a high-medieval date.
- **Last notice.** An icon brought from "the monastery of Kastellion" to Mar Saba is dated 1355 AD.
- **Identification and recent occupation.** Van Kasteren and Rhétoré first identified Hyrcania with Kastellion and Mird. Mar Saba monks settled there in 1925, made the crypt an oratory, cleared the church and left after several years under Bedouin pressure.

## What the source does NOT say

- It does not say the Mird work was the Louvain/de Langhe expedition. It credits the work to Allegro's Copper Scroll Expedition (April 1960). De Langhe appears only as a member, and separately as leader of the 1952 Belgian search.
- No exact day-by-day dates, only "first half of April 1960". There is no statement linking this to a "winter 1959–60" season.
- It does not call Wright the "surveyor" in so many words. The role is inferred from his signed plans and the "work of surveying" acknowledgement.
- No excavation on the summit ("No excavation was possible"). No soundings anywhere, except the clearance at the monument and recording of Bedouin-opened graves.
- No count of cisterns. No dimensions of its own for the two square reservoirs north of the bridge; only the quoted *SWP* figure of 30–40 ft. No dimension for the vault under the eastern range.
- No ancient steps or entrance on the east side, and no discussion of a "fortress in the Vale of Achor" or "forty cubits under the steps".
- No step count for the tunnels. No account of the expedition clearing the W tunnel beyond the Bedouin's 30 m.
- No mine or metal detector, no "signals", no magnetic rock.
- No explicit statement that the expedition itself excavated a grave. No precise date for the monument beyond "Herodian" sherds. No chamber, burial or deposit at the monument.
- Fig. 5 (the graves plan) is not in this PDF, although the text cites it twice.
- No modern coordinates (only Palestine-grid references for the site and the monument). No levels or elevations in metres above sea level.
- No identification of the Mird remains with Copper Scroll item 1 or 2 by Wright himself. He reports only that "possible references" directed the expedition's attention.
