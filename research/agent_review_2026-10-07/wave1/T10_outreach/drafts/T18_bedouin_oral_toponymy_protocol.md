# T18 — Protocol: Bedouin oral toponymy interviews for Copper Scroll landmarks

Status: DRAFT PROTOCOL. No fieldwork, contact or recruitment has taken place. It must pass ethics review at a partner institution and be adapted with community representatives before any use.

## 1. Purpose and limits

Aim: record living Arabic micro-toponyms (names of wadis, caves, cisterns, springs, terraces, ruins, paths) in the landscapes proposed for Copper Scroll entries, so that names can be compared with the scroll's Hebrew landmark words and with 19th-century name lists, **without suggesting answers to the people interviewed**.

What it can show: that a name is in present oral use, where its feature lies, how old speakers believe it is, and how widely it is known. What it cannot show: that a modern Arabic name continues a Second Temple Hebrew name. Any such link is a separate argument made later from the recorded data.

Setting (EVIDENCE): the Ta'amireh, "a large Palestinian Bedouin tribe" whose heartland stretches "from the western Dead Sea shores to Bethlehem", used the Qumran area as "seasonal grazing grounds", and members discovered the Dead Sea Scrolls in the Qumran caves from 1946–47 and in the Murabba'at caves in 1951 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ta%27amireh). Other Bedouin groups live in the Jericho and Judaean-desert areas. Many candidate landmarks lie in politically contested areas and in protected or excavated sites.

Specific risks to plan for (INFERENCE, high confidence):
- **Contamination**: Ta'amireh knowledge has been shaped by 70+ years of contact with archaeologists, scroll dealers and published maps. A "name" may be an echo of a scholar's proposal.
- **Harm**: recorded place knowledge can be used in land disputes, can expose people to legal risk (e.g. over antiquities), and precise cave or cistern locations can help looters.
- **Leading**: the prestige of "treasure" questions invites polite agreement and invention.

## 3. Ethics and permissions

1. **Ethics review** at the partner institution(s) before any contact; local permissions as required by the relevant authorities for the area.
2. **Community entry**: first meetings with community or tribal representatives to explain the aims, the risks and the data rules; agree whether and how to proceed. Their decision is final.
3. **Informed consent**, individual and oral where preferred (recorded on audio), in the local Arabic dialect, covering: purpose; that participation is voluntary and unpaid answers are not expected to please anyone; separate yes/no choices for (a) audio recording, (b) photographs, (c) GPS recording of places, (d) use of the person's name, (e) archiving and (f) publication of place names and locations; the right to stop, skip questions or withdraw data later (until a stated date).
4. **No questions** about antiquities finds, digging, sales or ownership of objects. If people raise them, the interviewer does not record them.
5. **Protection of locations**: exact coordinates of caves, cisterns and tombs are held in a restricted dataset; public outputs are generalised (e.g. to 1 km) unless the community and the antiquities authority agree otherwise.
6. **Data ownership**: recordings and transcripts are co-owned with the community or deposited in an archive the community chooses; copies returned to participants on request (CARE principles).

## 4. People and language

- Interviewers: native speakers of the local Bedouin Arabic dialect, ideally from or accepted by the community, trained in this protocol; at least one woman interviewer so that women can be interviewed by women where that is expected.
- Sample: 20–40 people per landscape, across age groups, genders and lineages; include older herders who used the area before settlement. Continue until new interviews add few new names (saturation), and record the count.
- **Compensation**: a fixed payment per interview session at a fair local day rate, agreed in advance with community representatives and **not linked to the number or kind of names given**; hospitality customs respected; travel costs covered.

## 5. Interview design (eliciting names without leading)

Order matters; each step is completed before the next.

1. **Background** (5 min): age range, lineage (if consented), years and seasons spent in the area, how place knowledge was learned (family, herding, work with excavators, maps, guides). This is used later to weigh contamination.
2. **Free listing** (15–20 min): "Please tell me the names of all the places you know between [landmark A] and [landmark B]." No prompts beyond "any others?". Record names in the order given.
3. **Map biography** (30–45 min): on an unlabelled base map or satellite image (no place names printed, no candidate sites marked), the person marks where each listed place is, its extent and type (cave, cistern, spring, wadi, ruin, path…). Use the methods and data standards of Tobias (2009).
4. **Walk-along** (optional, half day): visit features with the person; ask "What is this place called?" and "What is that one called?" at each. Record GPS, photographs (if consented) and audio. Do not visit or approach active excavations or fenced sites without permission.
5. **Meaning and story** (open): "Why is it called that?", "Who told you this name?", "Do other people call it something else?" Record folk etymologies as given.
6. **Recognition task, last and controlled** (10 min): a list of 20–30 names read in random order. It mixes (a) names from 19th-century surveys, (b) Arabic forms that scholars have connected with Copper Scroll landmarks, and (c) plausible **foil names that do not exist locally**. For each: "Have you heard this name? Where is it?" Interviewers do not know which names are targets and which are foils (the list is prepared by someone else). Answers to foils measure suggestion and politeness; any target name "recognised" no more often than foils is not counted.
- Interviewers never mention the Copper Scroll, treasure or any scholar's proposal during steps 1–5. The study is described as documenting local place names.

## 6. Recording and data standards

- Audio: uncompressed WAV (48 kHz, 24-bit), external microphone; backup recorder.
- Per name, one record: name in Arabic script; phonetic transcription (IPA); speaker code; date; step in which given (free list / map / walk / recognition); feature type; geometry (point/line/polygon) and positional uncertainty in metres; meaning or story; other names for the same feature; source of knowledge; consent flags; restricted/public flag.
- Transcription checked by a second dialect speaker; disagreements kept, not merged.
- Comparison layer (office work, after fieldwork): match recorded names against the 1881 *Survey of Western Palestine* name lists and other historical maps, recording exact, partial or no match. This is done blind to the Copper Scroll target list first, then compared.

## 7. Analysis rules (fixed in advance)

- A name counts as "in current use" only if given unprompted (steps 2–5) by at least 3 people from at least 2 families, or by 2 people on a walk-along at the same feature.
- Names given only in the recognition task are reported separately and adjusted against the foil rate.
- Any proposed link between a recorded name and a scroll word is a separate, later argument with its own linguistic justification; the toponymy data do not by themselves identify a scroll site.

## 8. Partner institution types

- A university department of anthropology, linguistics or archaeology in the region with research-ethics review and Arabic-dialect expertise (e.g. Palestinian or Jordanian universities with such departments), as lead or co-lead.
- A community-based organisation or recognised community representatives of the groups concerned.
- The antiquities authority responsible for the area (for site protection and coordinate restriction), consulted on what may be published.
- A foreign research institute based in the region (for example the British, American and French research institutes in Jerusalem and Amman) for logistics, archives and historical maps.
- An archive able to hold restricted audio and geodata long term on the community's terms.

## 9. Short invitation text for a partner institution (draft)

We are a small research project on the place descriptions in the Copper Scroll from Qumran. We would like to explore whether your department would lead or co-lead a documentation study of living Bedouin place names in [area], following an ethics-reviewed protocol (attached) that puts community consent, data ownership and site protection first. The study would record names and their locations as people use them, without asking about antiquities or suggesting answers. We would cover interviewers' and participants' costs and would deposit the data on terms agreed with the community. Would you be willing to discuss this?
