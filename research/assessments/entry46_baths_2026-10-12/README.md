## Armon ha-Naẓiv L5

**Observed remains.** At the southern tunnel entrance, a rock-cut approach about 27 m long contained L5. Its overall cut was approximately 8 × 3 m, with maximum depth about 4.5 m. Stairs ran west to east and then turned south into an initially oval basin about 4 × 3 m. A 1.6 m drop separated the lowest stair and basin floor. A roughly 3 m long, 0.2 m wide rock-cut channel branched from the aqueduct and supplied the bath. Plan 3's Sections 4–4 and 5–5, and Fig. 16, show the channel and aqueduct relationship (pp. 12*–15*).

**Phases.** W10 separated the bath from the aqueduct's entrance approach. Later W11 divided the oval basin across its width, leaving its northern part in use. Rock surfaces lacked general plaster; W11's north face had grey plaster, continuing a short distance onto adjoining rock (p. 12*; p. 49* n. 16). The southern part L19 contained large fallen stones, possibly from a collapsed roof (p. 49* n. 13). These details require a phase-specific entrance model.

**Date evidence.** Billig explicitly leaves open whether the southern approach and bath belonged to the first construction or were added later in the Second Temple period (p. 12*). Pottery in accumulations filling the stairs L5 dates mainly to the first century CE (pp. 15*–17*, Fig. 18). The proposed cessation during the Great Revolt is the excavator's interpretation of this fill. First-century BCE material in an aqueduct foundation trench dates that aqueduct context; it does not independently date L5's cutting or W11.

The later settling pool L18 was built onto the bath's north end. Foundation-trench pottery, pottery under the settling-pool stair plaster and structural relationships assign it to the Byzantine period (p. 34*). W1 cut the bath's northern boundary, stairs and their fills (p. 35*). Bijovsky lists fourth-/fifth-century coins from L5; Billig explains some Byzantine pottery/coins along the later supporting wall as consequences of this intrusion (p. 50* n. 27). L5 therefore needs basket/level separation before any deposit is treated as sealed. The late coins alone cannot overturn the published bath chronology.

**Function and text.** The English summary hedges the ritual-bath interpretation; the Hebrew discussion calls it a miqveh and considers funerary, agricultural and public uses. Those functions remain excavator interpretations. The actual pool row is `reading_r2/features.csv#142`. The distinct aggregate row `#141` describes Byzantine/Ottoman settling installations; its tunnel-entrance wording cannot transfer an in-period bath entrance to that row.

**Surviving branch.** L5 retains steps and a channel connection. It lies within the 2 km Ramat Raḥel sensitivity radius and outside 1 km. Whether the scroll's reservoir word permits this bath, whether the site was part of ancient Beth ha-Kerem and where entry 46's leftward target lies remain unresolved. For a person following the stairs, the west-to-east leg and the southward leg yield different leftward directions; the phrase alone selects neither leg nor origin.

## Rogem Gannim Cistern 2

**Observed remains.** East of WP3 is a repeatedly modified plastered installation. Broad stairs once descended into it. Plan 1 locates Cistern 2 separately from nearby Cistern 1, the winepress vats and Cave 1; Section 3–3 depicts its cut and access relationship.

**Function and phases.** The authors infer a bath serving grape-treaders from its stairs and the Early Roman WP3 association (p. 86). They also retain an earlier possibility that this basin was a collecting vat for the first winepress phase. Later alterations removed the stairs and renewed plaster, probably for rainwater storage. Table 1 marks the Roman bath remodelling as probable, and later modification separately (p. 81). The discussion likewise hedges the bath's Early Roman hewing (p. 102). This supports a tentative Early Roman stepped phase, rather than a securely dated initial cistern construction.

**Dating limit.** This report incorporates Sion's earlier excavation of the basin. It supplies no sealed construction deposit for the stairs. Finds securely dating WP3 or Cave 1 belong to their own contexts; they cannot silently become Cistern 2's foundation finds. Its pre-/post-bath sequence needs the 1999 locus and basket records or the original Sion 2002 report, pp. 115–122.

**Surviving branch.** The historical stepped phase enters the cistern sensitivity at 2 km from ʿEin Kerem; it misses 1 km. Entry 46's reservoir function and exact date remain conditional. In the later rainwater-cistern phase, the removed stairs positively contradict retaining those same steps as the entrance. Combining its bath-phase stairs with its later cistern function would create a phase-mixed match.

## Ramat Sharet miqveh I1

**Observed remains.** Four rock-cut stairs L30 entered basin L50, 0.7 × 1.9 m and about 1.6 m deep; the bottom step also served as a bench. Most walls were rock-cut. Built wall W3 separated the bath from shallow cut L20. Grey plaster with small gravel/charcoal inclusions covered surfaces. An approximately 2.7 m square surface I6 about 4 m northeast fed the basin through a rock-cut rainwater channel (pp. 39*–40*, Plan 5).

**Dates.** The authors date the bath by architectural and plaster comparisons. Their introduction explicitly says construction-associated finds were unavailable (p. 31*). In the Mamluk period it became a reservoir/cistern, with its bottom-of-stairs opening blocked by a large stone and smaller stones bonded in mud. L50's pottery is predominantly Ayyubid/Mamluk; the Roman and Ottoman fragments and the 6–9 CE prefect's coin are interpreted as washed-in material (pp. 40*–42*, 44*). The coin therefore supplies no construction date and no sealed in-period use context.

**Branch consequence.** A cistern phase with a Second Temple date and an open stepped entrance fails this native phase check: the source assigns the cistern conversion and blocked opening to the Mamluk period. The earlier bath retains an entrance and a typological Second Temple assignment. Calling its bathing basin a pool could generate a separately declared exploratory reservoir interpretation; it requires a textual argument and consistent treatment of regional miqvaʾot. It cannot silently repair the earlier cistern row.
